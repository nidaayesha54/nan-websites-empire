How Much Does Umbrella Insurance Cost in 2026?

UmbrellaInsuranceHub · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

The headline number first: in 2026, the first $1 million of umbrella insurance typically costs $150–$350 per year — that's roughly $13–$30 a month, less than most streaming bundles. It remains one of the cheapest forms of protection money can buy, and prices have stayed remarkably stable for years.

Price per million: the full ladder

Umbrella policies are sold in $1 million increments, and the price per million falls as you climb — because a claim big enough to reach the third or fifth million is exponentially rarer than one that reaches the first.

What moves your premium up (or down)

  • Your state. Litigation-friendly states — California, Florida, New York — price higher. Some Midwestern states price notably lower.
  • Teen drivers. A young driver in the household is the single biggest surcharge; expect ~20–30% more.
  • Risky property features. Pools, trampolines, certain dog breeds, and boats each add a loading.
  • Rental properties. Each rental adds exposure and premium — and some insurers require every property to be listed.
  • Driving and claims record. DUIs, at-fault accidents, and prior liability claims push umbrella rates up sharply.
  • Underlying limits. You must first raise your auto/home liability to the insurer's required minimums (often 250/500/100 auto, $300k home). That underlying bump is a real extra cost — sometimes as much as the umbrella policy itself.
  • Credit-based insurance score. In states where it's allowed, a stronger score means a lower premium.

The hidden cost: raising your underlying limits

This surprises buyers. If your auto policy currently carries state-minimum liability (say 25/50/25), you can't just bolt on an umbrella policy — the insurer will require you to raise auto to 250/500/100 first. That increase might add $200–$600/year to your auto premium on its own. So the "true" first-year cost of going from minimum coverage to $1M umbrella protection is often $400–$900, not just the $200 umbrella premium. Still cheap for $1M of protection — but budget honestly.

Umbrella vs. just raising your auto limits: which is the better buy?

Some buyers wonder: instead of buying a separate umbrella policy, why not just raise auto liability from $250k to $1M? You can — but it's usually the worse deal. Raising auto liability to $1M often costs $400–$800/year extra on the auto policy alone, and it only covers car accidents. A $1M umbrella policy at ~$200/year covers auto plus home, rental, boat, and personal-liability claims like defamation — a far wider net for less money. The umbrella wins on both price and breadth, which is exactly why insurers price it so aggressively: the underlying policies absorb all the small and medium claims.

7 ways to pay less for umbrella insurance

  1. Bundle with your current insurer. The insurer that already holds your auto/home policies almost always quotes the cheapest umbrella rate — and throws in a multi-policy discount.
  2. Raise underlying deductibles (where you can afford it) to offset the cost of raising underlying liability limits.
  3. Keep a clean driving record. One at-fault accident can raise umbrella pricing for 3–5 years.
  4. Remove or disclose risks carefully. Getting rid of a trampoline or adding a pool fence can reduce loadings.
  5. Ask about the second million. Many agents default-quote $1M; the $2M quote is often only ~$75 more — ask.
  6. Pay annually. Monthly billing fees add up across multiple policies.
  7. Shop every 2–3 years. Umbrella pricing is sticky, but underlying auto/home rates move — and bundling discounts change.

Is it worth it? The math

At ~$200/year, a $1M umbrella policy costs about 0.02% of the coverage per year. Compare that to auto insurance, where you might pay $1,500/year for $250k of liability (0.6%). Per dollar of protection, umbrella coverage is roughly 30× cheaper than the auto liability sitting beneath it. That's because the underlying policy eats the first few hundred thousand of every claim — the umbrella only pays in catastrophes.

The break-even question isn't "will I file a claim?" — it's "can I afford a $500k+ judgment if the one-in-a-thousand event happens?" For most homeowners, the answer makes $200/year an easy yes. Read who actually needs umbrella insurance to see if you're in the high-risk group.

How to buy it

  1. Call or go online with the insurer that holds your auto/home policies.
  2. Raise underlying liability to their required minimums (250/500/100 auto, $300k home is typical).
  3. Quote $1M, $2M, and $3M — compare the per-million cost.
  4. Confirm defense costs are paid in addition to the limit (most good policies do this).
  5. List all properties, vehicles, and household drivers so nothing is excluded.
