What is an EAN-13 barcode?

Walk into any supermarket in Pakistan, Europe or Asia and look at a product's barcode — that's almost certainly EAN-13. It holds 13 digits: a country/company prefix, your product code, and a final check digit that lets scanners detect misreads.

The good news: you only need to supply 12 digits. A proper EAN-13 generator computes the 13th check digit automatically (our tool does this — you even get a "check digit added" confirmation).

Tip: If you're selling your own products in local shops, ask each shop whether they need EAN-13 or UPC-A. In Pakistan and most of the world, it's EAN-13.

What is UPC-A? (US & Canada retail)

UPC-A is the 12-digit retail barcode used across the USA and Canada — the classic barcode with the number printed below in blocks. Type your 11 digits into a free online generator and the 12th check digit is calculated for you.

Important: barcodes for products sold in big US retailers (Amazon, Walmart) usually need officially purchased GS1 prefix numbers. For small shops, local markets and internal inventory, a free generator is all you need.

What is Code128? (the "do anything" barcode)

Code128 is the most versatile barcode: it encodes any letters, numbers and symbols in a compact pattern. Warehouses use it for bin labels, courier companies use it for tracking numbers, and shops use it for internal SKUs that don't need a retail-standard format.

If you're making barcodes for your own internal use — stock rooms, file boxes, price stickers — Code128 is the simplest choice.

What is Code39? (industrial classic)

Code39 is an older, very simple format still common in factories, military logistics and ID badges. It only allows uppercase letters A–Z, digits 0–9 and a few symbols (- . space $ / + %). It's less compact than Code128, but it's extremely forgiving for low-quality printers.

EAN-13 vs UPC-A: can I use one barcode for both?

This is one of the most common questions from new exporters. UPC-A (12 digits) and EAN-13 (13 digits) are technically compatible — any modern scanner reads both, and a UPC-A number can be written as an EAN-13 by adding a leading zero. So a US product with UPC-A can scan fine in a European or Pakistani shop.

The catch is the paperwork, not the technology: large retail chains verify that your number is properly registered with GS1 in their region. If you sell through Amazon USA, get a UPC; if you sell in local Pakistani stores or on Daraz, EAN-13 is the standard. When in doubt, ask your biggest buyer which one they require before you print ten thousand labels — reprinting is the expensive mistake, not the barcode itself.

Printing tips for scannable barcodes

  • Size matters: print EAN-13 at least 3 cm wide; tiny barcodes won't scan.
  • Contrast: black bars on white background scans best. Light-on-dark barcodes often fail.
  • Quiet zone: leave blank space (at least 2–3 mm) on both sides of the barcode.
  • Don't stretch: scale proportionally. Squashed barcodes misread.
  • Test before bulk printing: scan the printed barcode with a phone scanner app first.
Heads-up: A barcode only stores a number — your shop's POS or inventory system must link that number to the product. The barcode alone doesn't carry the price or name.

Do I need to pay for barcodes?

Making the barcode image is free — you can generate as many as you like with a free tool. What can cost money is the official number behind it: retail chains in many countries require products to carry GS1-registered EAN/UPC numbers. For local shops, online selling, internal stock, shipping and event tickets, free generated barcodes work fine.

Barcode vs QR code — what's the difference?

A barcode holds a short number (13 digits for EAN-13) and needs the scanner to look it up in a database. A QR code can hold a full website link or text and any phone can scan it with the camera.
