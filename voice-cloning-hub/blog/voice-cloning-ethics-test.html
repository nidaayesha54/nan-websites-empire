The 5-Minute Ethics Test for Any Voice Cloning Project

October 12, 2026 · 5 min read

Voice cloning tools make it technically easy to replicate anyone's voice in minutes. The question is never "can I?" — it's "should I, and with what permission?" Before you hit "train," run your project through these five questions. If it fails any one of them, don't clone.

1. Do I have explicit consent — from the actual voice owner?

Not implied, not "they probably wouldn't mind," not "it's for a good cause." Explicit means the person was told their voice would be cloned, understood what that means, and agreed — in writing or on a recording. Reputable platforms like ElevenLabs and Descript require this before training; if your chosen tool doesn't, that's your warning sign.

2. Does the consent cover THIS use?

Consent is scoped. Permission to clone a voice for an internal training video is not permission to use it in a national ad campaign, a product, or someone else's project. Check: does the agreement name the specific deliverable, the channels, and the time period? If you're expanding the scope, go back and ask again.

3. Would the voice owner be comfortable if this went public?

The "front page test": imagine the voice owner sees your finished project shared widely. Would they be proud, neutral, or horrified? If there's any chance of the third, stop. This catches the edge cases consent paperwork misses — like cloning a deceased loved one's voice for a commercial product the family never discussed.

4. Am I disclosing that this audio is AI-generated?

Disclosure is becoming a legal requirement in more places (the EU AI Act phases in transparency obligations; several US states require deepfake disclosure in specific contexts). But beyond law, it's basic honesty: audiences deserve to know when the voice they're hearing was synthesized. Label it in descriptions, credits, or metadata.

5. Can the person revoke consent — and what happens then?

Real consent includes an exit. Before you start, agree on what revocation looks like: the voice model gets deleted, existing published content is addressed (takedown or grandfathered with limits), and there's a contact point for the request. If your answer is "they can't take it back," you don't have consent — you have a trap.
