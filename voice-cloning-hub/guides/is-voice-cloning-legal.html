Is Voice Cloning Legal? US Law in Plain English (2026)

Updated October 2026. Laws are evolving — this is general information, not legal advice, and not a substitute for a licensed attorney.

⚠️ Not legal advice. This guide summarizes publicly known US laws in plain language. Voice-cloning law varies by state and changes quickly. For any commercial project, talk to a licensed attorney in your state.

The short answer

Voice cloning technology itself is not illegal. What matters is whose voice, with what permission, for what purpose. Cloning your own voice, or a voice you have explicit written permission to use, is generally fine. Cloning someone else's voice without consent — especially to sell something, deceive people, or commit fraud — can violate several different laws. The rest of this guide explains which ones.

1. Right of publicity: your voice is your property (sort of)

In the US, there is no single federal "voice cloning law" as of 2026 (a federal proposal called the NO FAKES Act has been debated in Congress). Instead, protection comes mainly from state right-of-publicity laws — and they differ a lot:

  • California explicitly protects against unauthorized use of someone's voice or likeness, including digital replicas, and extended protections to deceased personalities. A 2024 California law also targets AI-generated digital replicas in entertainment contracts.
  • Tennessee's ELVIS Act (2024) was the first US law to explicitly name AI voice cloning, protecting musicians' voices from unauthorized simulation.
  • Many other states (New York, Illinois, Texas, Florida, and others) have right-of-publicity or deepfake-related statutes that can cover recognizable voices used commercially without permission.

The practical rule: if a voice is recognizable and you're using it commercially without permission, you're in risky territory in most states — even if no law mentions "AI" by name.

2. Fraud and impersonation: scams are illegal, cloned or not

Using a cloned voice to deceive people — the classic "grandparent scam" call, fake CEO wire-transfer requests, or impersonating a public official — is prosecuted under existing fraud, wire fraud, and impersonation laws. The AI doesn't create a new crime; it creates a new way to commit old ones, and prosecutors are using those old laws aggressively.

  • In February 2024, the FCC banned AI-generated voices in robocalls outright — one of the clearest federal actions on synthetic voices.
  • The FTC's impersonation rule targets scammers impersonating businesses and government, and the agency has pursued AI-enabled fraud schemes.
  • Several states have specific deepfake election laws restricting deceptive synthetic media around elections.

3. Copyright and contracts: the fine print matters

Even when the voice owner says yes, check the paperwork. A voice actor's union contract, an employee's IP assignment clause, or a platform's terms of service can all restrict how a cloned voice may be used. SAG-AFTRA's contracts, for example, now address AI replicas of performers. If you're hiring talent, put voice-cloning rights in writing, in the contract, with scope and duration — a handshake isn't enough.

4. When voice cloning is clearly fine

  • Your own voice — for your podcast, audiobook, videos, or accessibility tools. (Check employer or platform contracts that might claim related rights.)
  • Licensed voices — stock voice libraries and marketplace voices where the license explicitly covers cloning or synthetic use.
  • Written-permission projects — a client, employee, or collaborator who signed a release naming the allowed uses.
  • Parody and commentary — these have First Amendment protection, but the boundaries are fact-specific and narrow. "It's parody" is a legal argument, not a magic shield.

5. The consent standard you should actually follow

Law sets the floor; ethics sets the ceiling. Our recommended standard — stricter than what the law requires in most places:

  • Explicit: the person clearly agrees to voice cloning specifically (not just "recording").
  • Informed: they know what the clone will be used for, where it will appear, and for how long.
  • Written: recorded or signed, dated, and stored where you can find it.
  • Revocable: they can withdraw consent, with a clear plan for existing recordings.

6. Outside the US

The EU's AI Act phases in transparency obligations for AI-generated content, including disclosure requirements for deepfakes. The UK, Canada, Australia, and others are legislating in this space too. If your audience or voice owner is outside the US, don't assume US rules apply — check local law.

Bottom line

Voice cloning is a powerful, legal technology when built on consent — and a legal minefield when it isn't. The pattern across every US law in this space is the same: permission and honesty keep you safe; deception and commercial exploitation get you sued or prosecuted. When in doubt, get it in writing, disclose that audio is AI-generated, and talk to a lawyer before you publish.
