Nigeria's Electricity Tariff Bands Explained

How NERC's service-based tariff bands A–E set your prepaid meter rate in Nigeria, why Band A costs 3x more, and how to check your band.

Home  /  Guides

Your prepaid meter doesn't charge you by how much electricity you use — it charges you by the service band of the feeder that supplies your home. Nigeria's service-based tariff system means two neighbours in different bands can pay wildly different amounts for the same recharge. This guide explains how the bands work, what the 2026 rates look like, and how to make sure you're not overpaying.

The five NERC tariff bands

The Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC) prices electricity by service bands, based on the average daily hours of supply on your feeder:

Band A — 20 to 24 hours of supply daily≈ ₦209.50/kWh
Band B — 16 to 19 hours of supply daily≈ ₦62.50/kWh
Band C — 12 to 15 hours of supply daily≈ ₦53.00/kWh
Band D — 8 to 11 hours of supply daily≈ ₦45.00/kWh
Band E — 4 to 7 hours of supply daily≈ ₦35.00/kWh

These are indicative 2026 single-phase residential figures. Each of the eleven distribution companies — Ikeja Electric, Eko Electric, Abuja Electric, Port Harcourt Electric, Enugu, Ibadan, Kano, Kaduna, Jos, Benin and Yola — publishes its own NERC-approved schedule, so the exact rate can differ by a few naira per kWh between DisCos. The figure printed on your last token is the most accurate rate for your meter.

Why Band A costs so much more

In April 2024, the Federal Government removed most of the electricity subsidy for Band A customers. The Band A rate jumped from about ₦66 to about ₦209.50 per kWh almost overnight. Customers on Bands B to E remained partly subsidised, which is why Band B pays roughly ₦62.50 while Band A pays more than three times that for the same unit of electricity.

This created an unusual incentive: a Band A feeder gives you the most reliable supply, but at 3–4× the cost per unit. For a household using 7.5 kWh a day, a ₦5,000 recharge buys roughly 74 units on Band B — about ten days of power — but only about 22 units on Band A, barely three days. If your actual supply doesn't match your band's promised hours, you may be paying premium rates for substandard service — and that's worth complaining about (more on that below).

VAT and the other deductions that shrink your units

Most DisCos quote the NERC-approved band rate and then add 7.5% VAT on top when issuing the token. That's why ₦5,000 rarely buys exactly ₦5,000 ÷ rate in units. Beyond VAT, several deductions can reduce the units you receive:

  • Fixed monthly service charges, where your DisCo applies them, are deducted before units are issued.
  • Debt or arrears deductions — if the meter carries an outstanding balance, a portion of each recharge clears it first.
  • Lifeline and consumption thresholds — NERC's framework can shift the effective rate as your monthly usage crosses certain thresholds.

The practical rule: divide what you paid by the units you received. That single number is your effective rate, and it's the only figure that matters when comparing recharges.

How to find your band

Three reliable ways:

  1. Your DisCo's feeder verification page — enter your meter or account number (Ikeja Electric, for example, has a customer feeder-verification tool).
  2. Your last token — the rate is usually printed on the receipt.
  3. The division test — divide your last recharge amount by the units received, then compare the result against the band table above. The band whose rate makes the numbers match is almost certainly yours.

A worked example

Band B, rate ₦62.50/kWh, VAT added on top: a ₦5,000 recharge buys 5,000 ÷ (62.50 × 1.075) ≈ 74.4 units. A typical Nigerian household uses roughly 5–10 kWh a day, so those units cover about 8–15 days of normal use. The same ₦5,000 on Band A buys only about 22 units — roughly 3 days. Five ways to stretch your units

  1. Run heavy appliances in one window. Irons, water heaters and washing machines draw the most power. Batch their use instead of running them scattered through the day.
  2. Switch to LED bulbs. Lighting can fall by ~80% compared to incandescent bulbs.
  3. Kill standby loads. Decoders, chargers and TVs on standby add up — switch them off at the socket.
  4. Track your actual supply hours. If you're on Band A but getting Band C hours, log it. You have the right to complain to your DisCo, and to escalate to NERC if the DisCo doesn't resolve it.
  5. Buy in one recharge, not many. While the rate structure is mostly linear, fewer transactions mean fewer chances of fixed charges eating into each top-up.

Common questions

How many units will ₦1,000 buy in Nigeria?

It depends on your band. On Band B (~₦62.50/kWh plus VAT), ₦1,000 buys roughly 15 units. On Band A (~₦209.50/kWh plus VAT), it buys roughly 4.4 units. Use the calculator with your exact rate for the precise figure.

Can my band change?

Yes. NERC periodically reclassifies feeders based on actual supply performance. If your area's supply improves or deteriorates, your feeder — and your band — can be moved. Your DisCo should notify you, but it's worth re-checking your effective rate every few months.

Why did the same recharge buy fewer units this month?

Tariff adjustments, VAT handling, fixed charges, arrears deductions and crossing monthly consumption thresholds can all shift the effective rate. Compare the per-unit rate on consecutive tokens rather than the totals.

Is the calculator on this site official?

No — it's an independent estimate built from published summaries of NERC's service-based bands. Always confirm the final figures on your token or with your distribution company.

Band A vs Band B: the real maths of a feeder change

When NERC reclassifies feeders, some neighbourhoods move between bands — and the financial impact is enormous. Consider a household using 225 kWh a month (7.5 kWh/day):

Band B (≅ ₦62.50/kWh + VAT)≈ ₦15,100/month
Band A (≅ ₦209.50/kWh + VAT)≈ ₦50,700/month

That's a ₦35,000+ monthly difference for identical consumption. If your feeder was upgraded to Band A, the rational response is to cut consumption hard: every kWh you save is worth ₦225. An old deep freezer running 24/7 can cost ₦8,000–₦12,000 a month alone on Band A rates. Audit your big loads first — freezer, water heater, iron, air conditioner — because on Band A, one wasteful appliance costs more than an entire Band E household's monthly bill.

Conversely, if your area was downgraded from Band A to a lower band, your per-unit cost falls sharply — but so do your guaranteed supply hours. Track both sides of the trade for a month before celebrating.

How to complain when supply doesn't match your band

Your band is a promise: Band A feeders should deliver 20–24 hours of supply daily. If yours doesn't, you're overpaying for a service you're not receiving. The complaint ladder:

  1. Log the outages yourself. Note dates and durations for two weeks. A simple notebook beats "the light is always off" when you complain.
  2. Complain to your DisCo first — through their app, website, or customer care line. Get a complaint reference number and keep it.
  3. Escalate to NERC if the DisCo doesn't resolve it. NERC's complaint channels exist precisely for service-band disputes, and DisCos take NERC-escalated complaints seriously.
  4. Ask for reclassification. If your feeder's actual supply consistently falls below your band's threshold, the correct remedy is a band downgrade — which cuts your rate to match the service you actually get.

Communities that complain with logged data get results far more often than those that complain with anecdotes. Two weeks of notes is all it takes.
