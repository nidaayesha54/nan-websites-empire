Federal Paid Parental Leave: 12 Weeks, Rules Explained

Federal employees are entitled to up to 12 weeks of paid parental leave per birth or placement — but the 12-month service rule trips up many new parents. Here is how the benefit actually works.

Under the Federal Employee Paid Leave Act (FEPLA), effective since October 2020, eligible federal employees may substitute up to 12 administrative workweeks of paid parental leave (PPL) for unpaid FMLA leave for a birth, adoption, or foster placement. PPL is not a separate benefit with its own gate: it is paid leave substituted for FMLA leave, so the FMLA eligibility rules decide who qualifies — and the most common misunderstanding is the 12-month service requirement.

How Paid Parental Leave Works

PPL is leave substituted for FMLA leave. You must meet all FMLA eligibility requirements: employees with temporary appointments, intermittent schedules, or fewer than 12 months of qualifying service are not eligible for FMLA leave and therefore not for PPL. PPL is separate from accrued sick or annual leave, usable only during the 12 months after the birth or placement and only while in a parental role.

Before using PPL, sign a written agreement to complete a 12-week work obligation after PPL ends. Intermittent or reduced-schedule use requires agency agreement — it is not an entitlement. Unused PPL cannot be paid out or banked.

The 12-Month Trap — and the Good News

Many new feds assume they must be eligible on the birth date to use PPL at all. That reading is wrong: OPM's fact sheet confirms the 12 months after birth or placement are an expiration window, not an eligibility test date. An employee ineligible at the birth may establish eligibility during the following 12 months and use PPL then — up to the full 12 weeks, but only before the first birthday. The work agreement is signed before that later PPL period begins, not at the birth.

Part-time employees get a pro-rated entitlement: 6 times their biweekly tour of duty. — 240 hours for a 40-hour tour, not 480.

What PPL Does Not Cover — Yet

PPL covers birth, adoption, and foster placement only. A federal employee who has a baby gets 12 weeks of paid leave; that same employee, diagnosed with a serious illness six months later, has no equivalent benefit and must piece together sick leave, annual leave, donated leave, or leave without pay — as must anyone caring for a seriously ill spouse or parent. A bipartisan bill, H.R. 9261 and S. 5168, would grant up to 12 weeks of paid family and medical leave per year for all FMLA-covered situations, As of October 2026 it sits in committee with no floor action — a proposal, not a claimable benefit.

Plan around the first birthday: PPL expires 12 months after the birth or placement. Late eligibility shrinks usable leave — plan timing with your agency early.

How to Use Federal Paid Parental Leave

  1. Confirm FMLA eligibility: 12 months of qualifying service, covered appointment type.
  2. Notify your agency early. Give at least 30 days' notice, or as soon as practicable.
  3. Sign the work agreement. Agree in writing to the 12-week work obligation after PPL ends.
  4. Use it within 12 months of the birth or placement.
  5. Coordinate other leave with your supervisor.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much paid parental leave do federal employees get?

Up to 12 administrative workweeks per birth, adoption, or foster placement, substituted for FMLA leave under FEPLA.

Do I need 12 months of service before my child's birth to use PPL?

No. The 12 months after birth are an expiration window, not a test date. Reach 12 months of service during your child's first year and PPL becomes available then, usable only before the first birthday.

Do part-time federal employees get the full 12 weeks of PPL?

No. Part-time staff receive 6 times their biweekly tour — 240 hours for a 40-hour tour.

Can I save unused paid parental leave for a future child?

No. Unused PPL cannot be paid out or saved for a future birth or placement.

Does paid parental leave cover caring for a sick parent or spouse?

Not today. PPL covers birth, adoption, and foster placement only; the bill to add family and medical leave (H.R. 9261 / S. 5168) has not passed.
