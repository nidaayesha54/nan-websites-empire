Does Motorcycle Insurance Cover Gear?

Updated October 2026 · 5-minute read · By MotorcycleInsuranceHub

A quality helmet costs $400–$800. A good riding jacket another $300–$500. Add gloves, boots, comms and custom parts, and many riders wear $2,000–$4,000 worth of gear and accessories — then discover after a crash that their policy covers almost none of it.

The short answer

Standard motorcycle insurance covers gear only in narrow ways: collision and comprehensive typically include $0–$1,000 of accessory coverage, and liability-only policies cover none of your gear at all. If your gear and custom parts are worth more than ~$1,000, you need an accessory/custom-parts endorsement — usually $30–$100/year for $3,000–$5,000 of coverage.

What standard policies actually cover

  • Collision: pays for damage to the bike in a crash. Most policies include a small accessory allowance ($0–$1,000) — check your declarations page.
  • Comprehensive: pays for theft, fire, vandalism and similar. If your locked garage is broken into and your helmet is stolen with the bike, there may be limited coverage — a helmet stolen from your car or home usually isn't.
  • Liability: pays the other party. It never covers your gear.
  • Homeowners/renters insurance: may cover gear stolen from home (minus a high deductible), but not crash damage — and filing can raise those rates.

Is my helmet covered after a crash?

Here's what surprises riders: even when a policy technically covers a helmet, insurers depreciate gear. A $600 helmet that's two years old might be valued at $200. And a crashed helmet should be replaced regardless — manufacturers and safety experts agree a helmet that's taken an impact is done.

The practical move: an accessory endorsement with replacement-cost (not actual-cash-value) terms for gear. It costs little and means your $600 helmet is replaced with $600, not depreciated to pocket change.

Custom parts and accessories

Exhausts, seats, luggage, lighting, windscreens, performance mods — standard policies cover the bike as it left the factory. Custom parts are typically excluded or capped at that same small $0–$1,000 accessory limit. If you've put $3,000 of mods on your bike, a standard policy leaves most of it uninsured.

📸 Document everything: photograph your gear and mods, save receipts, and send the list to your insurer when you add the endorsement. Claims without proof get lowballed.

The accessory endorsement fix

Almost every major motorcycle insurer offers an accessory / custom-parts and equipment (CPE) endorsement:

  • Coverage: typically $1,000–$30,000 in scheduled add-ons; most riders need $3,000–$5,000.
  • Cost: roughly $30–$100 per year for $3,000 of coverage — among the cheapest add-ons in all of insurance.
  • What it covers: helmets, jackets, gloves, boots, comms systems, saddlebags, custom paint, exhausts, chrome — essentially everything bolted to or worn on the bike.
  • How to get it: call your insurer or agent and ask for "increased accessory coverage" or "custom parts and equipment." It takes one phone call.

How to file a gear claim

  1. Don't throw anything away. Keep damaged gear — the adjuster may want to see it.
  2. Photograph everything at the scene if safe to do so.
  3. List each item with brand, model, age and replacement cost. Receipts or order emails are gold.
  4. File under collision/comprehensive + endorsement together with the bike claim — one deductible usually applies.
  5. Push back on depreciation if you have replacement-cost terms — that's what you paid for.

What about a passenger's gear?

Your accessory endorsement typically covers gear owned by you — not your passenger's $500 helmet. A passenger's damaged gear is generally claimed under their own policy or renters/homeowners insurance, or pursued against the at-fault driver's liability coverage. If you regularly ride two-up, make sure your passenger knows this gap exists — and confirm your guest passenger liability is active, since some budget policies exclude passengers entirely.

One more edge case: gear damaged while not on the bike — say a helmet knocked off a table at a rally — isn't an insurance event at all. That's what the gear's own warranty or a careful hand is for. Insurance covers crashes, theft and named perils, not everyday drops.
