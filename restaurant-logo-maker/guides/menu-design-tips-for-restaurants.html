Menu Design Tips for Restaurants

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

Your menu is a sales tool disguised as a list. Guests spend about two minutes with it, and in that window your layout, prices, and words decide your average order value. These are the techniques professional menu engineers use — no design degree required.

The golden triangle

Eye-tracking studies show diners scan menus in a predictable pattern: first the middle, then the top right, then the top left. These three zones are your prime real estate — place your highest-margin dishes there, not your cheapest crowd-pleasers.

Practical moves: box or shade 2–3 "star" dishes, keep the triangle uncluttered, and never bury your signature in the bottom-left corner where eyes rarely go.

Pricing psychology

  • Drop the currency symbol. "24" outperforms "$24.00" — removing the $ sign measurably increases spending because it disconnects the number from the pain of paying.
  • Nestle prices into descriptions. Put the price at the end of the dish description in the same type size. A right-hand column of prices turns your menu into a price-comparison sheet.
  • Break the price ladder. Listing items cheapest-to-most-expensive trains guests to anchor on the low end. Scatter price points within each section.
  • Add a premium anchor. One expensive signature dish makes everything else feel reasonable — and a few guests will actually order it.
  • Charm pricing is optional in restaurants. 19.95 works in retail; in restaurants, round numbers (20) feel more honest and upscale.

Typography that gets read

  • Two fonts maximum: one display font with personality for headings, one highly readable font for body text.
  • Size for dim light: body text at 11–12pt minimum in print. Test your menu under warm, low restaurant lighting — not your bright office.
  • Hierarchy: section headings clearly bigger than dish names, dish names clearly bigger than descriptions. Guests should scan the structure in five seconds.
  • Line length: keep description lines under ~60 characters for comfortable reading.

Descriptions that sell

Two lines of sensory language beat five lines of adjectives:

  • Bad: "Delicious tasty chicken pasta, very yummy."
  • Good: "Char-grilled chicken, wild garlic cream, hand-torn basil, pecorino."

Name the origin of 2–3 ingredients ("San Marzano tomatoes", "Highland beef") — provenance adds perceived value and justifies price. And retire the words delicious, tasty, and yummy entirely; they tell the guest nothing.

Photos: fewer, better, or none

One stunning photo per section beats a wall of mediocre ones — bad food photography actively reduces orders. Upscale menus often use zero photos and let typography and paper do the work. If you use photos: natural light, tight crops, no stock-photo steam that doesn't exist in real life.

Section order by concept

  • Fine dining: Amuse-bouche → Starters → Mains → Cheese → Desserts → Digestifs. Single column, generous white space.
  • Fast food: Combos & Deals → Signatures → Mains → Sides → Desserts → Drinks. Combos first — they're the margin engine.
  • Café: Signature Drinks → Espresso & Coffee → Tea & More → Breakfast → Sandwiches & Sweets. Two sections max on the counter card.
  • Pizzeria: Specialty Pizzas → Classics → Calzones → Appetizers → Salads → Desserts. Group by style, not by price.
