Retrenchment Benefits in Malaysia, Explained

What Malaysian law says about retrenchment payouts: 10/15/20 days' wages per year of service, notice periods, VSS offers and unfair dismissal claims.

Home  /  Guides

Being retrenched in Malaysia is stressful enough without wondering whether your payout is correct. The law is actually quite specific: under the Employment (Termination and Lay-Off Benefits) Regulations 1980, eligible employees are owed termination benefits of 10 to 20 days' wages for every year of service — plus notice or pay in lieu. This guide breaks down exactly what you're entitled to and the steps to protect yourself.

Who qualifies for termination benefits

Two conditions must both hold:

  1. Coverage under the Employment Act 1955 — the Regulations apply to employees within the Act's purview. Many employers extend similar packages to staff outside the statutory minimum as company policy, so check your contract and handbook even if you're unsure.
  2. At least 12 months of continuous service before the termination date. If you haven't completed a full year, the statutory termination benefits don't apply (though notice pay still might).

Benefits are not payable if your contract is terminated for misconduct, if you resign voluntarily, or if your contract is renewed immediately on equal or better terms. Reaching the retirement age stated in your contract also excludes the benefit.

The payout formula: 10, 15 or 20 days' wages per year

Under the Regulations, the benefit depends on your total length of service:

Less than 2 years10 days' wages for every year of service
2 years to less than 5 years15 days' wages for every year of service
5 years or more20 days' wages for every year of service

A "day's wages" is based on your ordinary rate of pay — commonly calculated as monthly wages divided by 26 working days. Pro-rata applies to part years: 2 years and 6 months at the 15-day tier means 2.5 × 15 = 37.5 days' wages.

Worked example

Monthly salary RM3,000, 4 years of service: daily wage = 3,000 ÷ 26 ≈ RM115.38. Benefit = 4 × 15 days = 60 days × RM115.38 ≈ RM6,923. That's before any notice pay in lieu, which is separate and additional.

Notice periods and pay in lieu

Section 12(2) of the Employment Act 1955 sets minimum written notice periods, which also apply to retrenchment:

Less than 2 years' service4 weeks' notice
2 to 5 years' service6 weeks' notice
More than 5 years' service8 weeks' notice

If your employer doesn't want you to work the notice period, Section 13 requires payment in lieu of notice — your wages for the full notice period, paid on top of the termination benefits. Your contract may provide longer notice than the statutory minimum; the longer period is the one that counts.

What a lawful retrenchment process looks like

Retrenchment is legal in Malaysia — for genuine redundancy caused by restructuring, downsizing, automation or closure — but it must be done fairly. The Code of Conduct for Industrial Harmony and case law expect employers to:

  1. Establish genuine grounds — retrenchment must be a last resort, not a disguised dismissal.
  2. Consider alternatives first — cutting overtime, freezing hiring, reducing hours, internal transfers, or voluntary separation schemes.
  3. Apply fair selection criteria — the default is "last in, first out" (LIFO) for employees in similar roles, though employers can depart from it for documented reasons like skills or performance.
  4. Give proper written notice — at least the statutory periods above.
  5. Notify the authorities — employers must file the retrenchment notification (Borang PK) with the Labour Office at least 30 days before the retrenchment. Non-compliance carries fines of up to RM50,000.
  6. Pay what is owed — termination benefits, notice pay, unused annual leave, and final salary.

Foreign workers in similar posts to local employees must generally be terminated before locals.

Your retrenchment checklist

  1. Get it in writing. Request an official retrenchment letter stating the reason, your last day, and the compensation breakdown.
  2. Verify the maths. Run your service length, daily wage and tier through the formula above — then run it through the calculator on this site as a cross-check.
  3. Check your final salary deadline. Final salary is due on your last working day if you worked your notice, or within 3 days if terminated without notice — and it must be paid into your bank account.
  4. Confirm EPF and SOCSO. Check that all contributions are up to date; gaps affect your benefits.
  5. Claim EIS. The Employment Insurance System provides benefits and re-employment support for retrenched employees who contributed. When you land your next role, check whether the employer invests in training — many fund it through the HRD Corp levy.
  6. Keep everything. Offer letter, contract, payslips, the retrenchment letter, all correspondence.

If the retrenchment was unfair

If you believe you were dismissed without just cause or excuse — for example, the redundancy wasn't genuine, the selection was discriminatory, or procedure was ignored — you can lodge a claim under Section 20 of the Industrial Relations Act 1967 within 60 days of dismissal. The Industrial Court can order reinstatement or compensation (commonly around one month's wages per year of service, plus back wages capped at 24 months for confirmed employees). Don't wait: the 60-day window is strict.

Frequently asked questions

Am I entitled to retrenchment benefits if I earn more than RM4,000?

The statutory Regulations under the Employment Act 1955 have a coverage ceiling, but many employers pay equivalent or better packages to higher earners as company policy or under collective agreements. Check your contract first — then negotiate.

Is retrenchment pay taxed?

Compensation for loss of employment has specific tax treatment under Malaysian law — generally, there's an exemption threshold with the excess taxable. Get current advice from LHDN or a tax professional for your exact figure.

Can my employer retrench me while I'm on medical leave?

Retrenchment must still be genuine and procedurally fair. If redundancy is real, it can include employees on leave — but the selection criteria and process remain challengeable if they look like a pretext.

What's the difference between retrenchment and being fired for misconduct?

Retrenchment ends your job because the role is redundant — you're entitled to benefits. Dismissal for misconduct ends it because of your behaviour — no termination benefits are payable, though the employer must prove the misconduct fairly.

Voluntary separation schemes: should you take the VSS?

Many Malaysian employers offer a Voluntary Separation Scheme (VSS) before forced retrenchment — typically a lump sum sweeter than the statutory minimum, in exchange for your voluntary resignation. Should you take it?

Reasons to take it: the package usually exceeds the 10/15/20-day statutory formula (sometimes significantly — 1–2 months' salary per year of service is common in generous schemes); you leave on your own terms with a clean record; and you can start job-hunting immediately while still employed.

Reasons to pause: accepting a VSS is a voluntary resignation — you generally can't then claim unfair dismissal, and your EIS (Employment Insurance System) eligibility may be affected since you weren't involuntarily retrenched. Also compare the offer against what forced retrenchment would pay you: statutory benefits + notice pay + the possibility of negotiating more.

The negotiation lever most people miss: VSS terms are negotiable, especially for long-serving staff. Employers would rather pay a bit more per volunteer than run a forced exercise. Ask for: a higher multiplier, extended medical coverage, outplacement support, an agreed reference letter, and your unused leave encashed. Get every promise in the separation agreement — verbal assurances from HR are worth nothing later. Tax treatment of your payout

Not all of your retrenchment money is taxed the same way. Under Malaysian tax law, compensation for loss of employment enjoys a partial exemption — historically, a threshold amount per completed year of service is exempt, with the excess taxable as employment income. The exact threshold changes with budgets, so confirm the current figure with LHDN or a tax professional before you file.

Practical implications:

  • Timing matters. If your payout straddles two tax years (e.g., notice pay in December, benefits in January), the split can affect which year's exemption you use. Ask payroll how the payment will be scheduled.
  • Keep the breakdown. Your EA form should separate termination benefits from ordinary salary. If it's lumped together, ask for an itemised statement — it matters at filing time.
  • EPF on termination benefits: statutory termination benefits are generally not subject to EPF contributions, unlike notice pay in lieu, which typically is. This is another reason the itemised breakdown matters.

When in doubt, a one-hour consultation with a tax agent costs far less than the tax you'd overpay by guessing.
