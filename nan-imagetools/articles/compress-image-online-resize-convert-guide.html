Compress Image Online Free: Resize & Convert JPG, PNG, WebP — Full Guide

Last updated: October 2026

Phone cameras now shoot 12-megapixel photos that weigh 3 to 8 MB each. That is fine for your gallery, but it becomes a problem the moment you need to email a photo, upload it to a job portal, or add it to a website. This guide shows you how to compress image online for free, resize image online to exact pixel dimensions, and convert image to JPG (or PNG and WebP) — all in your browser, with no signup and no software to install.

The tools on NAN Image Tools do all of this locally on your device. Your photos are never uploaded to a server, which means the tools work fast, protect your privacy, and keep working even with a shaky connection after the page has loaded.

How to compress an image online in 3 steps

Compressing an image online takes less than a minute:

  1. Upload your image. Open the compressor tool, tap the upload zone or drag and drop a JPG, PNG or WebP file. A preview appears with the original file size and pixel dimensions.
  2. Drag the quality slider. The compressed size updates live as you move the slider. Lower numbers give smaller files; higher numbers keep more detail. The before/after panel shows the original size, the new size and the percentage saved.
  3. Download. Hit the download button. The compressed copy saves to your device with a clear -compressed filename — your original stays untouched.

Most phone photos shrink by 60–80% at 80% quality with no visible difference on screen. That is the single fastest way to beat email attachment limits and upload forms that reject files over a few megabytes.

The quality slider is a trade-off between file size and visible detail. These ranges cover nearly every real-world use case:

Why compressing images helps your website rank

Unoptimized images are the number-one reason web pages load slowly, and page speed is a confirmed Google ranking factor. Google's Core Web Vitals — especially Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) — directly measure how fast the biggest visible element (usually a hero image) appears on screen.

Modern formats make a measurable difference: WebP delivers files roughly 25–35% smaller than JPEG at equivalent quality, and PNG files with photographic content can shrink by 60% or more when re-encoded as quality-optimized WebP or JPG. Compressing images before you upload them to WordPress, Blogger or any CMS is one of the highest-ROI SEO tasks there is — it costs nothing and improves every page the image appears on.

A practical workflow: compress every image to under 200 KB for content images (or under 100 KB for thumbnails), serve WebP where your platform supports it, and set explicit width/height attributes so the page layout does not shift while images load.

How to resize an image online to exact pixels

Resizing changes an image's dimensions (width × height in pixels), while compression changes its file size. You often need both: a 4000-pixel phone photo displayed at 1200 pixels on a website wastes most of its bytes.

To resize image online with our free resizer:

  1. Upload the image. The tool reads its original dimensions and pre-fills the width and height fields.
  2. Type the new dimensions. Keep the "aspect ratio" lock on and the second dimension recalculates automatically — your photo never gets stretched or squished. Turn the lock off only if you deliberately want different proportions.
  3. Use a preset (optional). One click sets the exact size for common platforms — Instagram post, Instagram story, YouTube thumbnail, Facebook cover or Open Graph.
  4. Choose the format and download. The result line confirms the exact pixels and file size before you save.

A useful rule of thumb: resize first, compress second. Fewer pixels almost always saves more space than the quality slider alone, and the combination routinely turns a 5 MB photo into a 120 KB web-ready file.

How to convert image to JPG (and when not to)

Converting formats is the third pillar of image optimization. To convert image to JPG, PNG or WebP: upload the file in the converter tool, pick the target format, adjust quality for JPG/WebP, and download. The original dimensions are preserved — only the encoding changes.

Choose by content:

  • JPG — photographs and anything with smooth gradients. Smallest files for photos, but lossy: every re-save loses a little detail, so keep one high-quality master.
  • PNG — logos, screenshots, text-heavy graphics and anything needing transparency. Lossless, but photos become huge — never use PNG for camera photos on the web.
  • WebP — the modern middle ground: 25–35% smaller than JPG for photos, supports transparency like PNG, and works in all modern browsers. Best default for websites in 2026.

Two conversions people search for constantly: PNG to JPG (a screenshot saved as a 2 MB PNG becomes a ~200 KB JPG) and convert image to JPG for portals and forms that only accept JPEG uploads. Both are instant with the converter.

FAQs

How do I compress an image online without losing quality?

Use 80–85% quality and, for photos, convert to WebP. Most images shrink 60–80% with no visible difference. The compressor on this site shows the exact before/after sizes so you can verify.

How do I resize an image online to a specific size like 1080×1080?

Open the resizer, upload your photo, type 1080 in both the width and height fields (or click the Instagram preset), and download. Keep the aspect-ratio lock on unless you want to crop.

How do I convert an image to JPG?

Upload the image in the converter, select JPG as the target format, set quality to 85–90%, and download. Use JPG for photos; use PNG for logos and screenshots.

Is there a file size limit?

No limit is imposed by the tools — very large images are limited only by your device's memory, because everything runs locally in your browser.

Do these tools work on mobile?

Yes. The upload zones work with your phone's gallery and camera roll, and the whole page is mobile-first. Tap, adjust, download.

Will compressing hurt print quality?

For printing, keep quality at 90–100% and do not downscale below your print dimensions (300 DPI × inches). For screen use, 70–85% is plenty.
