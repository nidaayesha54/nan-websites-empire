What Is ROI? The Formula, Real Examples & Good Benchmarks

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

ROI — Return on Investment — is the single most-used profitability metric in finance. It tells you, as a percentage, how much money an investment made (or lost) relative to what it cost. Whether you're buying stocks, running ads, or flipping houses, ROI is how you answer the only question that matters: was it worth it?

The ROI Formula

ROI = (Net Profit ÷ Cost of Investment) × 100

Where Net Profit = Final Value − Total Cost. The critical word is total: honest ROI includes every dollar — purchase price, fees, taxes, repairs, interest. Skip the calculator below and try our free ROI calculator, which has a dedicated field for additional costs.

Worked Example: Stock Investment

You invest $10,000 in an index fund, pay $150 in fees over 3 years, and the position is now worth $13,200.

  • Total cost = $10,000 + $150 = $10,150
  • Net profit = $13,200 − $10,150 = $3,050
  • ROI = ($3,050 ÷ $10,150) × 100 = 30.05%
  • Annualized ≈ 9.2%/year (use our CAGR calculator for this)

Worked Example: Small Business Marketing

A bakery spends $2,000 on local ads. The campaign brings $9,000 in attributable sales, and ingredients/labor for those orders cost $4,000.

  • Total cost = $2,000 (ads) + $4,000 (fulfillment) = $6,000
  • Net profit = $9,000 − $6,000 = $3,000
  • ROI = ($3,000 ÷ $6,000) × 100 = 50%

Note: marketers often quote ROAS instead ($9,000 ÷ $2,000 = 4.5:1), which ignores fulfillment costs. ROI is the stricter, more honest number.

What Counts as a "Good" ROI?

There is no universal answer — it depends on risk, time horizon and asset class. But these benchmarks are widely used:

Investment TypeTypical "Good" TargetNotes
US stock market (long-term)~10%/yearS&P 500 historical average, before inflation (~7% real)
Rental real estate8–12% cash-on-cash/yearAfter expenses; appreciation is a bonus
Small business15–30%/yearHigher risk and illiquidity demand higher returns
Digital ad campaigns100–400%+ (2:1 to 5:1 ROAS)Varies hugely by industry and margins
Angel/startup investing10x+ on winnersMost fail; portfolio math requires home runs

The golden rule: higher risk must be compensated with higher expected ROI. A "safe" 4% and a "risky" 4% are not the same deal.

ROI vs Annualized ROI: Don't Skip the Time Dimension

A 40% ROI sounds great — until you learn it took 12 years (≈2.8%/year). Always pair ROI with the holding period, or better, convert to an annualized rate. That's exactly what the CAGR calculator does.

Common ROI Mistakes

  1. Ignoring hidden costs — fees, taxes, maintenance and your own time all count.
  2. Comparing ROIs over different time periods — annualize first.
  3. Forgetting inflation — 10% nominal is ~7% real in a 3% inflation world.
  4. Ignoring risk — two investments with equal ROI are not equal if one could go to zero.
  5. Survivorship bias — counting only your winners flatters the average.

FAQ

What is a good ROI percentage?

It depends on the asset class. Rough rules of thumb: ~10%/year for broad stock indexes, 8–12% cash-on-cash for rental real estate, 15–30% for small businesses, and 2:1 to 5:1 ROAS for ad campaigns. Higher risk should demand higher returns.

What is the ROI formula?

ROI = (Net Profit ÷ Cost of Investment) × 100, where net profit is final value minus total cost including fees and taxes.

Can ROI be more than 100%?

Yes — anything above 100% means you more than doubled your money. A $1,000 investment returning $2,500 is a 150% ROI.
