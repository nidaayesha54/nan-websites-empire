South Africa COIDA Assessment: How the Return of Earnings Fee Works

Every South African employer must file a Return of Earnings and pay a COIDA assessment based on payroll and industry risk. This guide walks through the calculation step by step.

Key takeaways:
  • Every employer files a Return of Earnings each year — actual plus provisional payroll.
  • Per-employee earnings are capped (R633,168 for 2025/26; R668,000 for 2026/27).
  • Assessment = (capped payroll ÷ 100) × tariff, minimum R1,621 (R560 domestic).

Every employer in South Africa — from a factory with 500 staff to a household employing one gardener — must be registered with the Compensation Fund and pay an annual COIDA assessment. Miss the Return of Earnings, and the Fund can assess you on its own estimate plus a fine of up to 10%.

This guide explains how the assessment works: the Return of Earnings (W.As.8), the gazetted earnings ceilings, how industry tariffs apply, the minimum fees, payment deadlines, and the Letter of Good Standing that unlocks tenders and contracts.

What COIDA Is

The Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act (COIDA) runs a no-fault insurance fund: when an employee is injured or contracts a disease because of work, the Fund — not the employer — pays medical costs and compensation. Employers fund it through the annual assessment. Recent amendments have widened coverage (including domestic workers) and increased employer accountability around rehabilitation.

Who Must Register?

Essentially every employer: companies and close corporations with even one employee, working directors who draw a salary, and domestic/household employers. Registration is once; the Return of Earnings is annual.

The Return of Earnings (W.As.8)

Each year you declare payroll on form W.As.8, reporting two figures:

  • Actual earnings for the year just ended (1 March – 28 February).
  • Provisional earnings — your estimate for the year ahead.

The submission window is gazetted each season (the 2026 window ran 1 April–30 June); the statutory deadline in the Act is 31 March. File through the Department of Employment and Labour's online portal, and don't mix up the two years' ceilings — it's the most common filing error.

The Earnings Ceilings

Earnings above the gazetted ceiling per employee are excluded from the assessment:

2025/26 assessment year (1 Mar 2025 – 28 Feb 2026, actual earnings)R633,168 per employee
2026/27 assessment year (1 Mar 2026 – 28 Feb 2027, provisional earnings)R668,000 per employee

So a director earning R900,000 counts as R633,168 for 2025/26 purposes. Always verify the current gazetted figure before filing — thresholds move most years.

Industry Tariffs: Risk Sets the Rate

The Fund assigns every employer an industry class with a tariff — a percentage of remuneration reflecting that industry's claims risk. Higher-risk industries (construction, mining, manufacturing) carry markedly higher tariffs than low-risk ones (professional services, administration). Your tariff appears on your assessment notice; if your business activity changes, tell the Fund.

The Formula and the Minimums

Assessment = (capped remuneration ÷ 100) × industry tariff

But the fee can never fall below the statutory minimum: R1,621 a year for commercial employers, R560 for domestic employers. If your calculated fee is R900, you pay R1,621.

Worked Example

A small construction company: 5 employees, combined remuneration R800,000 (all under the ceiling), tariff 1.9%:

Assessment = (800,000 ÷ 100) × 1.9 = R15,200

A professional-services firm: 3 employees, R450,000 payroll, 0.2% tariff → R900 calculated → minimum R1,621 applies.

Payment: 30 Days, Then Penalties

The assessment is payable within 30 days of the notice (COIDA s86). Late or missing returns let the Fund assess you on its own estimate of your earnings and add a penalty of up to 10% of the assessment. File late rather than never — the portal accepts late returns, and a late return with a penalty is far cheaper than an unfiled one.

The Letter of Good Standing

Once your return is filed and the assessment is paid (or under arrangement), you can get a Letter of Good Standing — the Fund's confirmation of compliance, valid up to 12 months. Tender boards, main contractors, and procurement teams routinely demand it before your staff set foot on site. The Fund runs a public online verifier, so expired or forged letters are caught in seconds. Build the annual rhythm into your calendar: file, pay, renew.

What the Fund Pays Injured Workers

Understanding the employer's side is easier when you see what the money buys. The Compensation Fund pays medical costs for work injuries and occupational diseases, temporary disability payments while a worker recovers, permanent disability compensation scaled to the degree of disablement, and death benefits to dependants. That's the no-fault bargain: workers give up the right to sue the employer for covered injuries, and the Fund guarantees payment regardless of who's at fault.

Employers have duties on the injury side too: report workplace accidents promptly and assist the worker's claim. A business current on its assessments but sloppy on incident reporting still creates problems for its people.

Registering: What the Process Looks Like

New employers register with the Compensation Fund through the Department of Employment and Labour — online registration is the standard route. You'll need your company registration details (CIPC), PAYE/SDL/UIF numbers where applicable, and an accurate description of your business activity, because that description determines your industry tariff class. Get the description right the first time: reclassification later means back-and-forth with the Fund and possibly revised assessments.

Contractors and Labour Brokers: Who Pays?

A recurring grey area: if you use contractors or a labour broker (TES), who covers their workers for COIDA? The general principle is that the employer of record — the entity actually employing the worker — carries the COIDA duty. With labour brokers, that's typically the broker, not the client. But misclassified "independent contractors" who are really employees in disguise leave the client exposed. If someone works under your direction, on your premises, on your schedule, treat their COIDA coverage as your problem until a professional tells you otherwise.

COIDA at a Glance

What: annual no-fault workplace-injury insurance funded by employers. Who files: every employer, including domestic employers — on form W.As.8 (Return of Earnings), reporting actual and provisional payroll. Ceilings: R633,168 per employee for 2025/26; R668,000 for 2026/27. Formula: (capped remuneration ÷ 100) × industry tariff, subject to minimums of R1,621 (commercial) or R560 (domestic). When: within the gazetted window; payment due within 30 days of assessment. Proof: the Letter of Good Standing, valid up to 12 months.

Frequently Asked Questions

I employ one domestic worker. Do I really need to register?

Yes. Domestic employers are covered by COIDA and must register, file the Return of Earnings, and pay at least the R560 domestic minimum.

We had no injuries this year. Do we still file?

Yes — the return and assessment are compulsory regardless of claims history. The minimum assessment still applies.

What counts as "remuneration" for the return?

Salaries, wages, overtime, bonuses and other remuneration paid during the assessment year — per employee, capped at the gazetted ceiling.

Can I pay the assessment in instalments?

The Act requires payment within 30 days of assessment. If cash flow is tight, contact the Fund about an arrangement — but don't just ignore the notice.

My tariff looks wrong for my industry. What now?

Query it with the Compensation Fund and make sure your registered business activity description matches what you actually do — misclassification is a common cause of wrong tariffs.
