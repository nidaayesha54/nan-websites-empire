How to Write Voiceover Scripts That Sound Human

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Here's the uncomfortable truth about voiceover: the microphone doesn't fix the script. A $500 mic and an ElevenLabs voice can't save sentences that were written for the eye instead of the ear. The good news? Writing for the ear is a learnable skill, and it follows rules — rules you can check with our free Script Optimizer as you write.

Rule 1: Write like you talk

Read your script aloud. If a sentence makes you stumble, rewrite it — your audience will stumble too. Concretely:

  • Use contractions. "You are going to love this" → "You're gonna love this."
  • One idea per sentence. If a sentence has two commas and a semicolon, split it.
  • Start sentences with "And" or "But" sometimes. It's "wrong" in essays and perfect in speech.
  • Kill formal throat-clearing. "In order to," "due to the fact that," "at this point in time" → "to," "because," "now."

Rule 2: Master the 150-words-per-minute clock

Conversational English runs about 150 words per minute. That single number lets you write to the clock:

FormatWord target
15-second Short / hook~35–40 words
30-second ad~70–80 words
60-second spot~140–160 words
5-minute YouTube video~700–800 words
10-minute YouTube video~1,400–1,600 words

Slow it to 130 WPM for documentaries and courses; push to 170 for high-energy ads. Check any script's exact duration in the Script Optimizer — and plan your whole session around the length with the Voiceover Planner.

Rule 3: The first 10 seconds are everything

Viewers decide in seconds whether to stay. Your opening must do one of three things:

  1. State the payoff: "By the end of this video, you'll be able to price any project in 60 seconds."
  2. Open a curiosity gap: "Your brain is lying to you right now — and it's costing you money."
  3. Name the enemy: "Most voiceover advice is written by people who've never held a mic. Here's what actually works."

Never open with "Hey guys, welcome back to my channel, don't forget to like and subscribe." That's a retention funeral.

Rule 4: Pace like a heartbeat, not a metronome

Flat pacing is the #1 reason voiceovers sound boring — even AI ones. Build rhythm deliberately:

  • Vary sentence length. A short sentence after three long ones feels like a punch. Use it for key points.
  • Mark your pauses. Write [beat] or [pause] right in the script. Silence is a tool, not dead air.
  • Energy-reset every 60–90 seconds. A rhetorical question, a story beat, or a "here's the crazy part" keeps listeners leaning in.
  • Read the last word of each paragraph aloud. If your energy drops at every period, mark emphasis cues.

Rule 5: Cut the filler before you record

"Um," "like," "basically," "actually," "you know" — in writing they look harmless; on mic they shred your authority. Also watch written filler: "very," "really," "quite," "stuff," "things." Replace weak modifiers with strong verbs: not "ran very quickly" but "sprinted." Our Script Optimizer flags fillers automatically with counts.

Rule 6: Direct the performance in the script

Pros don't just write words — they write delivery. Add bracketed cues: [warm], [lean in], [smile], [slow down]. If you're hiring a voice actor or directing yourself, these cues are the difference between "read it" and "performed it." Get style-specific direction notes from our Voice Director.

A reusable script template

Hook (0:00–0:10): Payoff, curiosity gap, or enemy — one sharp idea.
Credibility (0:10–0:25): One line on why you're worth listening to.
Roadmap (0:25–0:40): "Here's what we'll cover" — 3 beats max.
Body: One idea per section. Story → lesson → proof, repeat.
Payoff recap: Restate the promise from the hook — now fulfilled.
CTA: One action. Not three. One.

The 3-pass editing method

  1. Pass 1 — structure: Does every paragraph earn its place? Cut 10% ruthlessly.
  2. Pass 2 — ear test: Read it aloud (or run it through our TTS Preview). Rewrite every stumble.
  3. Pass 3 — performance: Add delivery cues, mark breaths, highlight the 3 lines that must land perfectly.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Writing too long. First drafts run 20% over. Write to the clock from draft one.
  • No contractions. "Do not" and "cannot" sound like a legal disclaimer.
  • Three CTAs. "Like, subscribe, comment, share, follow, buy" — pick one.
  • Skipping the read-aloud. The #1 predictor of a good voiceover is whether the writer heard it first.
