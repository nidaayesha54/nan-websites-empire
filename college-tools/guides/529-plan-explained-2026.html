529 Plan Explained (2026): Rules, Limits & Tax Benefits

A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged investment account for education expenses. It's the single most powerful tool American families have for college savings — yet about half of parents have never heard of it. Here's everything that matters.

On this page
  • How a 529 works
  • The tax benefits
  • What counts as a qualified expense
  • What if money is left over?
  • Contribution limits and rules

How a 529 works

You open a 529 with your state's plan (or any state's — you're not restricted to your own), contribute after-tax dollars, and invest in portfolios ranging from aggressive stock funds to conservative age-based tracks that automatically get safer as college approaches. Growth compounds tax-deferred.

Anyone can open one for anyone — parents, grandparents, even yourself for graduate school.

The tax benefits

  • Federal: no deduction for contributions, but growth is tax-free and withdrawals for qualified expenses are federal income-tax-free.
  • State: 30+ states offer a deduction or credit for contributions to their own plan — often worth hundreds per year.
  • Estate: contributions are removed from your taxable estate, and you can "superfund" five years of gift-tax exclusions at once.

What counts as a qualified expense

Tuition, fees, room and board (for at least half-time students), books, computers, and required supplies at eligible institutions — including many trade schools and community colleges. Up to $10,000 per year can also go toward K–12 tuition.

What if money is left over?

This fear stops many families from saving — but it's manageable:

  • Change the beneficiary to another child or family member, tax-free.
  • Save it for graduate school.
  • Roll up to $35,000 (lifetime, subject to rules) into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary.
  • Last resort: withdraw and pay income tax plus a 10% penalty on the earnings only — your contributions come back penalty-free.

Contribution limits and rules

  • No federal annual limit — but contributions count as gifts ($19,000 per recipient per year in 2026 before gift-tax filing).
  • State aggregate caps typically $300,000–$550,000 per beneficiary.
  • Age-based portfolios rebalance automatically — set it and forget it.
