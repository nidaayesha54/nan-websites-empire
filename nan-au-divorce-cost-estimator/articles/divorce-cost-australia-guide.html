How Much Does a Divorce Cost in Australia? Fees, Lawyers & Saving Money

The fixed court filing fee, consent vs contested costs, property settlement, and smart ways to keep costs down.

In-depth 2026 guide~6 min readFree

Divorce in Australia has one reassuring feature: the court filing fee is fixed and published — $1,170 (or $390 reduced) from 1 July 2026, the same in every state. Everything beyond that fee is variable, and the range is enormous: from a few thousand dollars for an amicable split to $50,000–$100,000+ for a fully contested fight. The choices you make in the first month largely determine where on that spectrum you land.

This guide explains every cost layer — the divorce itself, property settlement, parenting matters, and lawyer fees — plus the proven ways to keep the total down. Estimate your own scenario with our Divorce Cost Estimator.

The fixed cost: applying for divorce

Australia has had no-fault divorce since the Family Law Act 1975 — you don't need to prove adultery, cruelty, or any misconduct. You need to show:

  • 12 months of separation (you can be separated under one roof if living separate lives)
  • A valid marriage (Australian marriage certificate or certified copy)
  • Jurisdiction: at least one spouse is an Australian citizen, permanent resident, or has lived in Australia for 12 months before filing

The application goes to the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia (FCFCOA) through the Commonwealth Courts Portal. Fees from 1 July 2026:

The reduced fee is available to concession card holders and those who can demonstrate financial hardship — the court publishes guidelines and an application form. If you have children under 18, the court must be satisfied proper arrangements are in place for them before granting the divorce.

Joint vs. sole application: filing jointly (both spouses together) is cheaper, faster, and usually avoids a court attendance. A sole application requires formally serving the documents on your spouse — an extra step with its own cost and potential for conflict.

The real cost: divorce vs. property vs. parenting

Here's the distinction most guides blur: the divorce (legally ending the marriage) is the $1,170 part. Property settlement and parenting arrangements are separate proceedings with separate costs — and they're where the money goes:

PathTypical total costWhen it applies
DIY divorce, nothing else disputed$1,170 ($390 reduced)No property split or kids' issues to resolve
Amicable + consent orders$3,000 – $8,000You agree; lawyers document it; court approves ($215 filing)
Negotiated with lawyers$8,000 – $25,000Some disagreement; lawyers negotiate; mediation helps
Contested, litigated to trial$50,000 – $100,000+No agreement; judge decides; barristers, experts, multiple hearings

Lawyer rates drive the upper ranges: Australian family lawyers typically charge $350–$700+/hour depending on seniority and city, and a contested trial can consume hundreds of hours across solicitors, barristers, valuers, and expert reports. Property valuations, forensic accounting for hidden assets, and family reports in parenting disputes each add thousands.

Property settlement: how the costs build

Property settlement follows a structured legal process — identifying the asset pool, assessing contributions (financial and non-financial, including homemaking and parenting), considering future needs, and checking the outcome is "just and equitable." Each stage can generate cost:

  • Disclosure: both parties must fully disclose finances. Incomplete disclosure is the #1 cost multiplier — it breeds suspicion, demands forensic work, and judges punish it.
  • Valuations: real estate, businesses, and superannuation often need independent valuations ($500–$5,000+ each).
  • Superannuation splitting: super is property. Splitting it requires specific orders and sometimes actuarial valuation for defined-benefit funds.
  • Mediation: family dispute resolution is mandatory before filing parenting applications (with exceptions for urgency/violence) and strongly encouraged for property. A day of mediation ($2,000–$5,000 split between parties) routinely saves tens of thousands in litigation.

The cheapest property settlement is a consent order: you agree privately, lawyers draft the orders, the court approves them for $215. It has the same legal force as a litigated judgment.

Parenting matters: cost and process

If you can't agree on parenting arrangements, the court pathway adds:

  • Family dispute resolution (FDR): required first step; government-funded FDR centres are low-cost or free.
  • Lawyer negotiation: parenting disputes are emotionally charged and lawyer-intensive — this is where hourly billing hurts most.
  • Family reports: a court-appointed family consultant interviews parents and children and recommends arrangements ($5,000–$10,000+ if privately engaged).
  • Independent children's lawyer: appointed in complex cases; adds cost but protects the children's interests.

Courts decide parenting matters by the best interests of the child — not by which parent spent more on lawyers. Every dollar both parents burn in a parenting fight is a dollar not available for the children, a point judges make explicitly.

Seven proven ways to cut the cost

1. Agree on what you can, early. Every issue settled privately is an issue unbilled. Even partial agreement narrows (and cheapens) what remains.

2. Use mediation before lawyers escalate. FDR practitioners resolve a large share of disputes in a day or two.

3. Choose a joint application. Filing together avoids service costs, reduces conflict, and usually avoids court attendance.

4. Disclose fully and early. Hiding assets is the most expensive tactic in family law — it always surfaces, and the costs consequences are severe.

5. Use lawyers surgically. Pay for advice and document drafting; do your own organizing, copying, and communicating. Unbundled/limited-scope retainers exist — ask.

6. Apply for the reduced fee. If you're on a concession card or in genuine hardship, the $390 divorce fee (vs. $1,170) is straightforward to claim with evidence.

7. Get consent orders, not a trial. A $215 consent-order filing that both parties sign beats a $50,000 trial on every dimension — cost, time, stress, and co-parenting aftermath.

Legal aid and free help

You don't have to navigate this alone or pay full private rates for everything. Each state and territory has a Legal Aid commission offering free advice lines, and means-tested grants of aid for family law matters. Community legal centres provide free initial advice regardless of income. The FCFCOA website publishes step-by-step guides for self-represented applicants, and family dispute resolution through government-funded centres is low-cost or free. For property matters, some firms offer fixed-fee consent-order packages ($2,000–$4,000) that cap the cost of an amicable settlement. And if there is any family violence, 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) is free, confidential, and available 24/7.

FAQs

How long does divorce take in Australia?

A straightforward joint application is typically granted 2–4 months after filing, and the divorce becomes final one month and one day after the order. Contested property or parenting proceedings can take 12–24+ months.

Do we have to go to court?

For a joint application with no children under 18, usually not — it's handled on the papers. Sole applications and any defended matters generally require attendance (now often by video).

Can we get divorced if my spouse won't cooperate?

Yes. A sole application can proceed without their agreement as long as the 12-month separation and service requirements are met. An uncooperative spouse can slow things but not stop them.

Is there a time limit for property settlement?

Yes — applications must generally be filed within 12 months of the divorce becoming final (or 2 years from separation for de facto couples). After that you need the court's permission, which isn't guaranteed.

Who pays the legal costs?

Each party usually bears their own costs in family law, unlike some other litigation. Courts can make costs orders for misconduct — another reason to behave reasonably throughout.
