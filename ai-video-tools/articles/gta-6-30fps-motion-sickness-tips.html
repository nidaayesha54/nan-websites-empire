GTA 6 at 30 FPS Making You Sick? Try These Fixes

November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Here is the fact every GTA 6 player needs to hear first: the game runs at 30 frames per second on every console, and there is no 60 fps performance mode. Rockstar confirmed it to Kotaku on November 18, 2026, the day before launch. For most players, 30 fps is fine. For some, it means nausea — headaches, dizziness, and that queasy feeling that grows the longer you play. GTA 6 launched on November 19, 2026, on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, and its visual ambition is the reason for the cap: a dense, living Vice City and the wider Leonida state, with night lighting, real traffic, and detailed streets, at a scale no console can push to 60 fps. You cannot raise the frame rate — but you can make it much easier to live with, and this guide explains how.

The nausea is real, and it is not a sign that something is wrong with you or your console — some people are simply more sensitive to lower frame rates. The good news is that the fixes are small, free, and mostly in settings you already have. Work through them in order, and 30 fps becomes comfortable to play with.

Why 30 FPS Feels the Way It Does

Thirty fps is the target, not a guarantee. On base PS5, the frame rate dips below 30 in heavy combat and explosions, when the screen is full of effects, traffic, and physics. The Xbox Series S is the roughest experience of all: it runs in the mid-20s in busy scenes and can drop to around 18 to 20 frames per second in shootouts. The dips are worst exactly where the game is most demanding — big fights, explosions, and dense city traffic at night.

That unevenness is what makes people feel ill: a steady 30 fps is easy to adapt to, but a rate that jumps between 30 and 20 is much harder to watch. The fixes here do not raise the frame rate — nothing does — but they make the motion smoother and easier on your eyes.

Slow the Camera Down

The single most effective change is the simplest: turn the camera more slowly. Whipping the camera around is what makes 30 fps feel worst, because fast camera movement at a low frame rate turns the whole screen into a juddering blur. Slow, deliberate camera turns give your eyes time to track the motion, and the game feels calmer immediately. It takes a few minutes to get used to — the instinct is to snap the camera around — but it is the habit that helps most.

While you are in the settings, try the Motion Blur Strength slider. Set it low, play for a few minutes, then try it high, and keep whichever feels better. There is no universal right answer here: some players feel better with more blur smoothing the motion, others feel worse. The slider exists precisely because people differ, so test both ends and trust what your own eyes tell you.

Pull the Camera Back

The second setting to change is the camera distance. Pull the camera back to the far view, which makes the motion on screen feel slower and wider. A close camera makes every movement feel fast and close; a far camera gives you more of the world in frame and makes the same movement feel gentler. It is a small change in the settings, and it makes a noticeable difference to how the game feels at 30 fps.

Combine that with the slower camera turns, and the game starts to feel like a different experience: calmer, wider, easier to watch. The action is the same — the car chases, the shootouts, the neon streets — but the way it moves across your screen is gentler, and gentler is what your stomach wants.

Change the Room, Not Just the Game

The room matters as much as the settings. Sit further from the TV: the further you sit, the less of your field of vision the screen fills, and the less the motion dominates what you see. Keep a light on in the room — playing in the dark makes the screen the only thing your eyes can see, which makes the motion feel stronger. A lamp on, a little distance, and the game becomes one thing in a lit room instead of the whole world.

And play in shorter sessions: twenty or thirty minutes, then a break — stand up, look out a window, get some water. Short sessions with breaks are the most reliable fix of all, and they cost nothing.

Console Settings That Help

Two console settings can make the dips less painful, though neither raises the frame rate. On PS5, clear the console's cache and turn OFF HDCP in the system settings — disabling it has been reported to smooth out frame drops on base PS5. Turn it back on if you stream or record, since some capture apps require HDCP.

On Xbox, enable VRR — variable refresh rate — under Settings, General, TV and Display Options, with the display set to 120Hz. VRR lets the TV match the game's frame rate, so a dip from 30 to 25 feels like a small stutter instead of a juddery drop — and on the Series S, where the dips are deepest, it makes the biggest difference. Your TV or monitor must support VRR and 120Hz.

When to Stop Playing

If the nausea is severe, stop playing and rest — no setting is worth feeling ill over. Try the changes one at a time, in short sessions: most players find that two or three together are enough. And if nothing helps, the game will still be there tomorrow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will a future patch add a 60 fps mode?

Rockstar has not announced one. Treat 30 fps as permanent, and use the settings in this guide to make it comfortable.

Is 30 fps a bug on my console?

No. GTA 6 runs at 30 fps on every console by design. Dips below 30 in heavy scenes are performance limits of the hardware, not a fault in your console — and the settings in this guide are about making those dips feel gentler.

The Bottom Line

GTA 6 is a 30 fps game on every console, with no 60 fps mode — Rockstar confirmed it. If that makes you feel sick, slow the camera down, try the motion blur slider both ways, pull the camera back, sit further from the TV, keep a light on, and play in shorter sessions. Clear the cache and turn off HDCP on PS5, or enable VRR at 120Hz on Xbox. The frame rate is not going to change — but how it feels can, and these fixes are the way to change it.
