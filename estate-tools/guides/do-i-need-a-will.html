Do I Need a Will? 5 Questions That Answer It

Most adults without a will assume they don't have "enough" to need one. That misses the point: a will isn't just about money. Answer these five questions honestly.

The five questions

  1. Do you have minor children? Only a will names their guardian. Without one, a court decides — possibly choosing someone you'd never pick.
  2. Would you want someone specific handling things? A will names your executor. Without one, the court appoints an administrator, and family fights over the role are depressingly common.
  3. Do you own anything at all? A car, a bank account, personal belongings — someone has to deal with them. A will says who gets what instead of leaving it to default state formulas.
  4. Is your family situation "non-standard"? Unmarried partners, stepchildren, estranged relatives — intestacy laws follow blood and marriage, not your actual relationships. A will is the only way to protect the people the law overlooks.
  5. Do you have opinions about your own care? A will pairs with advance directives: who decides, and what you'd want. Without documents, strangers and statutes decide.

If you answered yes to any of these, you need a will. Most adults answer yes to at least two.

What happens without one

Dying "intestate" hands everything to state law: a fixed inheritance formula, court-appointed guardians for kids, a court-appointed administrator, and a slower, more expensive probate. Your unmarried partner typically gets nothing. Your wishes — unwritten — count for nothing.

The minimum every adult should have

  • A signed will (valid under your state's rules)
  • Financial power of attorney
  • Healthcare proxy and advance directive
  • Reviewed beneficiary designations
