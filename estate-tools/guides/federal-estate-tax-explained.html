Federal Estate Tax Explained (2026): Who Pays & How Much

The federal estate tax sounds scary, but it touches very few families — roughly 2,000 estates a year. Here's how it actually works, who it hits, and the part most articles bury: state estate taxes.

How the tax works

At death, everything you own (minus debts) forms your gross estate. Subtract allowed deductions to get the taxable estate. Subtract the exemption. The remainder — if any — is taxed at a flat 40%. Only the amount above the exemption is taxed.

The exemption (2026 estimate)

An estimated $13.6 million per individual in 2026. The IRS adjusts it for inflation annually, so treat this as an estimate and verify current figures. Married couples can effectively double it (see portability below).

Deductions that shrink the taxable estate

  • Marital deduction: unlimited — assets passing to a US-citizen spouse are fully deductible.
  • Charitable deduction: unlimited for qualifying charities.
  • Debts & expenses: mortgages, funeral costs, executor and attorney fees.

Portability for married couples

When the first spouse dies, any unused exemption can transfer to the survivor — but only if the executor files a federal estate tax return (Form 706) to elect it, even when no tax is owed. Skipping that filing is a common, expensive mistake.

The state tax trap

About a dozen states plus DC impose their own estate taxes — many with exemptions of just $1–2 million. Several others levy inheritance taxes on beneficiaries. A family comfortably under the federal threshold can still owe six figures to their state. Check your state's rules; they're the likelier bill.

Bottom line: federal estate tax is a concern for very large estates; state taxes and proper documents matter for everyone. Start with the will checklist — it covers far more families than the tax ever will.
