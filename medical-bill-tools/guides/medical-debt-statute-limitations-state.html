Medical Debt Statute of Limitations by State (2026 Guide)

Updated January 2026 · 7 min read

Collectors can't chase you forever. Every state sets a statute of limitations — a deadline for filing a lawsuit over a debt. After it expires, the debt is "time-barred": collectors can still ask you to pay, but they generally can't sue you for it (and if they do, the expired deadline is your defense).

Warning: in many states, making even a small payment — or acknowledging the debt in writing — can restart the clock. Know your state's rule before you pay or promise to pay old debt.

What starts the clock?

Generally, the clock starts on the date of last activity — usually your last payment or the date the account went delinquent. Key rules:

  • Paying revives it (in most states). Even $5 can restart the full period.
  • Written acknowledgment can revive it. Be careful what you put in emails or letters to collectors.
  • The clock is a defense, not a shield. A collector can still file suit after expiry — you must show up and raise the defense.
  • Never ignore a lawsuit. If you don't appear, the collector can win by default even on time-barred debt.

Your rights under federal law

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) bans collectors from suing or threatening to sue on debt they know is time-barred, and from using deceptive tactics. If a collector violates the FDCPA, you can sue them — many consumer attorneys take these cases at no upfront cost. The CFPB's debt collection resources explain your rights in plain language.

Common questions

Can a debt collector sue me after the statute of limitations expires?

They can still try, but the expired statute of limitations is a defense you raise in court. Never ignore a lawsuit — show up and assert the defense, ideally with an attorney.

Does paying an old medical debt restart the statute of limitations?

In many states, yes — making a payment or even acknowledging the debt in writing can restart ("revive") the clock. Know your state's rules before paying or promising to pay old debt.

Does the statute of limitations remove debt from my credit report?

No — those are separate clocks. Negative items generally fall off credit reports after 7 years regardless of the lawsuit deadline, and medical collections under $500 no longer appear at all.
