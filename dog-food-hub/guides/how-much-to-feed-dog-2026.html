How Much Should I Feed My Dog in 2026? The Complete Feeding Guide

Ask ten dog owners how much they feed their dog and you'll get ten different answers. The truth is, the right amount depends on your dog's weight, age, activity level, and the calorie density of the food itself. This guide gives you real numbers you can use today — and our daily portion calculator can do the math for your specific dog in seconds.

What Changes How Much Your Dog Needs

Weight alone doesn't decide the portion. Five factors move the number up or down:

  • Age: Puppies burn through calories growing; seniors burn fewer. Neutered adults typically need about 20% less than intact dogs.
  • Activity level: A couch-potato bulldog and a dock-diving border collie of the same weight can differ by 50% or more in calorie needs.
  • Food calorie density: Kibbles range from 300 to 450+ kcal per cup. Always check the label — "one cup" is not a universal unit of nutrition.
  • Body condition: You should feel (not see) your dog's ribs. If the waist is disappearing, feed less, not more.
  • Health status: Pregnancy, nursing, recovery, and conditions like hypothyroidism change needs. Your vet should guide these cases.

Puppies: Feeding by Age

Puppies eat far more per pound than adults because they're building their entire body. As a rule of thumb, puppies need roughly double the calories per pound of an adult dog, spread across more meals:

  • 8–12 weeks: 4 meals per day of a puppy-formulated food
  • 3–6 months: 3 meals per day
  • 6–12 months: 2 meals per day; begin transitioning large breeds to adult food around 12 months (small breeds around 9–12 months)

Always choose a food labeled "complete and balanced" for growth or "all life stages" — puppy-specific nutrition matters more than the brand name.

Senior Dogs

Most dogs are considered seniors around age 7 (earlier for giant breeds). Metabolism slows, so seniors usually need 20–30% fewer calories than in their prime — but protein needs stay high to preserve muscle. Look for senior formulas with joint support, and keep weighing your dog: unplanned weight loss in seniors deserves a vet visit.

How to Read the Feeding Label

The feeding chart on the bag is written by the manufacturer to cover every dog, which means it's intentionally generous. Treat it as the upper bound, not the target. Start at the lower end of the bag's range for your dog's weight, watch body condition for two to three weeks, then adjust.

Two label details that matter most:

  1. kcal per cup (or per kg): This is the number that lets you compare foods and calculate portions. It's usually in small print on the back.
  2. AAFCO statement: Look for "formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog Food Nutrient Profiles" — for growth, maintenance, or all life stages.

How to Adjust Portions Over Time

The real feeding plan is a feedback loop, not a fixed number:

  • Weigh monthly (or weigh yourself holding the dog, then subtract). A 2–3 lb swing on a 30 lb dog is a big deal.
  • Adjust in 10% steps. If your dog is gaining, cut food by 10% and re-check in 2–3 weeks.
  • Count treats. Treats, dental chews, and table scraps add up fast — keep them under 10% of daily calories (see our treat calculator).
  • Seasonal shifts are normal: many dogs eat slightly less in hot summers and more in cold winters.
Bottom line: Start with the chart above or our portion calculator, then let your dog's body condition be the boss. A healthy dog at a healthy weight is the only proof that matters.
Note: Feeding guidelines are estimates — consult your vet for your dog's specific needs.
