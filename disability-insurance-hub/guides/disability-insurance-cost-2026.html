How Much Does Disability Insurance Cost in 2026?

Disability insurance protects your biggest asset — your ability to earn. But what does it actually cost? In 2026, an individual long-term disability policy typically costs 1% to 3% of your annual income. On a $75,000 salary, that means roughly $63 to $188 per month. Short-term policies are cheaper, usually $25 to $75 per month. The exact number depends on you, your job, and the policy you choose.

Group vs Individual: The Price Gap

Employer-sponsored group disability insurance is dramatically cheaper than individual coverage — often 50% to 70% less — because the insurer spreads risk across the whole workforce and skips individual medical underwriting. Some employers even pay the full premium. The catch: group coverage is less generous (lower caps, weaker definitions of disability, and you lose it if you change jobs). The smartest and most affordable strategy for most workers is a hybrid approach: take the maximum group LTD your employer offers, then buy a smaller individual policy to close the remaining gap. A $2,000/month supplemental individual policy on top of group coverage can cost under $50/month for a healthy 35-year-old — a fraction of a standalone policy.

The 6 Biggest Price Drivers

1. Age. Premiums rise steeply with age. A 45-year-old can pay double what a 30-year-old pays for the same benefit. Buying younger locks in lower rates for life with a non-cancelable policy.

2. Occupation. Insurers classify jobs by risk. Office workers and professionals get the best rates (class 5A/6A). Physical trades, healthcare workers who lift patients, and anyone working at heights pay more — sometimes 50% to 100% more.

3. Benefit amount and duration. A $5,000 monthly benefit to age 67 costs far more than $3,000 to age 65. Shorter benefit periods (5 or 10 years instead of to-65) cut premiums substantially.

4. Elimination period. The waiting period before benefits begin has a huge effect on price. Moving from a 30-day to a 90-day elimination period can cut premiums 20% to 30%. Going to 180 days cuts them even further. Take our elimination period quiz to find your sweet spot.

5. Health and lifestyle. Smoking, chronic conditions, and hazardous hobbies (motorcycling, scuba diving, rock climbing) raise rates. Some conditions result in exclusions rather than higher prices.

6. Riders and extras. Cost-of-living adjustments (COLA), future-increase options, residual/partial disability riders, and student-loan riders all add meaningful cost. Each rider can add 10% to 25% to the base premium.

Short-Term vs Long-Term: Cost Comparison

Short-term disability insurance is far cheaper — $25 to $75 per month for typical coverage — because it only pays for weeks to months. Long-term disability costs more because it covers the catastrophic scenario: years without income. Most financial planners recommend prioritizing LTD, since a long illness is the bigger financial risk. 6 Ways to Lower Your Disability Insurance Cost

1. Buy young. Every year you wait, premiums rise — and a new health condition could make you uninsurable. Your 30s are the value sweet spot.

2. Choose a longer elimination period. If your emergency fund covers 3 to 6 months, a 90-day or 180-day waiting period delivers major savings.

3. Shorten the benefit period. Benefits to age 65 is the gold standard, but a 5- or 10-year benefit period costs much less and still covers most disability scenarios.

4. Skip riders you don't need. A COLA rider is nice but expensive. If you're young with a tight budget, get the base policy now and add riders later with a future-increase option.

5. Use group coverage as a base. If your employer offers LTD, take it — then supplement with a smaller individual policy to close the gap. Combined coverage is usually cheaper than an individual policy alone.

6. Quit tobacco and get healthy. Smokers routinely pay 25% to 50% more. Some insurers offer better rates after 12 months tobacco-free.

The Real Question: Can You Afford NOT to Have It?

The Social Security Administration estimates that roughly 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before reaching retirement age. The average long-term disability claim lasts about 3 years. For someone earning $70,000 a year, that's $210,000 of lost income — versus roughly $1,500 a year in premiums.
