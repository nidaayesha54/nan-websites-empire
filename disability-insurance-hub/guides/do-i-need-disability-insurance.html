Do I Need Disability Insurance?

Most people insure their car, their home, even their phone — but not their income, even though income is what pays for all of those things. Here's an honest, no-sales-pitch framework for deciding whether disability insurance makes sense for you.

The Odds Are Higher Than You Think

The Social Security Administration estimates that about 1 in 4 of today's 20-year-olds will experience a disability before reaching retirement age. And "disability" here doesn't mean a dramatic accident — over 90% of long-term disability claims stem from illness, not injury. Cancer, back disorders, heart disease, and mental health conditions top the list. The average long-term claim lasts nearly three years, wiping out roughly three years of income at exactly the moment medical bills spike.

Now compare that risk to the cost: individual long-term coverage runs 1% to 3% of income — for a $70,000 earner, roughly $60 to $175 a month. Details in our 2026 cost guide.

Who Needs Disability Insurance Most?

You likely need it if:

  • Others depend on your income. A spouse, kids, or aging parents who rely on your paycheck make coverage close to essential.
  • You have significant debts. A mortgage, student loans, or car payments don't pause when your income does.
  • Your savings couldn't cover a year without income. If 12 months of expenses would drain you, you're one bad diagnosis from financial crisis.
  • You work in a physical or high-stress occupation. Trades, healthcare, and manual labor carry elevated injury risk; high-stress roles correlate with mental-health claims.
  • You're self-employed or a freelancer. No employer safety net, no paid sick leave, no group policy — you're entirely on your own.
  • You're the higher earner in a two-income household. Losing the bigger paycheck often means the mortgage is suddenly unaffordable.

Who Might Be Able to Skip It?

Honesty cuts both ways. You may reasonably skip individual disability insurance if:

  • You're financially independent. If your investments and savings fully cover your lifestyle indefinitely, you are self-insured.
  • You have a working spouse whose income covers all essential expenses — and that arrangement is stable.
  • You already have strong group LTD through an employer that replaces 60% of income to age 65 with an own-occupation definition. (Most group plans aren't this generous — check the fine print.)
  • You're close to retirement with a paid-off home and a short runway to Social Security and savings.

Note that even in these cases, a serious disability brings extra costs — medical bills, home modifications, caregiving — that income replacement alone doesn't capture.

When Is the Best Time to Buy?

Earlier is almost always better. Premiums are lowest in your late 20s and early 30s, and a non-cancelable, guaranteed-renewable policy locks in your health rating for life — meaning a future diagnosis can't raise your rates or get you dropped. Major life events are natural triggers: starting a first real job, getting married, having a child, buying a home, or going freelance. Each of these increases the financial damage a disability would cause. The worst time to shop for disability insurance is after symptoms appear — most insurers impose waiting periods or exclusions for pre-existing conditions, and some will decline you outright. If you're healthy today, today is the cheapest day you'll ever buy.

The 5-Question Self-Check

Answer honestly:

  1. Could you pay your essential bills for 12 months with no income? If no, you have a coverage gap.
  2. Does anyone depend on your paycheck? If yes, the stakes aren't just yours.
  3. What does your employer actually provide? Many people discover their "coverage" is 60% of base salary (no bonus/commission), capped at $5,000/month, and taxable. Calculate your real gap with our coverage calculator.
  4. How long is your elimination period vs. your savings? A 180-day wait with 2 months of savings is a plan to fail. Use the elimination period quiz to match them.
  5. What would a 3-year income loss cost you? Multiply your annual income by 3. Compare that number to one year of premiums. For most people, the math isn't close.

"But I Have Savings / Workers' Comp / Social Security"

Three common objections, answered briefly:

Savings: A solid emergency fund covers 3–6 months. The average LTD claim lasts about 3 years. Savings are the bridge, not the destination — they pair with insurance, they don't replace it.

Workers' comp: Only covers work-related injuries and illnesses — a small slice of disability claims. Your cancer diagnosis or slipped disc at home isn't covered.

Social Security Disability (SSDI): The average SSDI payment is modest (around $1,500–$1,600/month), approval takes months or years, and most initial applications are denied. It's a last-resort floor, not a plan.

The Bottom Line

If your lifestyle depends on your next paychecks — and for most working Americans, it does — disability insurance is the highest-leverage protection you can buy. It costs a fraction of what a single year of lost income costs. Start by finding your number with the coverage calculator, then compare short-term vs long-term options to build the right package.
