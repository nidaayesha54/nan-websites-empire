Signs of a Healthy Dog: Wellness Checklist

By PetHealthHub · Updated October 2026 · Wellness information — not a diagnostic tool.

Dogs can't tell us when something feels off — but their bodies broadcast signals every day. Learning what's normal for your dog is one of the most powerful wellness skills you can build, because early changes are often subtle. Run through this 15-point checklist monthly (and glance at the big ones daily). Anything that changes suddenly or persists for more than a day or two is worth a call to your vet.

Important: This checklist helps you observe and track wellness. It cannot diagnose illness. If you're ever unsure, your veterinarian — not the internet — makes the call.

1–3: Coat, Skin & Shedding

  1. Coat: A healthy coat looks shiny and feels soft or appropriately textured for the breed, without excessive dandruff, bald patches, or a strong odor.
  2. Skin: Skin should be free of persistent redness, scabs, hot spots, or greasy flakes. Some scratching is normal; constant scratching is not.
  3. Shedding: Shedding varies wildly by breed and season. What's "normal" is your dog's usual pattern — a sudden increase, or patchy loss, deserves attention.

4–6: Eyes, Ears & Nose

  1. Eyes: Bright, clear, and free of heavy discharge. A little "sleep" in the corner is fine; yellow-green discharge, cloudiness, or squinting is not.
  2. Ears: Clean and odor-free, with light wax at most. Head shaking, scratching, or a yeasty smell often points to infection — especially in floppy-eared breeds.
  3. Nose: Forget the old "wet nose = healthy" rule. A healthy nose can be wet or dry; what matters is no cracking, bleeding, or colored discharge.

7–9: Mouth, Teeth & Breath

  1. Teeth: White-to-cream teeth without heavy tartar buildup. By age three, most dogs show some tartar — that's normal, but thick brown buildup needs a dental check.
  2. Gums: Healthy gums are pink (some breeds have pigmented gums — know your dog's baseline) and bounce back within 2 seconds when pressed.
  3. Breath: Dog breath is never minty, but it shouldn't be foul. Persistently bad breath often signals dental disease.

10–12: Body, Weight & Movement

  1. Weight: You should feel (not see prominently) the ribs with light pressure, and see a visible waist from above and the side. Obesity is the most common preventable health issue in US dogs.
  2. Movement: Smooth, even gait without limping, stiffness after rest, or reluctance to climb stairs. Puppies are clumsy; adults shouldn't be.
  3. Energy: Consistent with your dog's age and breed. A Lab that suddenly won't fetch or a senior that's suddenly restless are both telling you something.

13–15: Appetite, Water & Bathroom Habits

  1. Appetite: Eats normal meals with normal enthusiasm. Skipping one meal occasionally happens; refusing food for 24+ hours (or eating far less for 2+ days) warrants a call.
  2. Water intake: Steady daily drinking. A sudden spike in thirst — especially paired with more frequent urination — is a classic early sign of several conditions and should be checked promptly.
  3. Stool & urine: Firm, formed stools and normal-colored urine, on your dog's usual schedule. Note what's typical so you can spot diarrhea, straining, blood, or accidents in a housetrained dog.

Red Flags: Call Your Vet Promptly

Some signs shouldn't wait for the next checkup. Contact your vet the same day for:

  • Difficulty breathing, persistent coughing, or pale/blue gums.
  • Repeated vomiting or diarrhea, especially with blood.
  • Sudden inability to stand, walk, or use a limb.
  • A bloated, hard abdomen with retching (possible bloat — an emergency).
  • Seizures, collapse, or sudden behavior changes like aggression or hiding.
  • Not eating or drinking for 24 hours, or suspected ingestion of toxins.

For after-hours emergencies, keep your nearest 24-hour emergency vet clinic's number saved in your phone — before you need it. Our Pet Emergency Preparedness Guide walks you through building a full kit.

Seasonal Wellness Notes

A few checks deserve extra attention at certain times of year. In summer, watch for heat stress — excessive panting, drooling, or lethargy on hot days — and check paws after walks on hot pavement. Spring and fall are peak tick season in much of the US; run your hands against the grain of the coat weekly to feel for ticks, especially around ears, armpits, and between toes. In winter, road salt and ice-melt chemicals irritate paws, so rinse or wipe feet after walks, and watch for dry, cracked paw pads. Year-round, keep up with the parasite prevention schedule your vet recommends — fleas, ticks, and heartworm don't take holidays, and prevention is far cheaper and safer than treatment.

How to Track Wellness Over Time

  • Monthly weigh-ins: Use our free weight tracker to log weight and spot slow creep — the earliest obesity warning.
  • Vaccine records: Keep shots current with the vaccination tracker; preventive care is wellness care.
  • A simple note: Jot down anything unusual with a date ("Oct 3 — skipping breakfast, still playful"). Patterns across weeks tell your vet more than any single symptom.
  • Annual exams: Even a perfectly healthy-feeling dog needs a yearly checkup (twice yearly for seniors) — vets catch what checklists can't.
Bottom line: You know your dog better than anyone. The checklist gives you the vocabulary; your daily attention provides the early warning. When in doubt, call the vet — that's what they're there for.
