Puppy Vaccination Schedule 2026

By PetHealthHub · Updated October 2026 · Wellness information — your vet confirms the final plan.

Vaccines are one of the highest-value health investments you can make for a new puppy. A full puppy series costs a few hundred dollars; treating parvovirus can cost several thousand — and is not always survivable. This guide explains the standard US puppy vaccination timeline for 2026, which shots are core versus optional, what rabies law requires, and what side effects are normal.

Reminder: This guide summarizes widely used veterinary guidelines (AAHA). It is wellness information only — not a diagnosis or prescription. Your veterinarian sets the final schedule based on your puppy's age, health, and local disease risk.

Core vs. Non-Core Vaccines

Veterinarians divide vaccines into core (recommended for every dog) and non-core (recommended based on lifestyle and geography):

  • Core: DHPP (distemper, adenovirus/hepatitis, parainfluenza, parvovirus) and rabies.
  • Non-core: Bordetella (kennel cough), canine influenza, Lyme disease, and leptospirosis — recommended for dogs with relevant exposure, such as boarding, dog parks, hiking, or tick-prone areas.

The Standard Puppy Timeline (6 to 16 Weeks)

Puppies receive a series of shots every 3–4 weeks until about 16 weeks old, because maternal antibodies fade gradually and can block early vaccines:

What Does Puppy Vaccination Cost in 2026?

Costs vary by region and clinic type, but typical US ranges are:

  • DHPP dose: $25–$50 per visit at a private clinic.
  • Rabies: $20–$40.
  • Full puppy series: roughly $150–$400 total across 3–4 visits.
  • Low-cost options: shelter clinics, humane societies, and mobile vaccine clinics often charge $15–$25 per shot — a safe, legitimate way to save.

Many pet insurance wellness plans reimburse part of the puppy series, so check your policy if you have one.

Rabies Law in the USA

Rabies vaccination is not optional in most of the country. Key facts:

  • Nearly all US states require dogs to be vaccinated against rabies, usually by 4–6 months of age.
  • Keep the official rabies certificate — boarding facilities, groomers, dog parks, and travel (including Hawaii and international trips) commonly require proof.
  • After the first rabies shot, a booster is given at one year; subsequent boosters follow a 1- or 3-year schedule depending on the vaccine product and state rules.

Normal Side Effects vs. Red Flags

Mild reactions are common and usually fade within 24–48 hours:

  • Slight soreness or a small lump at the injection site.
  • Mild tiredness or reduced appetite for a day.
  • A low-grade, temporary dip in energy.

Call your vet promptly if you see facial swelling, hives, vomiting, diarrhea, difficulty breathing, or collapse — these can signal an allergic reaction and need same-day care. Severe reactions are rare but real, which is why many vets suggest monitoring your puppy for 30–60 minutes after vaccination.

Before the Series Is Complete: Socialization vs. Safety

Puppies need socialization during the same weeks they're unprotected, so vets recommend a middle path: carry your puppy in public places, arrange playdates with healthy, vaccinated dogs, and enroll in a puppy class that requires proof of vaccination. Avoid dog parks, pet-store floors, and unknown dogs until the series is done — usually around 16 weeks.

Missed or Late Shots

If you adopt an older puppy or miss a dose, don't restart the whole series — call your vet. They will slot the missing shot into an adjusted timeline. There is no penalty for being late other than a longer window of vulnerability, and a vet can get your puppy caught up quickly.

Keep Records Like a Pro

  • Photograph every vaccine certificate and keep digital copies.
  • Use our free vaccination tracker to log dates and see what's due next.
  • Bring records to every new vet, boarder, or groomer visit.

What About Titer Testing?

You may hear about titer testing — a blood test that measures existing antibody levels. Some vets use titers to decide whether an adult dog truly needs a booster, which can reduce over-vaccination in sensitive dogs. Titers typically cost $50–$100 per test, more than a booster shot, and they don't replace legally required rabies vaccination: no US state currently accepts a rabies titer in place of the shot. For puppies completing their initial series, titers aren't a substitute — the series itself is what builds protection. If you're curious, it's a reasonable question to raise with your vet at the one-year booster visit.

Bottom line: Start at 6–8 weeks, finish the series by 16 weeks, booster at one year, and keep rabies current per your state. It's the simplest, cheapest way to protect your puppy's first year — and your vet is your partner in timing it right.
