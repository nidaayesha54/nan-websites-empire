Freelance Hourly Rate Calculator: How to Price Your Work in 2026

Published October 11, 2026 · 8 min read · By Creator Calculators

Most freelancers pick their hourly rate by copying what others charge — or worse, by guessing. The result: chronic undercharging, burnout, and a business that barely survives. A freelance hourly rate calculator fixes this by working backwards from what you actually need to earn.

In this guide you’ll learn the exact formula professionals use, the three mistakes that silently destroy freelance income, and realistic 2026 rate benchmarks by skill. Try our free freelance rate calculator as you read.

The freelance rate formula (the only one you need)

Gross needed = (Take-home goal + Monthly expenses) ÷ (1 − Tax rate)
Hourly rate = Gross needed ÷ Billable hours per month

Let’s walk through a real example. Say you want $3,000/month take-home, spend $400 on expenses, pay 20% tax, and can bill 100 hours/month:

($3,000 + $400) ÷ (1 − 0.20) = $4,375 gross needed
$4,375 ÷ 100 = $43.75/hour minimum rate

Then add a 15% buffer for unpaid work (proposals, revisions, admin): $43.75 × 1.15 ≈ $50/hour recommended rate.

Why most freelancers undercharge (3 silent killers)

1. Forgetting taxes

Employees have taxes withheld; freelancers don’t. With a 20–30% effective tax rate, a $3,000 take-home goal requires $3,750–$4,300 in gross revenue before expenses. Skip this and you’re working for free one week in four.

2. Counting every hour as billable

You might work 160 hours a month but only bill 80–100. Marketing, proposals, bookkeeping, and learning are real work that clients don’t directly pay for. Use billable hours only in the formula — typically 50–70% of total work hours.

3. Pricing without a buffer

Scope creep, revision rounds, and late payments are inevitable. A 15% buffer isn’t greed — it’s the difference between a sustainable business and a slow-motion burnout.

Hourly vs. project pricing: which wins?

Hourly billing is honest and simple, but it punishes efficiency: the faster you get, the less you earn. Most experienced freelancers use a hybrid:

  • Calculate hourly (using the formula above) as your internal baseline.
  • Quote projects at hourly × estimated hours × 1.2 (complexity premium).
  • Offer retainers for ongoing work — stable income, happier clients.

How to raise your rates without losing clients

  1. Raise rates for new clients first. Existing clients keep old rates for 60–90 days, then get a polite increase notice.
  2. Anchor on value, not hours. “This landing page typically lifts conversions 15–30%” beats “I charge $60/hour.”
  3. Increase 10–15% yearly. Small, regular raises compound; big jumps shock.
  4. Fire the bottom 20%. Your cheapest clients consume the most time. Replace them at your new rate.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good hourly rate for a beginner freelancer?

Calculate your minimum with the formula above first — never go below it. Most beginners land between $25–$50/hour depending on skill. Raise rates every 3–6 months as your portfolio grows.

Should I show my hourly rate publicly?

For commodity services (editing, design tweaks), yes — it filters tire-kickers. For high-value work (strategy, copywriting), quote per project to avoid hourly anchoring.

How many billable hours should I assume per month?

100 hours/month (≈25/week) is a healthy planning number. Full-time freelancers rarely exceed 120 billable hours without burning out.
