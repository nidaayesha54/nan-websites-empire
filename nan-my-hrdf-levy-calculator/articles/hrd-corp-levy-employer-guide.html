HRD Corp Levy: The Employer's Guide

Who must pay the HRD Corp (HRDF) levy in Malaysia, the 1% and 0.5% rates, how it's calculated, and how to claim it back for training.

Home  /  Guides

Every statutory payment your company makes is money gone — EPF, SOCSO, EIS, PCB, all of it leaves the business and never comes back. There is exactly one exception: the HRD Corp levy. The 1% you pay on your Malaysian payroll sits in your own training account, and you can claim it back to fund your team's development. This guide explains who must pay, how much, and how to stop leaving your own money on the table.

HRDF vs HRD Corp: the name change

You'll hear both terms. HRDF (Human Resources Development Fund) is the original name of the fund. In 2021 the organisation managing it rebranded as HRD Corp (Human Resources Development Corporation), a statutory body under the Ministry of Human Resources. When someone says "HRDF levy," they mean the levy collected by HRD Corp under the Pembangunan Sumber Manusia Berhad (PSMB) Act 2001. Same money, same obligation, current name.

Who must register — and who can volunteer

10 or more Malaysian employees (in a covered sector)Mandatory — 1% of monthly wages
5 to 9 Malaysian employeesVoluntary — 0.5% of monthly wages
Fewer than 5Not required

The trigger is headcount, not revenue: the month you hire your tenth Malaysian employee in a covered sector, registration becomes a legal obligation. And it sticks — even if headcount later drops below 10, you keep contributing at 1% for the rest of that calendar year. Many SMEs with 5–9 staff register voluntarily anyway, because the training grants they unlock are worth far more than the 0.5% cost.

Exemptions are narrow: federal and state government bodies, local councils, and purely social-welfare NGOs are generally outside the scheme. Everyone else in a scheduled sector should assume they're in. Operating across the Causeway too? Singapore runs its own employer levy system — see the Singapore foreign worker levy calculator.

How the levy is calculated

The levy is 1% of each Malaysian employee's monthly wages, where "wages" means basic salary plus fixed allowances. Variable pay is excluded: overtime, bonuses, commissions and similar fluctuating payments don't count.

Worked example

12 Malaysian employees, average basic salary RM3,000, average fixed allowance RM300: monthly wage base per employee = RM3,300. Levy = 1% × 3,300 × 12 = RM396 per month, or RM4,752 a year — all of it sitting in your HRD Corp training account, claimable for approved training.

Three rules employers get wrong:

  1. Only the employer pays. The levy cannot be deducted from employees' salaries under Malaysian law — it's an employer cost, full stop.
  2. Payment deadline: levies are paid monthly, due before the 15th of the following month, through the HRD Corp portal (eTRiS at hrdcorp.gov.my).
  3. Only Malaysian employees count toward the headcount and the levy base.

Claiming it back: the part most employers miss

Your levy payments accumulate in your company's HRD Corp account. You can claim them back against approved training through schemes such as:

  • SBL and SBL-Khas — the main grant schemes for public and in-house training.
  • Apprenticeship and on-the-job training programmes.
  • Technology-based learning, including computer-based training.
  • Internal training infrastructure — certain equipment and facilities.

Claimable costs typically include course fees, trainer charges, training materials, and transport/accommodation where training is outside the base city. Two conditions: training must be run by HRD Corp-registered trainers/providers, and it must be approved in advance through eTRiS — retrospective claims for training you already ran don't work.

The five expensive mistakes

  1. Growing through the threshold unnoticed. The classic trap: hire employee number ten, change nothing, and quietly accrue a compliance gap that surfaces the moment HRD Corp audits. Calendar a headcount check quarterly. And if headcount changes mean restructuring rather than growth, know what retrenchment benefits you'd owe — workforce planning cuts both ways.
  2. Treating it as a tax. EPF leaves; the levy comes back as training. Companies that never claim are effectively donating their own money.
  3. Wrong levy base. Including overtime and bonuses overpays; excluding fixed allowances underpays. Base = basic + fixed allowances only.
  4. Deducting it from salaries. Illegal. The levy is 100% an employer obligation.
  5. Training without pre-approval. Book the course, pay the trainer, then discover HRD Corp won't reimburse because it wasn't approved in advance. Always apply first.

Frequently asked questions

Do I pay the levy on foreign employees?

No. The HRD Corp levy applies to Malaysian employees only — both for the 10-employee registration threshold and the monthly calculation.

My headcount dropped to 8. Can I stop paying?

Not mid-year. Once registered as mandatory, you continue at the 1% rate for the entire calendar year even if headcount falls below 10.

Can employees claim training directly?

No — the levy account belongs to the employer, and claims go through the employer's eTRiS account for eligible employees' training.

What happens to unclaimed levy money?

It stays in your HRD Corp account for future training claims. The smart move is to plan an annual training calendar that uses it — it's budgeted money you've already spent.

What training actually gets approved

HRD Corp doesn't reimburse just any course. Understanding what sails through approval — and what gets rejected — is the difference between a levy account that funds real upskilling and one that sits idle.

Almost always approved: technical and safety certifications relevant to your sector, digital skills (data tools, software, cybersecurity basics), leadership and supervisory programmes, customer service training, and train-the-trainer courses. Claims through the SBL-Khas scheme for public programmes are the most straightforward.

Often rejected or queried: generic motivational seminars with no skills outcome, training by unregistered providers, courses with no clear link to employees' roles, and — the classic — training completed before approval was granted. The pre-approval rule is absolute: apply through eTRiS first, train second.

Maximise the claim: approved claims can cover more than course fees — trainer charges, training materials, and transport/accommodation for outstation training are typically claimable. Many employers claim only the course fee and leave the rest on the table. Read the scheme guidelines for your specific programme and claim every allowable line.

A simple annual training calendar that uses your levy

The employers who get the most from HRD Corp treat the levy like a training budget — because that's what it is. Here's a lightweight annual rhythm:

  1. January: plan. Total your expected annual levy (monthly levy × 12). That's your training budget. Survey managers: what skills gaps hurt most?
  2. February–March: schedule. Book 2–4 core programmes for the year through registered providers. Submit eTRiS applications well ahead — approval takes time.
  3. Quarterly: execute and claim. Run the training, collect attendance and completion evidence, and file claims promptly. Don't let paperwork pile up to year-end.
  4. October: review. Check your levy balance. Unclaimed money is just savings sitting idle — schedule a final round of training to use it.
  5. December: report. Summarise training delivered, costs claimed, and skills gained. Present it to management as ROI on a statutory cost — it reframes the levy from "tax" to "investment" in every budget conversation.

A company paying RM500/month in levy has RM6,000 a year in training funds. That's two solid certification programmes for key staff — or a company-wide digital skills rollout. The money is already spent; the only question is whether you get the training or not.

A final word: treat the levy as a budget line, not a bill

The mindset shift is the whole game. Companies that file the HRD Corp levy under "statutory costs to minimise" get nothing back; companies that file it under "annual training budget" get a funded upskilling programme every year. Put the expected annual levy into next year's training budget today, assign one person to own the eTRiS calendar, and review claims quarterly. Within twelve months the levy stops feeling like a tax — because you'll be spending it on your own people.
