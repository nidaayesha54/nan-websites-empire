How to Become a Nurse in 2026: The Complete Step-by-Step Guide

Nursing remains one of the most stable, rewarding, and in-demand careers in America. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects continued strong demand for registered nurses through the decade. Here's exactly how to become one in 2026.

Step 1: Choose Your Entry Path

There are four main doors into nursing:

  • CNA (Certified Nursing Assistant) — 4–12 weeks, ~$1,500. Assists nurses with daily patient care. Median pay ~$38,200.
  • LPN/LVN (Licensed Practical Nurse) — ~1 year, ~$15,000. Works under RNs and doctors. Median pay ~$59,730.
  • ADN (Associate Degree in Nursing) — 2 years. Qualifies you as an RN. Median RN pay ~$101,000.
  • BSN (Bachelor of Science in Nursing) — 4 years. The gold standard; many hospitals now require it. Slightly higher starting pay and the foundation for advanced roles.

Step 2: Complete an Accredited Program

Make sure your program is accredited by the ACEN or CCNE and approved by your state's board of nursing — otherwise you won't be eligible for licensure. Community colleges offer the most affordable ADN programs; expect $6,000–$20,000 total. BSN programs at public universities average around $40,000–$50,000.

Step 3: Pass the NCLEX

After graduation, you must pass the NCLEX-RN (for registered nurses) or NCLEX-PN (for practical nurses). It's a computer-adaptive exam: it gets harder as you answer correctly and stops when it's confident in your ability level. Plan 6–12 weeks of dedicated study — our NCLEX study planner builds your schedule automatically.

Step 4: Get Licensed in Your State

Apply to your state board of nursing, submit fingerprints and background check, and pay the licensing fee ($75–$300 depending on state). If you want to work across state lines, look into the Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC) — 40+ states participate, letting you practice with one multistate license.

Step 5: Land Your First Job

New grads typically start in med-surg, telemetry, or long-term care. Residency programs at large hospitals are the best launchpad — they pair you with preceptors for 6–12 months. Night shifts often pay $4–$8/hour differentials, a fast way to boost early earnings.

Is It Worth It?

Financially, yes. A BSN-RN's tuition typically pays for itself within the first year of working, and lifetime earnings dwarf most careers requiring only a bachelor's degree. Beyond money, nursing offers unmatched job security, geographic flexibility (nurses are needed everywhere), and the rare privilege of meaningful daily work.
