Rent-a-Room Relief: €14,000 Tax-Free

How Ireland's Rent-a-Room relief works: the €14,000 tax-free threshold, qualifying conditions, the cliff-edge trap, and setup checklist.

Home  /  Guides

Ireland has one of the most generous landlord tax breaks in Europe — and most homeowners who could use it have never heard of it. Rent-a-Room relief lets you earn up to €14,000 a year tax-free from renting out rooms in your own home. No income tax, no PRSI, no USC. Here's how it works, who qualifies, and the traps that cost people the relief.

What the relief gives you

If you rent a room or rooms in your sole or main residence and your gross rental income stays at or below €14,000 in a tax year, that income is exempt from income tax, PRSI and USC. That's the whole deal — €14,000 a year that Revenue simply doesn't tax.

The key word is threshold, not band. This is the trap most people miss:

Gross qualifying income €12,000Fully exempt ✓
Gross qualifying income €14,000Fully exempt ✓
Gross qualifying income €14,001Exemption lost — the entire amount is taxable ✗

Exceed the limit by a single euro and the relief vanishes completely — you don't just pay tax on the euro over. This makes the €14,000 ceiling the most important number in your letting.

Who qualifies: the five conditions

  1. It's your sole or main residence. You must live in the property. You don't have to own it — tenants can sublet a room with their landlord's permission — but it must be your home, not an investment property.
  2. The room is part of or attached to the house. A spare bedroom, a basement flat or a converted attached garage all qualify. A detached garden unit does not, under Revenue's current guidance — it must be attached to the property.
  3. Long-term residential use. The occupant must use the room as their home. Short stays, holiday lets and Airbnb-style bookings do not qualify — stays that function like visitor bookings are excluded.
  4. Gross income ≤ €14,000. "Gross" means everything: rent plus any payments for utilities, meals, laundry or other services included in the arrangement. A €1,150/month rent with €100/month of bills included is €15,000 gross — over the line.
  5. You declare it. You must include the income on your annual tax return every year — as exempt income (Form 11 or Form 12). Under the limit, no tax is charged and no detailed rental accounts are required, but the declaration itself is mandatory.

How much could you actually save?

Take a homeowner on the 40% marginal rate renting a room for €1,000/month (€12,000/year). Under Rent-a-Room relief: €0 tax. As ordinary rental income, that €12,000 would face income tax (up to 40%), plus USC and PRSI — a bill of roughly €4,800–€5,500 depending on circumstances. The relief is worth thousands per year to anyone who'd otherwise pay tax on the rent.

And there's a CGT angle: staying within the relief helps your home retain its status as your principal private residence for capital gains tax purposes. Exceeding the threshold can complicate that position on a future sale.

When opting OUT is smarter

Sometimes the relief isn't the best deal. Consider opting out (you notify Revenue by the filing deadline for that year; the choice applies to that year only) when:

  • Your gross income will exceed €14,000 anyway — the whole amount becomes taxable under the scheme, so normal rental rules with deductible expenses may give a lower bill.
  • You have significant deductible expenses — repairs, wear and tear, utility costs tied to the letting. Under Rent-a-Room relief you can't claim expenses at all; under normal rules you can.

Run both calculations before deciding. The calculator on this site's homepage models the comparison.

Setting it up properly: a practical checklist

  1. Price with the gross figure in mind. Work backwards from €14,000: monthly rent × 12 + services must stay under it. €1,100/month with nothing extra = €13,200 — safe. €1,200/month = €14,400 — over.
  2. Use a written licence agreement. A lodger in your home is typically a licensee, not a tenant — but put the terms in writing anyway: rent, bills, notice period, house rules.
  3. Take a deposit and keep records. Bank statements showing rent received, plus the agreement, are your evidence if Revenue ever asks.
  4. Declare it every year on your tax return as exempt income — even when no tax is due.
  5. Review annually. Rent increases, added services, or an extra room can tip you over €14,000 without you noticing.
  6. Check your home insurance. Tell your insurer you have a lodger; most policies need the update and some charge a small premium.

What's changing: Budget 2027

The €14,000 limit has been static for years, but change is on the table. Budget 2026 reporting discussed raising the ceiling — figures of €16,000 from January 2027 have been reported, with proposals for higher limits linked to renting multiple rooms also under discussion. Nothing is final until enacted: budget for €14,000 today, and treat any increase as a bonus when it's law.

Frequently asked questions

Can I rent to a family member?

No — you generally can't claim the relief for renting to your own children or immediate family members. The arrangement must be a genuine arm's-length letting.

Does the room have to be furnished?

There's no furnishing requirement in the legislation, but a room genuinely set up as living accommodation supports the "residential use" condition. Keep it a real bedroom, not a storage room with a mattress.

Can I rent out two rooms?

Yes — the relief covers rooms (plural). The €14,000 cap applies to your total gross income from all rooms combined, not per room.

Do I need to register with the RTB?

A lodger living with you in your home under a licence arrangement is generally outside RTB registration, unlike a standard tenancy. If the arrangement ever looks like a full tenancy, get advice.

The numbers: three realistic scenarios

How the relief plays out at different rent levels (assuming 40% marginal rate, simplified):

€900/month (€10,800/year)Fully exempt. Tax saved vs normal rental: ≈ €4,300/year
€1,150/month (€13,800/year)Fully exempt — but only €200 of headroom. Any bills included in the rent could tip you over.
€1,250/month (€15,000/year)Relief lost entirely. Whole €15,000 taxable as rental income — likely €6,000+ in tax. Dropping rent to €1,166/month (€13,992/year) restores full exemption.

The third scenario is the eye-opener: charging €84/month less can save €6,000 in tax. Near the threshold, the marginal economics of rent-a-room are extreme. Always model the gross figure before setting — or raising — the rent.

Finding the right lodger

The tax relief is only half the equation — you're sharing your home. Practical steps:

  1. Write the advert honestly. Describe the room, the house, the household (who lives there, routines, noise levels), and the rules that matter to you — guests, cooking, cleaning, quiet hours. Vague adverts attract mismatched applicants.
  2. Meet before committing. A 20-minute coffee tells you more than a week of messages. You're assessing compatibility, not conducting an interrogation — but do ask about work hours, lifestyle and expectations.
  3. Check references. A previous landlord or employer reference is standard. For students, a college reference works.
  4. Agree a trial period. A one-month initial term with a review suits both sides — much easier than unwinding a bad year-long arrangement.
  5. Put it in writing. The licence agreement should cover: rent amount and due date, what's included (bills, wifi, laundry), deposit terms, notice period (typically 28 days is fair for lodgers), and house rules. Both sign, both keep a copy.
  6. Take the deposit properly. Usually one month's rent, held against damage — not as an extra month's rent. Document the room's condition with dated photos at move-in.

Where to advertise: Daft.ie and Rent.ie have dedicated "sharing" sections, and university accommodation offices run noticeboards that reach exactly the long-term student market the relief is designed for.
