How to Become a CRNA in 2026: The Complete Path

Published October 12, 2026 · 8 min read · Updated for the 2026 application cycle

Certified Registered Nurse Anesthetists (CRNAs) are the highest-paid nurses in the United States, with median salaries above $214,000. They administer anesthesia for surgeries, epidurals, and pain management — often practicing independently, especially in rural areas. The path is long and competitive, but for the right nurse it is one of the best ROI careers in healthcare.

Here is the complete, step-by-step path to becoming a CRNA in 2026.

Step 1: Earn a BSN (4 years)

Everything starts with a Bachelor of Science in Nursing from an accredited program. While some programs technically accept an ADN (Associate Degree in Nursing), the reality of 2026 admissions is that nearly all accepted students hold a BSN — and the doctoral programs themselves require a bachelor's degree for entry.

During your BSN, protect your GPA. Competitive CRNA applicants typically have a 3.5+ overall GPA, and many programs calculate your science GPA (chemistry, anatomy, physiology, pharmacology, statistics) separately. A strong science foundation also makes the brutal didactics of anesthesia school survivable.

Step 2: Pass the NCLEX-RN and get licensed (3–6 months)

After graduation, pass the NCLEX-RN and obtain your state nursing license. There is no shortcut here — you must be a licensed RN before you can accumulate the critical-care experience programs demand.

Step 3: Work 1–3 years in an adult ICU (the make-or-break step)

This is the single most important requirement. CRNA programs want nurses who have independently managed critically ill patients in a high-acuity adult ICU — think SICU, MICU, CVICU, or neuro ICU. You should be comfortable with:

  • Mechanical ventilation and ventilator management
  • Vasoactive drips (norepinephrine, vasopressin, phenylephrine)
  • Arterial lines, central lines, and hemodynamic monitoring
  • Rapid interpretation of labs, ABGs, and EKGs

Most programs state a minimum of one year, but the average accepted applicant in recent cycles has 2–3 years. Pediatric or neonatal ICU experience alone is usually not accepted — it must be adult critical care. Generate your personalized roadmap to see where you stand.

Step 4: Earn the CCRN certification (2–4 months of study)

The CCRN (Critical Care Registered Nurse) credential from AACN is not officially mandatory at most schools, but in practice it is close to expected. It requires roughly 1,750 hours of direct bedside critical-care practice and passing a rigorous exam. A CCRN signals to admissions committees that your ICU knowledge is deep, current, and validated by a third party — a major edge when programs accept 10–20% of applicants.

Step 5: Shadow a CRNA (40+ hours)

Nearly every program requires documented shadowing with a practicing CRNA. Beyond checking a box, shadowing confirms you actually want this career — anesthesia is high-stakes, detail-obsessed work that is nothing like floor nursing. A shadowing mentor can also write one of your recommendation letters, which carries real weight.

Step 6: Apply to accredited doctoral programs

Since 2025, all US nurse anesthesia programs are doctoral — you will earn a DNP (Doctor of Nursing Practice) or DNAP (Doctor of Nurse Anesthesia Practice). There is no master's route anymore. What to compare:

  • Total cost: $80,000–$250,000+ depending on public vs. private
  • First-time NCE board pass rate: aim for 85%+
  • Attrition rate: lower is better — it reflects student support
  • Clinical site variety: more sites = broader case experience

Typical application package: BSN transcripts, RN license, ICU experience verification, CCRN, three recommendation letters (one from a CRNA), personal statement, and an interview. Apply to 3–5 programs; application cycles open 9–12 months before start dates. Our CRNA interview tips article covers how to stand out.

Step 7: Survive 3 years of doctoral training

Nurse anesthesia programs run about 36 months: roughly 12–18 months of intense didactics (advanced pharmacology, pathophysiology, physics of anesthesia) followed by full-time clinical rotations. You will log around 2,000 clinical hours and 600+ anesthesia cases. You cannot realistically work during the program, so plan your finances in advance — most students live on federal loans. Step 8: Pass the NCE and get licensed

After graduation, pass the National Certification Examination (NCE) administered by the NBCRNA to earn the CRNA credential, then apply for APRN licensure in your state. Most graduates start applying for jobs 3–6 months before finishing. Starting salaries typically range from $180,000 to $220,000 depending on state — see our salary calculator for your state.
