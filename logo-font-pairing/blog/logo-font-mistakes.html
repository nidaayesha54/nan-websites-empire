7 Logo Font Mistakes That Kill Brand Trust

Customers judge your credibility in milliseconds — and typography does most of the talking. These are the seven font mistakes we see most often, with a fix for each.

1. Using an overused font, raw

Papyrus on a spa logo. Lobster on a bakery. Unmodified Montserrat on the tenth startup this week. When customers have seen the exact same letterforms on a dozen other brands, yours feels generic by association. Fix: if you love a popular font, customize at least 2–3 letters so the wordmark becomes ownable.

2. Three or more fonts

Every additional typeface dilutes the brand and screams "designed by committee." Fix: two fonts maximum — one distinctive headline font, one quiet body font. (See our beginner's pairing guide.)

3. Never kerning the wordmark

Default font spacing is built for paragraphs, not logos. Unkerned pairs like "AV", "To" and "Ya" create visible holes that make the logo look amateur. Fix: kern manually at large size — our typography guide walks through the technique.

4. Illegible at small sizes

A delicate thin serif that looks stunning on a billboard becomes gray mush as a favicon or Instagram avatar. Fix: test every logo at 32px wide. If it fails, design a simplified small-size variant with thicker strokes.

5. Trendy today, dated tomorrow

Chasing the typeface of the moment (remember the brutalist grotesque wave? the Y2K bubble letters?) dates your brand within two years. Fix: build on classic structures and add trend only through customization you control.

6. Ignoring the license

"Free download" ≠ free for commercial logos. Using a personal-use-only font in a client logo or product brand is a legal liability. Fix: verify the license before launch — Google Fonts and properly purchased commercial fonts are safe.

7. No clear-space or minimum-size rules

Without clear-space rules, partners will cram your logo against edges, other logos and busy backgrounds until it's unrecognizable. Fix: define clear space (e.g., the height of your logo's "o" on all sides) and a minimum reproduction size in a one-page brand sheet.
