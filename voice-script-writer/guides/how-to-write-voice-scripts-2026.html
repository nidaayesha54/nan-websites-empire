How to Write Voice Scripts in 2026: The Complete Guide

Everything you need to write voice-over scripts that hold attention and drive action — from structure and timing to tone and CTAs.

Home › Guides › How to Write Voice Scripts in 2026

By the VoiceScriptWriter team · Updated October 12, 2026 · 9-minute read

A voice script is not an essay read aloud. It is a performance blueprint — every word chosen for how it sounds, how it lands, and what it makes the listener do. In 2026, with AI voices everywhere and human attention scarcer than ever, the difference between a script that converts and one that gets skipped comes down to craft. This guide covers the full method professionals use, whether you're writing a 30-second radio ad, a 2-minute explainer, or a phone menu.

1. Write for the ear, not the eye

The single biggest mistake beginners make is writing a voice script like a blog post. Readers can re-read a dense sentence; listeners get one pass. That changes everything:

  • Short sentences win. Aim for 8–14 words per sentence. A 30-word sentence looks fine on paper and collapses when spoken.
  • Use contractions. "Do not miss this" sounds robotic; "don't miss this" sounds human. Voice scripts should read like natural speech.
  • One idea per sentence. If a sentence has two commas and an "and", split it. The ear processes linearly — it can't hold three clauses at once.
  • Say it out loud as you write. If you stumble reading your own line, the voice actor will too — and the listener will tune out.
"The ear is an unforgiving editor. Whatever sounds awkward in your kitchen will sound ten times worse through a speaker." — a good rule to tape above your desk.

2. The 3-part structure every script needs

Nearly every effective voice script — ad, explainer, IVR greeting, announcement — follows the same skeleton:

  1. Hook (10–15% of the script). The opener that earns the next 10 seconds. A question, a surprising fact, a bold claim. Never start with "Hello, welcome to…" — that wastes your most valuable seconds.
  2. Body (70–80%). The core message, built as a chain of short, connected sentences. For ads: one benefit, one proof point. For explainers: problem → solution → how it works. For IVR: clear options, fewest words possible.
  3. Call to action (10–15%). One specific action. "Visit freshbrew.com today" beats "check us out sometime" by a mile.

3. Timing math: words per minute

Professional voice-over in the US runs at roughly 150 words per minute — a natural conversational pace. (Fast-paced ads can push 160–170; calm IVR sits around 130–140.) The math is simple:

  • 15-second spot: ~37 words
  • 30-second ad: ~75 words
  • 60-second explainer: ~150 words
  • 2-minute narration: ~300 words

Always write to a word budget, not a feeling. A 90-word "30-second" script will get cut mid-sentence in production — an expensive, embarrassing fix. Our generator shows live word count and duration so you never overshoot.

4. Hooks: win the first 3 seconds

On radio, podcasts, and social video, listeners decide in about three seconds whether to keep listening. Your hook must create an open loop — a question the brain needs answered. Five styles cover almost every situation:

  • Question: "What if your coffee could make itself?"
  • Statistic: "73% of callers hang up on bad phone menus." (Use only real, verifiable numbers.)
  • Story: "Last Tuesday, a customer called us in tears…"
  • Bold claim: "This is the last budgeting app you'll ever need."
  • Curiosity gap: "There's one setting on your phone draining your battery — and it's not the screen."

5. Tone: match the voice to the job

Tone is not decoration — it tells the listener how to feel. A mismatch kills credibility: a hype-man voice on a funeral-home ad, or a monotone on a product launch. Match tone to purpose:

  • Ads: energetic, warm, confident. Energy scores high, formality low.
  • Explainers: clear, friendly, trustworthy. Think "smart friend explaining", not professor lecturing.
  • IVR / phone menus: calm, slow, neutral. Never rush a caller; never sound excited about menu options.
  • Announcements: authoritative but approachable. Lead with the news.

6. CTAs: end every script with one action

A script without a call to action is entertainment, not marketing. The rules of a strong spoken CTA:

  • One action only. "Visit the site AND call AND follow us" means the listener does none of them. Pick the single most valuable action.
  • Use a concrete verb. "Tap the link", "call now", "download free" — never "check out" or "learn more sometime".
  • Give a reason to act now. Deadlines ("sale ends tonight"), instant rewards ("set up in 2 minutes"), or risk removal ("free, no credit card").
  • Spell out tricky URLs and numbers. In audio, "freshbrew dot com slash deal" works; "our website" doesn't.

7. The read-aloud test

Before any script is final, read it aloud at normal pace with a timer running. You're checking four things:

  1. Timing. Does it hit the target duration? Cut or add words until it does.
  2. Stumbles. Any word you trip on gets replaced. Tongue-twisters ("she sells", repeated s-sounds) are script killers.
  3. Breath. Can you say each sentence in one comfortable breath? If not, split it.
  4. Believability. Would a real person actually say this sentence? If it sounds like a press release, rewrite it like a text to a friend.

8. Writing for a US audience

American listeners respond to directness. US voice scripts perform best when they:

  • Get to the point fast — no long cultural preambles.
  • Use American spelling and idioms your specific audience actually uses ("schedule" not "timetable", "cell phone" not "mobile").
  • Name concrete benefits over abstract claims — "saves 20 minutes a day" beats "revolutionary time solution".
  • Respect the skepticism — back bold claims with proof, or US audiences dismiss them.

9. The 10-point checklist

Run every script through this before recording:

  1. Hook in the first 3 seconds — no "hello and welcome".
  2. Word count matches the target duration (±10%).
  3. Sentences average 8–14 words.
  4. Contractions used throughout ("don't", "you'll").
  5. No filler words ("um", "basically", "literally").
  6. No weakeners ("very", "really", "just").
  7. Tone matches the purpose (ad ≠ IVR).
  8. Exactly one call to action at the end.
  9. URLs and phone numbers spelled out for audio.
  10. Read aloud, timed, and stumble-free.
