How Much Paint Do I Need? Room Paint Guide for Pakistan

1. The Paint Formula in One Line

Almost every paint calculation in the world uses the same formula, and once you understand it you will never need to trust a rough guess again:

Paint needed (litres) = Net paintable area × Number of coats ÷ Coverage per litre

Three inputs decide everything: the net paintable area (your walls minus doors and windows), the number of coats, and the coverage per litre printed on the paint can. Add a 10 percent wastage allowance at the end for roller absorption, cutting-in around edges, spills and future touch-ups, and you have a shopping list you can trust. The rest of this guide explains each input in detail, because each one has a trap that catches first-time painters.

2. Step 1: Measure the Room (Walls, Not Floor)

The most common mistake is measuring the floor area. Paint goes on walls, so you need wall area. For a normal rectangular room, the method is simple. Measure the length and width of the room in feet, and the height of the walls from floor to ceiling — in most Pakistani homes this is 10 to 11 feet, while older houses and flats may be 9 feet. Then:

  • Wall area = 2 × (length + width) × height. This is the perimeter of the room multiplied by its height, which gives all four walls.
  • Ceiling area = length × width — add this only if you are painting the ceiling in the same paint. Ceilings are usually painted in white distemper or a separate ceiling paint, so many people skip this step, but if your painter is doing the ceiling in the same emulsion, include it.
  • Subtract openings. A standard interior door is 3 × 7 feet, or 21 square feet. A standard window is 3 × 5 feet, or 15 square feet. Subtract one door and window area for each opening. If you have a large sliding window or a double door, measure it separately instead of using the standard size.
  • The result is your net paintable area. Everything else in the calculation flows from this one number, so measure twice. A tape measure and five minutes save more money than any discount at the shop.

Irregular rooms need a little more patience: measure each wall separately, multiply its width by its height, and add the walls together. Bay windows, niches and almari recesses are measured the same way — wall by wall. Do not subtract skirting boards or small fixtures; the wastage allowance covers those.

3. Step 2: Understand Coverage Per Litre

Coverage tells you how much wall one litre of paint covers in a single coat. It is the number that varies most, and it is the reason two people painting identical rooms can need different amounts of paint. For interior emulsion in Pakistan, these are realistic planning figures:

Smooth, previously painted, primed120–150 sq ftBest case. Your paint goes furthest here.
New plaster, primed100–120 sq ftFresh plaster drinks paint. Primer is essential.
Rough or textured surface90–110 sq ftTexture adds hidden surface area.
Dark-to-light colour changeEffectively lowerYou will need an extra coat, not just extra litres.

Always check the coverage printed on your own paint can first — it beats any online average, because brands and product lines differ. If the can says 130 square feet per litre, use 130. If you are painting new plaster without primer, expect the first coat to disappear into the wall; this is normal, and it is exactly why primer exists. One more caution: coverage figures assume a smooth, primed surface applied with a roller. Spraying, rough brushes and very hot, dry weather all increase consumption, because paint dries on the roller and the wall faster.

4. Step 3: Choose Your Coats (and Why Two Is the Standard)

Two coats is the professional standard for a durable, even finish, and it is what this guide and the NAN Paint Calculator use as the default. Here is when to change that number:

  • One coat is enough only for a refresh in the same colour on a wall that is already in good condition — no stains, no patches, no colour change.
  • Two coats suits almost every repaint, every new room, and every colour change within a similar shade family.
  • Three coats is the honest budget when you are going from a dark colour to a light one, covering stains or water marks, or painting over heavily patched plaster. A third coat is far cheaper than living with a patchy wall you notice every day.

Skipping the second coat is the classic false economy. The first coat soaks in and evens out absorption; the second coat delivers the true colour and the washable surface. Walls painted with a single coat look fine on day one and tired within months.

5. Step 4: Primer and Putty — the Steps People Skip

Primer is a preparatory coat that seals the surface so the finish paint can do its job. On new plaster it is not optional: unprimed plaster absorbs the first coat unevenly, leaving dull patches and using more of your expensive finish paint than a coat of primer would have cost. Primer coverage is usually higher than finish paint — roughly 150 square feet per litre per coat — so one coat of primer for a room is typically 4 to 6 litres. On previously painted walls in good condition, primer is optional, but it is still the right call when changing from dark to light, or when the old paint is chalky or flaking.

Putty (wall filler) is a different job: it fills hairline cracks, dents and uneven plaster before painting. It is sold by weight, usually in 20 kg bags, and one bag covers roughly 300 to 400 square feet for a thin skim coat, depending on how rough the wall is. If your walls are smooth and already painted, you may need no putty at all beyond small touch-ups. If the plaster is new or damaged, budget putty and its labour separately from paint — it is preparation, not paint, and painters often quote it as a separate line item. Never let anyone paint directly over loose, flaking old paint: scrape it, fill it, prime it, then paint.

6. Worked Example: A Standard 12 × 14 ft Bedroom

Let us work through the room most Pakistani families actually paint — a 12 by 14 foot bedroom with 10 foot walls, one door and two windows, smooth walls, two coats of emulsion, plus primer:

  1. Wall area: 2 × (12 + 14) × 10 = 520 sq ft.
  2. Ceiling: 12 × 14 = 168 sq ft. We will paint the walls only in this example, so we skip the ceiling. (Add 168 sq ft if your ceiling uses the same paint.)
  3. Openings: 1 door × 21 + 2 windows × 15 = 51 sq ft.
  4. Net paintable area: 520 − 51 = 469 sq ft.
  5. Finish paint: 469 × 2 coats ÷ 120 sq ft per litre = 7.8 litres, plus 10 percent wastage = 8.6 litres. Round up to cans: one 4-litre can twice, or one 16-litre drum if you are painting more rooms — for this single room, buy 9 litres (two 4-litre cans plus one 1-litre can).
  6. Primer: 469 × 1 coat ÷ 150 sq ft per litre = 3.1 litres, plus a little wastage = about 3.5 litres. One 4-litre can covers it.

So the shopping list for one bedroom is roughly 9 litres of finish paint and 4 litres of primer. If you add the ceiling in the same paint, the net area rises to 637 square feet and the finish paint to about 11.7 litres — a difference of nearly 3 litres, which is why deciding about the ceiling before shopping matters. 7. Budgeting the Cost in Rupees

Once you know the litres, the money part is simple multiplication — but a realistic budget has four lines, and people usually forget two of them:

  • Finish paint: litres × price per litre. Prices vary widely by brand and finish (matt, silk, weather-shield), so enter your shop's actual price in the calculator rather than trusting a remembered figure.
  • Primer: litres × price per litre. Primer is cheaper than finish paint, which is another reason to prime new plaster instead of feeding it extra finish coats.
  • Putty and preparation: putty bags, sandpaper, masking tape and filler for cracks. Small money, but it decides whether the finish looks professional.
  • Labour: painters in Pakistan usually charge per square foot or per room, and rates vary by city and by whether putty and primer are included. Always confirm in writing whether the quote includes material, labour, or both — the most common billing dispute in house painting is a quote that meant one thing to the homeowner and another to the painter.

As a planning habit, get the material estimate from the calculator first, then ask two painters for labour-only quotes against that same material list. Comparing quotes against an identical list is the only fair comparison, and it stops the common trick of a low quote that quietly assumes one coat where you wanted two.

8. Seven Mistakes That Waste Paint and Money

  1. Measuring the floor instead of the walls. Floor area can be half the wall area. Always measure walls.
  2. Forgetting to subtract doors and windows. In a small room, openings can be 10 percent of the wall area.
  3. Painting new plaster without primer. The first finish coat vanishes into the wall and the colour looks patchy.
  4. Buying paint in two trips. Different batches can dry slightly different shades. Buy the whole room at once and mix the cans together before starting.
  5. Skipping the wastage allowance. Roller loss, cutting-in and touch-ups are real consumption, not carelessness.
  6. Choosing one coat to save money. The saving is small; the patchy result lasts for years.
  7. Painting over damp or flaking walls. Fix the damp source and scrape loose paint first, or the new paint peels with the old.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

How much paint do I need for a 10 × 12 ft room?

With 10 ft walls, one door and one window, the net wall area is about 400 square feet. Two coats at 120 square feet per litre need about 6.7 litres, or about 7.3 litres with wastage — so two 4-litre cans. Add primer (about 3 litres) on new plaster.

How much paint covers 1,000 square feet of wall?

At 120 square feet per litre per coat, one coat needs about 8.3 litres and two coats about 16.7 litres, plus 10 percent wastage — roughly 18 to 19 litres in total. That is a useful rule of thumb for whole-house estimates: about 18 litres of finish paint per 1,000 square feet of wall for two coats.

Is one gallon enough for a bedroom?

One US gallon is 3.78 litres, which covers roughly 450 square feet in a single coat at 120 square feet per litre. It is not enough for two coats of a standard bedroom. In Pakistan, paint is usually sold by the litre (1, 4 and 16-litre packs), so work in litres and use the calculator's can breakdown.

Does the ceiling use the same paint?

Usually not. Ceilings are normally finished in a flat white ceiling paint or distemper, which is cheaper than wall emulsion. If your painter uses the same emulsion on the ceiling, include the ceiling area in the calculation — it adds roughly a third to a room's paint for the same number of coats.

How do I store leftover paint?

Seal the can tightly, store it upright in a cool, dry place away from direct sun, and label it with the room and colour. Leftover paint kept this way is usable for touch-ups for a year or more; stir it thoroughly before reuse, and discard it if it smells sour or has a skin that will not stir back in.
