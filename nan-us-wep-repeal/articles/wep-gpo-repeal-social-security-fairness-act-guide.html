WEP & GPO Repealed: The Social Security Fairness Act Guide (2026)

What the repeal of WEP and GPO means for your Social Security check: retroactive payments, new amounts, and what happens automatically.

In-depth 2026 guide~6 min readFree

For decades, millions of American public servants opened their Social Security statements to an unpleasant surprise: a benefit hundreds of dollars smaller than their earnings history suggested. Two obscure provisions — the Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP) and the Government Pension Offset (GPO) — were quietly shrinking or even erasing their checks. On January 5, 2025, President Biden signed the Social Security Fairness Act, repealing both provisions entirely.

This guide explains what WEP and GPO were, who was affected, what the repeal changed, how the retroactive payments worked, and — critically — what you still need to do if you never applied for benefits because these rules would have wiped them out. What were WEP and GPO?

Both provisions dated back decades and targeted people who split their careers between jobs that paid Social Security taxes and jobs that did not — typically state and local government work covered by a separate public pension instead.

The Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP) reduced your own Social Security retirement or disability benefit if you also received a pension from work not covered by Social Security. The reduction changed the benefit formula's first "bend point," and could cut a monthly check by several hundred dollars — up to roughly half of the pension amount in some cases.

The Government Pension Offset (GPO) reduced spousal and survivor benefits — the checks paid to spouses, widows, and widowers — by two-thirds of the recipient's non-covered government pension. In practice, GPO frequently reduced these benefits to zero: a retired teacher with a $3,000 monthly pension could see a $1,800 spousal benefit erased entirely.

The people hit hardest were teachers, firefighters, police officers, and federal retirees under the old Civil Service Retirement System (CSRS) — roughly 3.2 million beneficiaries and their families.

What the Social Security Fairness Act changed

The Act, designated Public Law 118-273, fully repealed both WEP and GPO. The repeal is retroactive: the provisions last applied to benefits for December 2023, so they no longer reduce any benefit payable for January 2024 onward. That single effective date is what created the wave of back payments.

The key points:

  • WEP is gone. Your own retirement or disability benefit is now calculated with the standard formula, regardless of any non-covered pension.
  • GPO is gone. Spousal and survivor benefits are no longer offset by two-thirds of your government pension.
  • Retroactive to January 2024. Everyone affected is owed the difference between what they received and what they should have received, going back to the start of 2024.
  • Foreign pensions count too. If your non-covered pension came from work abroad, WEP no longer reduces your U.S. benefit either.

The back payments: what happened

For beneficiaries already receiving reduced checks, the Social Security Administration handled everything automatically — no application was needed as long as addresses and direct deposit details were current. The SSA recalculated monthly amounts (higher checks began landing in spring 2025) and issued one-time retroactive lump sums covering the increase back to January 2024.

The scale was enormous: the SSA reported more than 3.1 million retroactive payments totaling about $17 billion processed by mid-2025, with the average lump sum around $6,710. Complex cases — particularly involving survivor benefits and foreign pensions — continued processing into 2026.

One tax warning that caught many retirees off guard: the lump sum is taxable Social Security income in the year you receive it, and a large one-time payment can push more of your benefits into the taxable range. The IRS offers a "lump-sum election" that lets you attribute portions of the payment to the years they cover, which can reduce the tax hit — worth discussing with a tax preparer.

The group that still needs to act: people who never applied

Here is the most important action step in this entire guide. If you never applied for a spousal or survivor benefit because GPO would have reduced it to zero, nothing happens automatically — there is no existing claim for the SSA to adjust. You must file an application.

This group includes many spouses and widows of public servants who were told years ago — sometimes by SSA staff themselves — that applying was pointless. The law now says otherwise, and eligibility stretches back to January 2024. But there is a catch: first-time applicants are generally limited to six months of retroactive benefits before their filing month, not the full period back to January 2024. That gap can be worth thousands of dollars, and lawmakers from both parties have pressed the SSA on it — but as of late 2026, the six-month limit stands. The lesson: apply now rather than waiting, because every month of delay is a month of benefits you cannot get back.

How to estimate your restored benefit

The math of the repeal is straightforward in concept:

  • Your own benefit (WEP): the new amount is your benefit calculated under the normal formula — the estimator shows this as your "post-repeal" figure. The difference from your old reduced check is your monthly increase.
  • Spousal/survivor benefit (GPO): the new amount is the full spousal or survivor benefit with no two-thirds pension offset. For many surviving spouses, this is the difference between $0 and a full monthly check.

Your exact figures depend on your earnings record, the pension amount, and your claiming age. The most authoritative source is your my Social Security account at ssa.gov, which now reflects the repeal in its estimates. What to check on your records

Even with automatic adjustments, verify three things:

1. Your monthly amount actually changed. Compare a 2024 statement against a current one. If you were subject to WEP or GPO and nothing changed, contact the SSA — data mismatches (for example, the SSA not knowing about a pension, or misclassifying covered work) delayed some cases.

2. You received the retroactive payment. The lump sum should have arrived separately from your monthly check. If you changed bank accounts or addresses in 2024–2025, confirm the payment reached you.

3. Spouses filed if needed. If your spouse never applied because of GPO, make sure they file now — and ask the SSA office explicitly about the retroactivity rules for new filers.

FAQs

Are WEP and GPO really gone for good?

Yes. The Social Security Fairness Act repealed both provisions in statute. They apply only to benefits for months before January 2024. No new legislation is needed to keep them repealed.

Do I need to do anything if my benefit was already reduced?

No — if you were already receiving a WEP- or GPO-reduced benefit, the SSA recalculated and back-paid you automatically. The only action group is people who never filed a claim.

Does the repeal affect my government pension?

No. Your state, local, or federal pension is untouched. The repeal only changes how Social Security treats that pension — it no longer triggers any reduction.

What if I work for a state or local government now?

Going forward, there is no WEP/GPO reduction to worry about at all. Your future Social Security benefit will be calculated on your covered earnings with the standard formula.

Can I still be affected by the old rules in any way?

Only historically: benefits for months before January 2024 were lawfully reduced under the old rules, and the repeal does not reopen those months. Everything from January 2024 forward is under the new rules.
