GTA 6 Controls Changed: L3 Sprint and What to Fix

November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

The first thing most GTA 5 players notice in GTA 6 is not the graphics or the map — it is the controls. The sprint button moved. The button you have pressed to run for thirteen years now does something else, and the first hour of the game is spent fighting muscle memory. GTA 6 launched on November 19, 2026, on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, and its control scheme is the biggest change to how a GTA game feels since the series went 3D. This guide explains what changed, the Classic scheme that puts it back, the aim and camera settings worth changing, and the defaults to leave alone — all from the launch-day settings menus, not from guesswork.

The settings are deep and the defaults are not all good — change the right ones and the game feels like yours within an hour.

The Big Change: L3 to Sprint

GTA 6 uses L3 — the left stick click — to run and sprint by default. On PS5, the default on-foot controls are L2 to aim or interact, R2 to shoot, R1 for cover, X to jump or climb, and Circle to crouch — and L3 to run. That is the change that catches everyone: in GTA 5, sprint was a face button, and your thumb knew where it was. In GTA 6, sprint is under the stick you are already pushing, and the game explicitly warns you about it during first-time setup.

If L3 to sprint feels unnatural after GTA 5, there is a built-in fix: open Settings, then Control Scheme, and switch to Classic. The Classic scheme is the layout for returning players, and it puts the controls closer to what your hands already know. There is no wrong choice here — Default is the layout Rockstar designed the game around, and Classic is the layout for players whose muscle memory is thirteen years old. Try Default for an hour, and if your thumb keeps reaching for the old button, switch to Classic and do not look back.

Targeting and Aim Assist: Start With Partial

The Gameplay menu holds the most important combat setting in the game: Targeting. GTA 6 offers three modes — Lock-On, Partial and Free Aim — and the default is Partial. Lock-On makes the aim try to lock onto the nearest target, which makes story missions easiest. Partial moves the aim toward a nearby target without fully locking on, which balances assistance and manual control. Free Aim never locks on, which is the hardest option and the most precise. For a first playthrough, Partial is the right starting point: it helps without taking over, and you can move to Free Aim once the controls feel natural.

Next to Targeting sit the assists: Aim Assist Strength is a slider from 0 to 5, defaulting to 4, and Shooting Assist and Drive-By Assist are both on by default. Leave all three alone for your first few hours.

Sensitivity: Aim Slower Than Looking

The Controller menu separates looking from aiming, and that separation is the key to a good setup. Horizontal and vertical look sensitivity have their own sliders, as do horizontal and vertical aim sensitivity, and all four default to 7. Look sensitivity controls camera turning; aim sensitivity controls the crosshair while aiming — and the useful rule is to keep aiming slower than looking. If the camera moves too quickly while you are aiming, try lowering the horizontal and vertical aim sensitivity to around 5 or 6, and leave the look sensitivity where it is. Your camera stays fast for exploring, and your aim stays steady for shooting.

Look Acceleration and Aim Acceleration, both defaulting to 7, control how quickly the camera speeds up as you hold the stick. And the Aim Response Curve offers four presets: Standard, Linear, Precision and Aggressive. Standard is the safest place to start — it balances speed and precision. Linear gives the stick a more direct relationship with camera movement, and Precision is worth testing if you prefer slower fine control around the centre of the stick. Try Standard first, then test Linear once you are comfortable.

Camera Comfort: Bob, Shake and Height

The Camera menu holds the settings that make long sessions comfortable, and two of them are worth changing immediately. Camera Bob controls how much the view moves while you walk, run or swim — the default is 10, and the guides that tested the launch build recommend trying somewhere around 3 to 5 instead. Camera Shake adds jarring movement on top of the bob, and turning it off gives you a steadier view. Both settings matter more than they sound: at 30 frames per second, a bobbing camera is what makes the game feel rough, and a steady one is what makes it feel calm.

Raise the camera height too — High on foot, 3 for vehicles — which gives you more of the world in frame.

What Not to Change

Two things are worth leaving alone. First, do not go looking for graphics or frame-rate settings — GTA 6 does not let you change them. The game runs at 30 frames per second on every console, with no 60 fps mode, and there is no performance menu to open. The motion blur slider, under Display and Visual Effects, is the closest thing to a graphics option: try it low, try it high, and keep whichever feels better, because there is no universal right answer. Second, do not change everything at once. Change one setting, play for twenty minutes, and see how it feels.

Frequently Asked Questions

The Bottom Line

GTA 6's controls changed in one big way — L3 to sprint — and in a dozen small ones that are worth an hour of your time. Switch to Classic if the new layout fights your hands, start with Partial targeting, keep aim sensitivity a little below look sensitivity, drop Camera Bob to 3 or 5, turn Camera Shake off, and leave the rest alone until you know the game. Do that, and the controller disappears — which is exactly what it should do.
