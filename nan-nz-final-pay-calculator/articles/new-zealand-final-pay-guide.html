Your NZ Final Pay, Explained

Your New Zealand final pay explained: unused annual holidays at the greater of ordinary weekly pay or average weekly earnings, plus the 8% component.

Home  /  Guides

Your last day at a New Zealand job doesn't end your employer's obligations — it starts the final calculation. Under the Holidays Act 2003, your final pay must include every dollar you're owed: wages for hours worked, unused annual holidays paid at the higher of two rates, and an 8% top-up on recent earnings. Get any piece wrong and you're leaving money behind. Here's the complete breakdown.

What must be in your final pay

Employment New Zealand lists the components every final pay must cover:

  1. Wages or salary owed — pay for all hours worked since your last pay date, up to your final day.
  2. Unused annual holidays — every week of entitled annual leave you haven't taken, paid out as if you took it on your last day.
  3. The 8% component — 8% of your gross earnings since your last leave anniversary (or since you started, if you never reached one).
  4. Notice pay — if your employer ends your employment without you working the notice period, the notice wages are owed too.
  5. Anything else contractual — agreed bonuses, allowances, or other lump sums in your agreement.

All figures are gross — PAYE is deducted before the money reaches you, but the calculations happen on gross earnings.

The two rates: ordinary weekly pay vs average weekly earnings

This is the part most people — and some payroll systems — get wrong. Unused entitled annual holidays (the four weeks you earn each year) must be paid at whichever is higher of:

  • Ordinary weekly pay (OWP) — what you'd normally earn in a week now, or
  • Average weekly earnings (AWE) — your gross earnings over the last 12 months divided by 52.

If you got a pay rise recently, OWP wins. If you earned overtime and bonuses through the year, AWE may win. The law requires the greater of the two — always check both.

Worked example

Two years' service, 2 unused weeks, OWP $1,400, AWE $1,475, $1,600 wages owed, $24,000 gross since last anniversary:

  • Unused holidays: 2 × $1,475 (the greater rate) = $2,950
  • 8% component: 8% × $24,000 = $1,920
  • Wages owed: $1,600
  • Final pay before tax: $6,470

Notice how the 8% component — nearly $2,000 — is the piece most people forget to check.

The under-12-months rule

If you leave before completing 12 months, you have no week-based annual holiday entitlement at all. Instead, you're owed 8% of your total gross earnings since you started — no OWP/AWE comparison, no weeks. On $18,000 of gross earnings, that's $1,440. Casual employees are often paid this 8% on a pay-as-you-go basis each pay cycle; if yours was, it's deducted from the final 8% figure so you're not paid twice.

Public holidays and alternative days

If a public holiday falls between your last pay date and your end of employment (including the notional extension created by your unused annual holidays), you're owed for it too. Unused alternative holidays (days in lieu for working public holidays) are paid out at relevant daily pay. Unused sick leave, however, is not legally required to be paid out — some employers do it as goodwill, but don't count on it.

Deductions: what your employer can and can't take

Your employer may only deduct from final pay:

  • Legally mandatory deductions (PAYE, student loan, child support, KiwiSaver), and
  • Authorised deductions you agreed to in writing — for example, a signed agreement to repay a training bond or an overpayment.

Anything else — "admin fees," vague "damages," unagreed uniform costs — cannot be taken without your written consent. If money disappears from your final pay without a signed authorisation, challenge it.

When should you be paid?

The timing can be agreed between you and your employer: on your last day or in the next normal pay cycle. What's not negotiable is the amount. Before your last day, ask payroll for a written breakdown of the final pay calculation — every component, every rate. It's much easier to fix an error before the money moves than after.

If your final pay is wrong

  1. Ask for the breakdown in writing and compare each line against the rules above.
  2. Raise it with your employer or payroll — many errors are genuine miscalculations of the OWP/AWE comparison.
  3. Contact Employment New Zealand for guidance if the employer won't correct it.
  4. Mediation and the Employment Relations Authority are the formal routes for unresolved disputes.

Frequently asked questions

Do I get paid for unused sick leave?

No — there's no legal requirement to pay out unused sick or bereavement leave. Some employers do it as a goodwill gesture, but it's not an entitlement.

What if I took annual leave in advance?

Holiday pay taken in advance (or paid pay-as-you-go) is deducted from your final 8% calculation so you're not paid twice for the same earnings.

Can I cash up annual leave instead of losing it?

While employed, you can ask your employer to cash up to one week of your minimum four-week entitlement per year. On leaving, all unused entitled leave is paid out automatically.

Does my final pay include KiwiSaver?

Your final pay is gross earnings, so compulsory employer KiwiSaver contributions apply to it as normal — and your own contributions continue on the final payment too. Make sure you're also getting the government's annual top-up — see how to get the full $260.72 KiwiSaver government contribution.

Redundancy vs resignation: does it change the maths?

The core formula doesn't change — the Holidays Act applies however your employment ends. But the surrounding payments often differ:

  • Redundancy: your final pay follows the standard rules above, plus any redundancy compensation in your employment agreement. Redundancy pay isn't legally required in New Zealand — it's purely contractual — so check your agreement. Many agreements specify a formula (e.g., a number of weeks per year of service).
  • Resignation: standard final pay only, and you must work (or be paid for) your notice period. If you leave without working notice and your agreement allows it, the employer can deduct the notice shortfall — but only as your agreement permits.
  • Dismissal for serious misconduct: you're still owed wages worked and accrued holidays — those can't be withheld as punishment. Notice pay depends on the agreement.
  • Fixed-term ending: the same final-pay rules apply at the end of the term, including the 8% component on earnings since your last anniversary.

One more scenario: retirement. Same rules again — your final pay is calculated identically, and any long-service provisions in your agreement apply on top. If you're retiring for good, our retirement hub can help you plan what comes next.

The payslip audit: five numbers to check

Before you sign off on a final pay, verify these five figures against your own records:

  1. Hours worked since last pay. Compare against your timesheets or roster. The most common error is simply missing shifts.
  2. Your ordinary weekly pay. Pull your last few payslips. Does the OWP figure payroll used match what you actually earn in a normal week, including regular allowances?
  3. Your average weekly earnings. Total your gross earnings for the last 12 months and divide by 52. If payroll's AWE is lower, ask why — missing bonuses or overtime from the total is a frequent cause.
  4. Gross earnings since your last anniversary. This drives the 8% component. Cross-check the start date: it's your last leave anniversary, not the calendar year.
  5. Deductions. Every deduction should map to PAYE, student loan, KiwiSaver, child support — or a specific written authorisation you signed. Anything else is a red flag.

Do this audit with your own spreadsheet, not just by eyeballing the payslip. The OWP-vs-AWE comparison alone is worth hundreds or thousands of dollars, and it's the line most often calculated lazily.
