What Does Boat Insurance Cover?

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

A boat insurance policy has more moving parts than auto insurance. Here's every coverage part, in plain English — what it pays for, what it doesn't, and the add-ons worth considering.

1. Hull coverage (physical damage)

This covers damage to your boat itself — the hull, machinery, fittings and permanently attached equipment. Collision with another boat or a dock, grounding, fire, lightning, theft, vandalism and storm damage are typically covered. Most policies are written on an agreed-value basis: you and the insurer agree on the boat's value upfront, and that's the payout in a total loss — no depreciation argument at claim time. (Some cheaper policies use actual cash value, which subtracts depreciation. Agreed value is usually worth it.)

2. Liability coverage

Pays for injuries to other people and damage to their property when you're at fault — a collision, a wake that swamps a smaller boat, a passenger injured in a fall. Typical limits are $100k–$500k. This is the coverage marinas and lenders care about, and it's the one that protects your savings from a lawsuit. We recommend at least $300k; $500k if you have significant assets.

3. Medical payments

Covers medical bills for you and your passengers after an accident, regardless of fault — usually $1k–$10k. It's inexpensive and particularly useful when family and guests ride along.

4. Uninsured / underinsured boater coverage

The boating equivalent of uninsured-motorist coverage. Since no state requires boat insurance, plenty of boats on the water carry none — this coverage protects you when one of them hits you. Strongly recommended.

5. Towing and assistance

On-water towing, fuel delivery, jump starts and soft ungroundings. A single tow can cost $500–$1,500, so this add-on often pays for itself with one use.

Worthwhile add-ons

Personal effects: covers gear, electronics and fishing equipment aboard (usually $1k–$5k). Trailer coverage: your boat trailer isn't automatically covered — add it. Salvage and wreck removal: recovering a sunk boat can cost tens of thousands; federal law can make you liable for removal. Fuel-spill liability: cleanup liability after a fuel leak. Ice and freeze coverage: for northern owners who winterize. Charter/rental endorsement: standard policies exclude commercial use — you need this if you rent your boat out.

What's usually NOT covered

Normal wear and tear, gradual deterioration, osmosis/blistering, mechanical breakdown (unless caused by a covered event), damage from racing, commercial use without an endorsement, boating outside your stated navigation area, and intentional acts. Read the navigation-area clause carefully — a policy written for inland lakes won't cover a trip offshore.

Agreed value vs actual cash value

This is the most important line in your policy, and many buyers miss it. Agreed value means you and the insurer lock in the boat's worth when the policy starts — in a total loss, you get that number, period. Actual cash value subtracts depreciation, so a five-year-old boat might pay out far less than you expect. Agreed value costs a bit more but eliminates the worst claims argument you'll ever have. For any boat worth more than about $15,000, agreed value is the standard recommendation.

How a boat insurance claim works

Report damage promptly — most policies require notice within days. Document everything: photos of the damage, the scene, and any other vessels involved; note the time, weather, location and witnesses. The insurer assigns a marine surveyor (not a generic auto adjuster) to inspect. Emergency repairs to prevent further damage are usually reimbursable, so act to protect the boat — tarp the hole, get it to a yard — and keep receipts. For liability claims, never admit fault at the scene; exchange information and let the insurer handle it. Claims typically resolve in 2–8 weeks depending on complexity.

Reading your policy: the 5 lines that matter most

When your policy arrives, skip to these five things before anything else: (1) agreed value vs actual cash value — know which you bought; (2) navigation area — the geographic box your coverage lives inside; (3) liability limit — per-occurrence and aggregate; (4) deductible — per claim, and whether it's waived for total losses; (5) lay-up dates — if you have a seasonal endorsement, know exactly when coverage changes. If any of these don't match what you discussed, call your agent before you pay — fixing a policy is easy before it binds and painful after a claim.

How much coverage do you need?

As a rule: agreed-value hull coverage if the boat is worth more than $10k or financed, at least $300k liability, medical payments if you carry passengers, and uninsured-boater coverage as default.
