Do I Need Boat Insurance?

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Here's the surprising truth: no US state requires liability insurance for recreational boats. Unlike car insurance, you can legally launch a boat with zero coverage. But "legal" and "smart" are very different things — and for many owners, insurance is effectively mandatory anyway.

When boat insurance is effectively required

If you financed your boat: virtually every marine lender requires full coverage (hull + liability) for the life of the loan, and they'll be listed as lienholder. Skip it and you're in breach of contract — the lender can force-place expensive coverage and bill you.

If you use a marina: most marinas require proof of liability insurance — commonly $300,000 to $500,000 — before they'll rent you a slip or haul your boat. Yacht clubs, dry-storage facilities and many boat ramps have similar rules.

If you boat in certain waters: some states and local jurisdictions require liability coverage for specific situations, such as boats with motors over a certain horsepower, commercial operation, or boats kept at state-run marinas. Check your state's boating regulations.

Why "not required" doesn't mean "not needed"

Consider the math. The average boat insurance policy costs $300–$500 a year (see 2026 cost breakdown). One bad day can cost far more:

  • A grounding that cracks the hull: $5,000–$25,000 in repairs.
  • A collision where you're at fault: $50,000–$500,000+ in liability — injuries, another boat's hull, environmental cleanup.
  • A sunk boat you must salvage: federal law can make you pay for wreck removal, often $10,000–$50,000.
  • A guest injured in a fall or tubing accident: medical bills and a potential lawsuit against your assets.

One claim can wipe out decades of "saved" premiums. That's why financial planners treat boat insurance like homeowner's insurance: not legally required everywhere, but financially reckless to skip.

When going uninsured is defensible

There is one narrow case where skipping hull coverage makes sense: a low-value boat (under ~$10k), owned outright, trailered at home, used on calm inland water — where you could truly afford to replace it and you carry an umbrella policy for liability. Even then, liability-only coverage at $150–$300/year is usually the smarter play. Our coverage quiz tells you in 60 seconds which camp you're in.

What happens if you boat uninsured

Legally, usually nothing — until something goes wrong. Then you're personally liable for every dollar: the other party's injuries, their boat, environmental cleanup, and your own wreck. If you cause a serious accident without liability coverage, a judgment can follow your wages and assets for years. And remember: because boat insurance isn't mandatory, many boats around you are uninsured — which is exactly why uninsured-boater coverage on your own policy matters (see what boat insurance covers).

How lenders and marinas verify your insurance

Lenders don't take your word for it. Your marine lender will require a declarations page naming them as lienholder (or "loss payee") and will check annually that the policy is active — if it lapses, they can force-place their own coverage, which typically costs 2–3× a normal policy and protects only their interest. Marinas are simpler: most ask for a certificate of insurance at slip-signing and renewal, showing liability limits that meet their minimum (usually $300k). Keep a PDF of your policy on your phone; you'll need it more often than you think, from haul-out yards to yacht clubs.

State-by-state: where rules are strictest

While no state mandates recreational boat liability insurance across the board, enforcement-style rules cluster in specific situations. Florida doesn't require insurance but leads the nation in registered boats and uninsured-boater accidents — making uninsured-boater coverage especially important there. California requires liability coverage for boats kept at certain state-managed facilities. Several states require proof of financial responsibility after an accident, similar to SR-22 rules for cars — meaning one crash without insurance can force you into expensive mandatory coverage afterward. And Coast Guard-documented vessels operating commercially always need coverage. Bottom line: check your specific state's boating handbook and your marina's contract, not just the general rule.

The real cost of going bare

Let's put numbers on the gamble. A typical policy costs roughly $400/year. Over 10 years that's $4,000 in premiums. Now compare: one grounding repair ($8,000), one at-fault collision with injuries ($150,000+), one sunk-boat salvage ($25,000). You don't need all three to lose — any single one dwarfs a decade of premiums. Insurance is a bad investment if you only look at years without claims; it's priced for the one year everything goes wrong. Boaters who self-insure successfully share one trait: enough liquid wealth that a total loss is an annoyance, not a crisis. If that's not you, the premium is the cheaper option.

Bottom line

The law probably doesn't require boat insurance — but your lender, your marina, and basic financial self-defense do. At a few hundred dollars a year, it's one of the cheapest ways to protect a five- or six-figure asset and everything else you own.
