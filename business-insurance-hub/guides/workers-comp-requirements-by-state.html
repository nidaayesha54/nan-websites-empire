The general rule

Workers' compensation is legally required in nearly every US state once you have employees. It pays medical costs and lost wages for work-related injuries and illnesses — and in exchange, employees generally can't sue you for those injuries (the "exclusive remedy" bargain). Only Texas makes it broadly optional for private employers; everywhere else, assume you must carry it.

Employee thresholds by state

Most states require coverage from the very first employee. Notable variations:

Thresholds change — verify your state's current rule before relying on this table.

Common exemptions

  • Sole proprietors and partners — usually exempt (but can often opt in)
  • Independent contractors — true contractors are excluded, but misclassification is heavily penalized; states look at control, not the 1099
  • Agricultural and domestic workers — exempt or under special rules in many states
  • Corporate officers / LLC members — some states allow (or require) exclusion elections

Penalties for going without

States take this seriously: fines of $1,000+ per day uninsured, stop-work orders that shut you down, personal liability for officers, and — worst of all — paying an injured worker's full medical bills and lost wages out of pocket. A single serious injury can cost more than a decade of premiums.

How to get covered

  1. Private carriers — shop 3+ agents; identical risk profiles quote 20–40% apart
  2. State funds — competitive or assigned-risk pools for hard-to-place businesses
  3. PEOs — professional employer organizations bundle workers' comp with payroll/HR
