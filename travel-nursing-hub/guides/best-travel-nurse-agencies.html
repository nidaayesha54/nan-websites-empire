How to Choose a Travel Nurse Agency

The questions that separate great agencies from the rest — and the red flags that should send you running.

Home › Guides › How to Choose a Travel Nurse Agency

Updated October 12, 2026 · 8 min read

Your agency controls your pay, your housing, your benefits, and who answers the phone at 2 a.m. when a facility cancels your contract. Yet most new travelers pick the first recruiter who calls them back. There are hundreds of travel nurse agencies — from national giants to boutique firms — and the differences between them are worth thousands of dollars per contract. Here is how to choose well.

Work with 2–3 agencies, not one

This is the single most valuable piece of agency advice: never rely on a single recruiter. Different agencies hold different exclusive contracts with facilities, and pay for the same job can vary by hundreds of dollars per week between agencies. Keeping two or three active agency relationships lets you compare every offer — which is exactly what our contract comparator is built for.

What to evaluate: the checklist

1. Pay transparency

A good agency sends the full itemized pay breakdown — hourly rate, guaranteed hours, housing stipend, M&IE, every bonus — in writing, before you sign anything. Ask for the bill rate too. Agencies that quote only a weekly total, or that get defensive about transparency, are telling you everything you need to know.

2. Recruiter quality

You are really choosing a recruiter, not a logo. The best recruiters answer calls and texts promptly, warn you about difficult units, push back on bad facility terms, and remember your preferences. Test responsiveness before you need it: if a recruiter takes three days to reply during courtship, imagine contract week eleven.

3. Benefits that start day one

Compare health insurance (start date, premiums, network), 401(k) matching and vesting, PTO accrual, and professional liability coverage. Day-one benefits beat "after 30 days" benefits — a gap in coverage between contracts is a real risk. Ask for the benefits summary document, not a verbal summary.

4. Housing support

Decide whether you want agency-provided housing or the stipend. If you take the stipend — usually the better financial move — check whether the agency helps with the housing search, covers deposits, or has partnerships with short-term rental platforms. Also confirm the housing stipend reflects realistic local costs, not an inflated number masking a low taxable rate.

5. Compliance and credentialing

Good agencies make onboarding painless: they pay for required certifications, reimburse licensing fees, and have a credentialing team that actually tracks deadlines. Ask who handles your file and how they communicate — disorganized credentialing delays start dates and costs you pay.

6. Support during the contract

Ask directly: "If my contract is cancelled mid-assignment, what do you do?" and "Who do I call on a weekend if payroll is wrong?" The answers reveal whether you are getting a partner or a placement mill. Look for agencies with dedicated housing, payroll, and clinical support teams — not one overwhelmed recruiter doing everything.

7. Contract terms

Compare cancellation clauses (how much notice the facility must give you), guaranteed hours, overtime and holiday pay rules, and extension bonus policies. Two agencies can offer the same weekly pay with very different downside protection.

Red flags: walk away when you see these

  • Pressure to sign today. Legitimate contracts do not evaporate in hours; urgency is a sales tactic.
  • Verbal-only pay quotes. If it is not in writing, it does not exist.
  • Non-compete clauses that restrict which facilities or agencies you can work with afterward.
  • Fees charged to you — legitimate agencies are paid by the facility, never by the nurse.
  • Ghosting after submission. If communication dies once your profile is submitted, it will not improve on contract.
  • Stipends that look engineered — a suspiciously low taxable rate with maxed-out stipends can create tax risk for you. See our tax tips article.

Big agency vs. boutique: which is better?

Large agencies offer more job volume, established compliance systems, and 24/7 support lines. Boutique agencies often offer higher pay (lower overhead), a more personal recruiter relationship, and flexibility on terms. Many experienced travelers keep one of each: the big agency for volume and reliability, the boutique for pay maximization. There is no universally "best" agency — only the best fit for your specialty, region, and working style.

Questions to ask every recruiter (copy-paste list)

  1. Can you send the full itemized pay breakdown and the bill rate?
  2. What are the guaranteed hours and the cancellation clause?
  3. When do health benefits start, and what do they cost?
  4. Do you reimburse licensing and certification costs?
  5. Who handles housing, payroll, and compliance — and how do I reach them?
  6. What happens if my contract is cancelled early?
  7. What is your extension and referral bonus policy?

A recruiter who answers all seven clearly, in writing, has earned your shortlist.

Putting it together
