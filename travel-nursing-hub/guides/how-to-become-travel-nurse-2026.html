How to Become a Travel Nurse in 2026

The complete step-by-step path from staff nurse to your first 13-week travel contract.

Home › Guides › How to Become a Travel Nurse in 2026

Updated October 12, 2026 · 9 min read

Travel nursing remains one of the most rewarding career moves a registered nurse can make: higher pay, new cities every few months, and a front-row seat to how different hospitals operate. But the path from staff nurse to traveler has real requirements, and skipping steps is the fastest way to a miserable first contract. Here is the honest, step-by-step roadmap for 2026.

Step 1: Meet the non-negotiable requirements

Before anything else, make sure you qualify:

  • Active RN license — an ADN or BSN both qualify; the license matters more than the degree.
  • 1–2 years of recent bedside experience in your specialty. Most agencies require at least 12 months, and competitive contracts want 18–24. Travelers are expected to hit the ground running with minimal orientation.
  • Specialty certifications — BLS is universal; add ACLS for critical care, PALS/NRP for peds and L&D. Certifications expand the contracts you qualify for.
  • A clean record — background checks and drug screens are standard; licensing issues must be disclosed and will limit options.

The single biggest gatekeeper is specialty experience. ICU, ER, OR, L&D, med-surg/tele, psych and home health all travel well, but the highest demand (and pay) concentrates in critical care and emergency specialties.

Step 2: Get your licensing strategy right

If you live in a Nurse Licensure Compact (NLC) state, claim your multistate license — it lets you practice in every compact state without extra applications. If you live in a non-compact state (California, New York, and others), you will apply for licensure by endorsement in each assignment state.

Practical advice: start endorsement paperwork for one or two target states before you need them, because boards can take 4–8 weeks. Good agencies reimburse licensing fees and some will walk your application through. Ask about this when you evaluate agencies — our agency guide lists the questions that matter.

Step 3: Choose your specialty positioning

Generalists get contracts; specialists get great contracts. If you are med-surg, consider adding tele or stepdown competency. If you are ICU, float experience makes you far more marketable. Every additional unit type you can safely cover multiplies your options, especially in the January and September hiring surges.

Step 4: Pick an agency (or two)

You do not marry one agency — most experienced travelers work with two or three simultaneously to compare offers. What matters: transparent pay breakdowns, a recruiter who answers the phone, real housing support, and benefits that start on day one. Red flags include vague "trust me" pay quotes, pressure to sign within hours, and agencies that cannot show you the itemized pay package.

Once you have offers, run every package through our pay package analyzer and put competing offers into the contract comparator. The numbers do not lie; recruiter enthusiasm does not count.

Step 5: Nail the submission and interview

Your recruiter submits your profile — resume, references, skills checklist — to the facility. If selected, expect a 15–30 minute phone interview with the unit manager. Prepare two clinical scenarios that show critical thinking, and ask about ratios, charting systems, and floating policies. A contract you decline after a bad interview costs nothing; a bad contract costs 13 weeks.

Step 6: Handle housing and the tax home

You have two options: take agency-provided housing or take the tax-free housing stipend and find your own place. The stipend route usually pays better and gives you control — furnished finder platforms, extended-stay hotels, and short-term rentals near the hospital are the standard playbook.

Critical: tax-free stipends legally require a tax home — a place where you maintain living expenses and return to regularly. Travelers who hop indefinitely without a tax home risk the IRS reclassifying stipends as taxable. Our travel nurse tax tips article covers the essentials, and a tax professional who knows traveler rules is worth every penny.

Step 7: Survive and thrive on contract #1

Your first two weeks set the tone. Arrive early, learn the charting system fast, say yes to help, and keep a small notebook of unit-specific protocols. Travelers are judged quickly — competence and attitude get you requested back, extended, and referred. Extensions and return offers are where the best pay lives, because facilities pay premiums to keep a known quantity.

Common first-timer mistakes

  • Chasing the highest weekly rate blindly. A $3,000/week contract in a brutal unit with forced overtime can be worse than $2,400/week somewhere sustainable. Compare the full package and the working conditions.
  • Signing without guaranteed hours. If the contract does not guarantee 36 (or 40) hours, a low-census week can slash your pay. Confirm the guarantee in writing.
  • Ignoring the cancellation clause. Most contracts let the facility cancel with 1–2 weeks' notice. Know your clause and keep an emergency fund.
  • Working with only one recruiter. Competition between recruiters is how you discover what an offer is really worth.

Your first-year timeline

A realistic pace: months 1–3 to finalize licensing and agency relationships, contract #1 in months 4–6, and by month 12 you will have 2–3 contracts under your belt, a feel for which regions and unit types suit you, and a recruiter shortlist you trust. Travelers who treat the first year as paid education — about pay structures, taxes, and their own preferences — build the highest-earning careers afterward.
