How Often Should You Groom Your Dog? 📖

The honest, coat-by-coat answer — because a Husky and a Poodle do not live on the same schedule.

"How often should I groom my dog?" is one of the most-searched dog care questions in the US — and the real answer is: it depends on your dog's coat. Bath a short-coated Lab as often as a Poodle and you'll dry out its skin; brush a Maltese as rarely as a Beagle and you'll get painful mats. This guide gives you the full frequency chart by coat type, plus the factors that shift the schedule.

Bathing: How Often Is Too Often?

Most US vets and groomers agree: once a month is the sweet spot for the average dog. Bathing too often strips the natural oils that protect skin and coat, causing dryness, flaking, and itching. Bathing too rarely lets dirt, allergens, and odor build up.

Exceptions that justify more frequent baths: hairless breeds (weekly — their exposed skin collects oil and grime), dogs with certain skin conditions (on vet advice), and dogs that swim regularly or roll in unpleasant things. Active outdoor dogs may need a bath every 2–3 weeks rather than monthly. Always use a dog-specific shampoo — human shampoo has the wrong pH and dries canine skin out fast.

Brushing by Coat Type

Brushing is the most underestimated grooming task. It's not just about looks — regular brushing distributes skin oils, removes loose and dead hair, prevents painful matting, and gives you a weekly health check for lumps, ticks, and skin issues.

  • Short coats: weekly with a rubber curry brush or bristle brush. Quick, easy, and dramatically reduces shedding.
  • Double coats: 3–4 times per week with an undercoat rake during shedding season (spring and fall "blowouts"), weekly otherwise. Never shave a double coat — it regulates temperature and may not grow back properly.
  • Long and curly coats: daily to every-other-day with a slicker brush followed by a steel comb. If the comb snags, there's a mat forming — work it out with detangler before it tightens against the skin.
  • Wire coats: 2–3 times per week. Many wire coats are hand-stripped rather than clipped to preserve texture.

Nail Trims and Paw Care

Nails that click on hard floors are too long. Overgrown nails change how your dog stands and walks, stressing joints over time. Most dogs need a trim every 3–4 weeks; small breeds and seniors often need it every 2–3 weeks because they wear their nails down less. Active dogs that run on pavement may go longer between trims.

Trim small slivers at a 45° angle, stopping 2–3 mm before the quick (the pink blood vessel inside the nail). Nervous? Our interactive nail trimming guide shows you exactly where to cut with a live safety simulator.

Haircuts and Coat Maintenance

Not every dog needs a haircut — Labs and Beagles never do. But continuously growing coats (Poodles, Shih Tzus, Maltese, Doodles, Yorkies) need a full groom every 6–8 weeks, or the coat mats into painful clumps. Ears, Teeth, and the Forgotten Tasks

Two grooming tasks owners most often skip:

  • Ears: check weekly, clean gently when you see wax or debris. Floppy-eared and swimming breeds are prone to infections — redness, odor, or head-shaking means a vet visit, not just a cleaning.
  • Teeth: brush 2–3 times per week minimum (daily is ideal) with dog toothpaste. Dental disease affects most dogs by age three and is entirely preventable with routine brushing.

What Changes Your Dog's Schedule

Treat the chart as a baseline, then adjust for:

  • Lifestyle: farm dogs, hikers, and swimmers need more baths; apartment couch potatoes need fewer.
  • Allergies and skin conditions: your vet may prescribe medicated baths weekly or more.
  • Season: double-coated breeds "blow" their undercoat in spring and fall — double your brushing during those weeks.
  • Age: puppies need gentle, short, positive grooming sessions to build good habits; seniors may need more frequent nail trims and gentler handling.
