Ghana Overtime Pay Rules Explained

How overtime pay works under Ghana's Labour Act 2003 (Act 651): the 1.5x/2x market standard, calculating your hourly rate, and your rights.

Home  /  Guides

Ghana's Labour Act, 2003 (Act 651) caps the working week at 40 hours — 8 hours a day. Anything beyond that is overtime, and it must be paid. But the Act doesn't hand you a single fixed multiplier the way some countries do. This guide explains what the law actually requires, what the market practice is, and how to calculate exactly what you're owed.

What counts as overtime

Overtime is any work beyond 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week. The Act also gives every worker at least a 30-minute daily rest break and 48 consecutive hours of weekly rest. Two legal points that surprise many workers:

  1. The rate must be fixed before overtime is worked. Overtime is only lawful where the contract or a collective agreement sets the rate in advance. An employer who springs extra hours on you with no agreed rate is already in breach.
  2. You can't be compelled — with exceptions. Workers can't be forced to work overtime except where it's necessary for the viability of the enterprise or in emergencies. Reasonable, agreed overtime is normal; compulsory unpaid extra hours are not.

The multipliers: law vs. practice

The Act itself does not prescribe a single statutory multiplier. Instead, the rates are set in employment contracts and collective bargaining agreements — and across Ghanaian workplaces they have converged on a clear standard:

Weekday overtime (beyond 8 hours/day)1.5× the normal hourly rate ("time and a half")
Rest days (typically Saturday/Sunday)2× the normal hourly rate ("double time")
Public holidays2× the normal hourly rate ("double time")

These are the figures you'll find in Ghanaian collective agreements — for example, agreements filed with the labour authorities commonly specify time-and-a-half for Monday–Friday overtime and double time for Saturdays, Sundays and public holidays. Your own contract is the final authority, but if your contract is silent, these are the benchmarks to negotiate from.

How to find your hourly rate

Overtime multiplies your hourly rate, so you need that first. The standard Ghanaian method, used in collective agreements, is:

Hourly rate = Monthly basic salary ÷ 27 days ÷ 8 hours

Example: a monthly basic salary of GH₵3,000 ÷ 27 ÷ 8 = GH₵13.89 per hour.

Then apply the multipliers: 10 weekday overtime hours = 10 × 13.89 × 1.5 = GH₵208.33. Four rest-day hours = 4 × 13.89 × 2 = GH₵111.11. Total overtime for the month: GH₵319.44 on top of the basic salary.

Night work and other premiums

Night shift work is compensated at a premium under specific provisions, and the Act regulates night work with additional safety requirements. If you regularly work nights, check that your contract prices it separately from ordinary overtime — many collective agreements do.

Related rights worth knowing

  • Annual leave: minimum 15 working days of paid annual leave after 12 months of continuous service, accruing at about 1.25 days per month. Unused leave must be paid out on termination.
  • Public holidays: employers must pay holiday wages for public holidays — working one earns you the overtime premium above.
  • Minimum wage: set by the National Tripartite Committee and reviewed periodically; your overtime hourly rate can never fall below the pro-rated minimum.
  • SSNIT: your employer must register you and remit both employer and employee social security contributions monthly.

If your overtime isn't paid

  1. Document everything. Keep your own log of overtime hours, dates and who authorised them — don't rely on memory or the company's records alone.
  2. Raise it in writing with HR or management, referencing your contract's overtime clause.
  3. Escalate to the National Labour Commission if internal channels fail. Unpaid overtime with a pre-agreed rate is one of the clearest claims a worker can bring.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 1.5× overtime rate actually in the Labour Act?

The Act requires overtime to be compensated and requires the rate to be fixed in the contract or collective agreement — but it doesn't fix one universal multiplier. In practice, 1.5× for weekday overtime and 2× for rest days and public holidays is the established Ghanaian standard.

Can my employer force me to work overtime?

Not as a routine matter. The Act says workers can't be compelled to work overtime except where necessary for the viability of the enterprise or in emergencies. Agreed, paid overtime is normal; forced unpaid overtime is not.

Are part-time or casual workers entitled to overtime?

Overtime is defined against the 8-hour day / 40-hour week standard. Casual and part-time workers' entitlements follow their contracts — check the hours threshold in yours.

Does overtime count toward my SSNIT or pension?

Overtime pay is part of your earnings and is generally subject to the same statutory deductions as ordinary pay, including PAYE. Your contract and payroll determine the exact treatment.

Shift workers, casuals and the 27-day divisor

The "monthly salary ÷ 27 ÷ 8" formula deserves a closer look, because it's where most overtime disputes actually start. The 27 represents working days in a month (30/31 days minus Sundays), and the 8 represents standard hours per day. It's the convention embedded in Ghanaian collective agreements — but it's not the only possible divisor, and your contract's method is what legally counts.

Shift workers: if your roster doesn't follow the Monday–Friday pattern — nurses, security, factory shifts — your "overtime" threshold still starts from the 8-hour day / 40-hour week standard unless your agreement says otherwise. Hours beyond your rostered shift that push past 8 in a day are overtime, even if your weekly total is under 40. Keep your roster and actual hours side by side; that's your evidence.

Casual workers: casual engagements often pay a daily rate with no overtime clause at all. If you regularly work beyond 8 hours, negotiate the overtime rate before accepting the engagement — the Act's requirement that rates be fixed in advance protects you only if there's a contract to fix them in. A WhatsApp message confirming the rate counts as an agreement; a verbal promise doesn't.

Salaried staff told they're "exempt": Ghanaian law doesn't have a broad managerial exemption from overtime the way some countries do. If your contract says all hours are covered by your salary, that clause is the rate agreement — but it must be genuine and conscionable. Senior managers on all-in salaries are the norm; junior staff on all-in salaries doing regular 60-hour weeks should question it.

Overtime vs. "extra duties": what employers try

Watch for these re-labelling tactics:

  • "It's part of your job description." A job description can include occasional extra effort, but it can't silently absorb systematic overtime. If the extra hours are regular and predictable, they're overtime.
  • Time off in lieu instead of pay. TOIL is legal if agreed — an hour off for an hour worked — but it must be genuinely equivalent and actually granted. TOIL that never materialises is unpaid overtime.
  • "Voluntary" overtime that's not voluntary. If refusing means a poor appraisal or losing shifts, it's compulsory in practice. The Act's protection against compelled overtime looks at reality, not labels.
  • Splitting pay to shrink the hourly rate. Some payslips bury allowances outside "basic salary" to lower the overtime base. Your contract defines what counts as the rate — read it, and compare with colleagues.

The pattern in every case: the employer benefits from ambiguity, so your defence is documentation. Log hours daily, keep the contract, and put every agreement about rates in writing.
