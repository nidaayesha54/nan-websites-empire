Canada Maternity & Parental Leave 2026: EI Benefits, Top-Ups & Income Planning

Planning a baby in Canada? Your income on leave comes from three pieces: EI maternity benefits, EI parental benefits (standard or extended), and possibly an employer top-up. Here's how the 2026 numbers fit together — and how to plan so there's no nasty surprise.

The three pieces of leave income

Most Canadians' parental-leave income is a stack of up to three layers:

  1. EI maternity benefits — 15 weeks, birth parent only, 55% of insurable earnings.
  2. EI parental benefits — shareable between parents; you choose standard (55%, shorter) or extended (33%, longer).
  3. Employer top-up — optional extra pay from your employer that bridges part of the gap to your normal salary.

EI benefits are taxable income — tax is deducted from the payments, and they count toward your annual income at filing time.

EI in 2026: rates, maximums, and weeks

Maternity (birth parent only)55% · max $729/week · up to 15 weeks
Standard parental55% · max $729/week · up to 35 weeks per parent (40 shared)
Extended parental33% · max $437/week · up to 61 weeks per parent (69 shared)

These maximums are tied to the 2026 maximum insurable earnings of $68,900/year. Earn above that and your benefit doesn't rise further. To qualify you need 600 insurable hours in the 52 weeks before your claim (about 4 months of full-time work). There's a one-week unpaid waiting period at the start of the claim — serve it once for a combined maternity + parental claim.

Timing rules: maternity can start up to 12 weeks before the due date; standard parental weeks must be used within 52 weeks of birth/placement, extended within 78 weeks. Both parents must choose the same option — you can't mix standard and extended — and the choice is final once payments begin. There's also a sharing incentive: if both parents take at least some leave, the family gets 5 extra weeks (standard) or 8 extra weeks (extended).

Low-income families (net income ≤ $25,921 with a child receiving the Canada Child Benefit) may get the family supplement added to the weekly payment.

Standard vs extended parental: the real trade-off

The total dollars paid out over the whole leave are roughly the same either way — extended just spreads a similar sum over more weeks at a lower weekly rate. So the choice is about cash flow vs time:

  • Standard (12 months): higher weekly income, back to work sooner. Better if your budget is tight or your career momentum matters.
  • Extended (18 months): more time with the baby, but roughly 40% less per week. Better if you can absorb the lower income (savings, partner income, top-up).

Common mistake: choosing extended for the extra time without budgeting the lower weekly amount — then struggling at month 10. Run both scenarios on our top-up calculator before you decide — our salary tools can help you model the monthly budget around each option.

Employer top-ups: how they work

A top-up is extra pay from your employer during leave — e.g. "93% of salary for 17 weeks" or "100% for 12 weeks." Key rules:

  • A proper top-up doesn't reduce your EI. Combined EI + top-up must not exceed your normal weekly earnings, or EI claws back the excess.
  • Top-ups are not legally required — they're a benefit some employers (governments, universities, banks, tech) offer to stay competitive.
  • Many plans require you to return to work for a set period (often 6 months) or repay the top-up if you resign. Read the fine print before counting on it.
  • You must report the top-up when applying for EI.
  • Top-up weeks often cover only part of the leave — know exactly which weeks, because EI continues after the top-up ends.

Worked example: $75,000 salary

Salary $75,000/year → above the $68,900 MIE, so EI pays the maximums. Employer tops up to 93% for 17 weeks.

Normal weekly earnings$75,000 ÷ 52 = $1,442
EI maternity (15 wks)$729/week → $10,935
Weeks 1–17 with top-up to 93%EI $729 + employer top-up $612 = $1,341/week
Standard parental after top-up ends$729/week EI only
Extended parental (if chosen)$437/week EI only

See the cliff: when the 17-week top-up ends, weekly income drops from $1,341 to $729 (standard) or $437 (extended). Budget for the cliff, not the peak.

Eligibility and how to apply

  • 600 insurable hours in the last 52 weeks (check your pay stubs for EI deductions).
  • Apply through Service Canada (online via canada.ca) as soon as you stop working — don't wait weeks, because the claim start date matters.
  • Have your ROE (Record of Employment) ready; employers file it electronically in most cases.
  • Expect the first payment about 28 days after applying, after the one-week waiting period.
  • Self-employed? You can opt into the EI program voluntarily — but you must register 12 months before claiming, so plan ahead.
  • Quebec runs its own program (QPIP), not EI — different rates and rules apply there.

Planning checklist

  1. 12 months out: confirm 600 insurable hours; self-employed? register for EI now.
  2. 6 months out: ask HR for the exact top-up policy in writing (percentage, weeks, return-to-work clause).
  3. 3 months out: model both standard and extended scenarios on our calculator — including the post-top-up cliff.
  4. Before leave: build a 2-month expense buffer; the first EI payment takes ~28 days. Also check any unused vacation pay owed to you — it's often paid out when leave starts and pads the buffer.
  5. During leave: file any required EI reports on time; report any earnings.
  6. Tax time: remember EI is taxable — set aside a little each month or face a bill in April.

The parental sharing benefit: free extra weeks

Buried in the rules is a genuine freebie: if both parents each take at least some parental leave, the family unlocks bonus weeks — 5 extra weeks on standard (40 → 45 total) or 8 extra on extended (69 → 77 total). These bonus weeks are lost entirely if only one parent claims. The second parent needs their own 600 insurable hours to qualify. For couples where the second parent assumed "there's no point," those 5–8 weeks at 55%/33% are found money — plus the bonding time research keeps validating.

Job protection: your leave vs your job

EI pays the money; provincial employment standards protect the job. Every province guarantees job-protected maternity + parental leave (lengths vary slightly — e.g. Ontario: 17 weeks pregnancy leave + 61/63 weeks parental). Your employer must reinstate you to the same or comparable position at the same pay. They can't fire you for taking leave, and the protection covers the full leave period even if it exceeds the EI benefit weeks. Know your province's specific statute — it's separate from the federal EI program.

Can you work while on EI parental leave?

Yes, with rules. You can earn income while collecting parental benefits, but earnings are deducted from your EI at 50 cents per dollar up to a threshold (90% of your prior weekly earnings), and dollar-for-dollar beyond it. Small freelance gigs are manageable; effectively returning to full-time work ends the claim. Always report earnings — unreported income discovered later means overpayment debts plus potential penalties. And remember: working doesn't extend your benefit weeks.

The hidden cost nobody mentions: CPP and retirement

EI parental benefits don't come with CPP contributions the way salary does — those 12–18 months create a small gap in your CPP contributory record. The good news: the child-rearing provision lets you exclude low-earning child-rearing years from your CPP calculation (for children under 7), largely neutralizing the hit. You must apply for it — it's not automatic. One form today protects hundreds in future monthly CPP.
