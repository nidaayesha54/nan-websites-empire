What Does Travel Insurance Actually Cover?

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

"Travel insurance" sounds like one thing, but it's actually a bundle of six or seven different coverages — and the fine print decides which of them pays when something goes wrong. This guide explains each coverage in plain English, shows typical benefit limits, and lists the exclusions that surprise travelers most.

1. Trip Cancellation (the headline coverage)

Reimburses your prepaid, non-refundable trip costs if you must cancel before departure for a covered reason. Typical covered reasons include: sudden illness or injury (you or a traveling companion), a family member's serious illness or death, severe weather that shuts down travel, jury duty, job loss, and terrorist incidents at your destination.

Typical limit: 100% of insured trip cost. Key detail: you only get back what you insured. If your trip costs $8,000 but you only insured $5,000, that's your ceiling.

2. Trip Interruption

Covers you if your trip is cut short after departure — you get sick mid-trip, a family emergency calls you home, or a hurricane evacuates the resort. It pays the unused prepaid portion plus extra transportation to get home (or rejoin the trip).

Typical limit: 100–150% of trip cost (the extra covers pricey last-minute flights home). This is the coverage that saves cruise passengers who miss the ship's departure and must fly to the next port.

3. Emergency Medical

Pays for doctor visits, hospital stays, and prescriptions if you get sick or injured while traveling. This is arguably the most important coverage for international trips, because most US health plans (including many employer plans and Medicare, which generally doesn't cover abroad) offer little or no coverage outside the country.

Typical limits: $50,000–$500,000. For international travel, don't accept less than $100,000; $250,000+ is safer for remote destinations. A single air-ambulance evacuation can cost $50,000–$100,000 — which is why the next coverage matters just as much.

4. Medical Evacuation & Repatriation

Pays to transport you to the nearest adequate hospital — or home — if local care can't treat you. Includes helicopter rescue from remote areas, air ambulances, and, in the worst case, repatriation of remains.

Typical limits: $250,000–$1,000,000. Given that one evacuation can exceed $100,000, this is the coverage where "more is better" genuinely applies. Budget plans sometimes cap this at $100,000 — too low for places like rural Asia or Africa.

5. Baggage Loss, Theft & Delay

Lost/stolen baggage: reimburses the depreciated value of your belongings, typically $1,000–$3,000 per person, with per-item sub-limits (often $250–$500 — so that $2,000 camera may not be fully covered).

Baggage delay: pays a fixed daily amount for essentials (toiletries, a change of clothes) if your bags are delayed, typically $100–$300/day after a 6–12 hour waiting period. Check the waiting period — 24-hour triggers are much less useful.

6. Travel Delay

Pays for meals, hotels, and rebooking costs when your trip is delayed by a covered reason (weather, mechanical issues, strikes). Typical: $150–$300/day, up to $1,500–$2,000 total, kicking in after 6–12 hours. Note: this usually does not cover delays caused by the airline going bankrupt — that's a separate "financial default" coverage some plans include.

7. Optional Upgrades Worth Knowing

  • CFAR (Cancel For Any Reason): lets you cancel for reasons not on the covered list — you just changed your mind. Costs ~40–50% extra, reimburses only 50–75%, and must usually be bought within 14–21 days of first payment.
  • Pre-existing condition waiver: normally free if you buy within 14–21 days of your first trip payment. Without it, flare-ups of known conditions may be excluded.
  • Adventure sports rider: standard plans exclude skiing, scuba, paragliding, and high-altitude hiking. If your trip includes them, you need this rider.
  • Rental car coverage: collision coverage for rental cars abroad, where your US auto policy doesn't apply.

9 Common Exclusions (What Is NOT Covered)

  1. Known events: you can't buy hurricane coverage after the storm is named, or cancel-for-illness coverage after you're already sick.
  2. Pre-existing conditions without the waiver described above.
  3. High-risk activities without the adventure rider (skiing, scuba, bungee jumping, mountaineering).
  4. Alcohol/drug-related incidents — injuries while intoxicated are routinely excluded.
  5. War, civil unrest, and (often) pandemics — check whether "epidemic" is excluded in your plan.
  6. Routine or elective medical care — only emergencies.
  7. Losses with no documentation — no receipts, no police report for theft, no claim. Keep everything.
  8. Travel against government advisories — visiting a country under a "do not travel" warning can void coverage.
  9. Mental health events — many plans limit or exclude these; read the wording.
The golden rule: "covered reason" lists are everything. Two plans can both say "trip cancellation" but cover totally different reasons. Before you buy, use our pre-trip checklist generator to verify each coverage against your actual trip.

How to Read a Policy in 10 Minutes

Skip the marketing page. Open the actual policy document (sometimes called "certificate of insurance") and search for three things: the covered reasons list for cancellation, the exclusions section, and the benefit schedule with dollar limits. If you can't find all three in ten minutes, that's a red flag about the insurer — not about you.
