GTA 6 AI Video Prompts: Make Vice City Clips That Look Real

November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

Grand Theft Auto 6 launched on November 19, 2026, but for months before that, millions of people were already watching "GTA 6 gameplay" that was never filmed at all. A wave of AI-generated videos — built with Veo 3.1 and Kling, voiced with cloned audio, sometimes scored with the trailers' own music — racked up millions of views, and Take-Two answered with DMCA takedowns. The videos were fake. The technique behind them was real, and it is a free masterclass in AI video generation.

This guide teaches that technique honestly: how to write prompts that make Vice City-style clips with Veo, Kling and Runway, five copy-paste prompts to start from, and the one rule that keeps you on the right side of the takedowns — label your clips as AI, and never present them as gameplay.

How the fake GTA 6 videos were made

The pipeline has five steps, and the first is the one most people miss: reuse audio that already exists. Many of the viral clips generate no audio at all — they take the score and narration from the trailers and generate only the picture on top. The second step is the prompt itself, and it is where the craft lives: current video models follow structured instructions far more faithfully than running text. A prose description becomes an average; a structured prompt becomes a specification.

The third step is the model choice. Veo 3.1 and Kling are the two names that keep appearing in the breakdowns of the viral clips. Sora 2 is out of the game — its app shut down in April 2026 and its API switched off on September 24, 2026. The fourth step is consistency: the same character, the same car, the same street, described the same way in every prompt, so the clips cut together. The fifth is honesty — or the lack of it. The first wave of fake clips in 2025 ended with an apology. The 2026 wave ended with DMCA takedowns, because the clips were presented as the game.

How each model reads a prompt

The same idea needs a different shape for each model, because they respond to different structures. Veo does well with one flowing paragraph that names the shot, the subject and the place, followed by lighting and mood, then a separate line for sound — Veo can generate audio, so an audio line is useful there. Sora does well with a clear scene description, then short labelled lines for camera, light, tone and sound.

Kling does well with comma-separated visual descriptors, plus a negative prompt line listing what you do not want — blur, text, watermarks. Runway does well when the camera movement comes first, then the action of the subject, then the look of the scene. These are habits to start from, not rules, and the vendors change their models often — always test a short clip before you spend credits on a long one.

Two habits work everywhere. Name the camera — "tracking shot", "low-angle dolly" and "handheld" steer the motion more reliably than adjectives. And set the aspect ratio in the prompt: 9:16 for Shorts, Reels and TikTok. Avoid real names, brands and game titles in prompts — platforms often block them, so describe the look instead of naming it.

Five copy-paste prompts

Each prompt below is written for the model it suits best. Change one detail at a time and re-roll — changing five things at once teaches you nothing about which one worked.

1. Neon night chase (Veo). Tracking shot following a sports car with a driver in a loud shirt as it drifts through a corner while a police car follows. Setting: a wet coastal highway lined with palm trees and pink-and-teal neon signs. Lighting: neon-lit night. Mood: tense, stylish, high energy. Audio: engine roar, tyre squeal, synth music. Aspect ratio 9:16.

2. Beach boardwalk (Veo). Handheld, slightly shaky, a group of friends on rollerblades and bikes weave through the crowd, laughing. Setting: a busy sunny beachfront boardwalk with art-deco buildings and palm trees. Lighting: golden hour sun. Mood: carefree summer, sun-soaked. Audio: crowd chatter, waves, upbeat pop. Aspect ratio 9:16.

3. Rainy Vice City night (Kling). Rain-slick street, neon reflections, palm trees, art-deco facades, lone figure in a yellow raincoat walking toward camera, cinematic, photoreal, 35mm. Negative prompt: blur, text, watermark, distorted hands, extra fingers.

4. Coastal drive (Runway). Slow aerial dolly forward over a coastal boulevard at sunset, a red off-road truck driving below, palm trees and ocean on the left, warm golden light, cinematic, photoreal.

5. Motel morning (Sora). Scene: a sun-bleached roadside motel at dawn, a couple loading bags into a pickup truck. Camera: static wide shot, slow push-in. Light: soft morning sun, long shadows. Tone: quiet, hopeful. Sound: birds, distant traffic, a screen door closing.

The honesty rule

Here is the rule that separates a creator from a takedown: label every clip as AI-generated, in the video and in the caption, and never present it as gameplay. Take-Two's DMCA responses followed the clips that were passed off as the game, not the ones that were honest about how they were made. The same applies to thumbnails — a frame from your clip labelled as AI is fine; a frame presented as a screenshot from the game is not.

The same honesty applies to the audio. Reusing the trailers' score and narration is what made the viral clips feel real, and it is also what makes them the easiest to take down. Generate your own audio, or use licensed music, and your clips survive. One creator built a 45-second trailer in 30 minutes from generated images and Runway clips — 20 clips made, 9 usable — and the lesson from that experiment is the same: the tools are fast, the failures are cheap, and the label is what keeps the work yours.

FAQ

Which AI video model is best for GTA 6-style clips? Veo 3.1 and Kling are the two behind the viral clips. Veo likes flowing paragraphs with a sound line; Kling likes comma-separated descriptors plus a negative prompt. Runway works best with camera movement first.

Can I use the game's name in my prompt? Platforms often block real names, brands and game titles, so describe the look instead — neon coastal city, art-deco boardwalk, palm-lined highway — and let the style do the work.

Is it legal to post AI GTA 6 videos? Making a stylised clip is one thing; presenting it as gameplay is another. Label it as AI in the video and the caption, generate your own audio, and do not use Rockstar's trailer audio or art as if it were yours.

The bottom line: the prompt is the camera, the model is the crew, and the label is the licence. Write the shot, test it short, and tell your audience it is AI — the clips that did that are still up.
