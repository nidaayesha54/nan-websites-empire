Get the Full $260.72 KiwiSaver Top-Up

How the KiwiSaver government contribution works after the 2025 changes: 25c per $1, the $1,042.86 target, eligibility, and the 30 June deadline.

Home  /  Guides

Every year, the New Zealand government tops up your KiwiSaver — free money for doing nothing more than saving for yourself. But since 1 July 2025, the rules changed: the top-up was halved, and high earners were cut out entirely. Here's exactly how the current government contribution works, and the simple steps to make sure you get every dollar you're owed.

How the government contribution works now

The government matches your personal KiwiSaver contributions each year. Since 1 July 2025:

  • 25 cents for every $1 you contribute (down from 50 cents before Budget 2025's changes),
  • up to a maximum of $260.72 per year (previously $521.43),
  • which means contributing $1,042.86 a year unlocks the full amount.

The KiwiSaver year runs 1 July to 30 June — not the calendar year, not the tax year. Contributions are assessed within that window, and the government payment lands in your account around July/August after the year ends.

Are you eligible?

Four conditions:

  1. Aged 16 to 65. Since 1 July 2025, 16- and 17-year-olds became eligible too — previously only 18+ qualified.
  2. Mainly living in New Zealand. Time overseas can reduce your entitlement pro-rata.
  3. You actually contributed during the KiwiSaver year — the match is based on what you put in.
  4. Annual income of $180,000 or less. From 1 July 2025, members earning more than $180,000 a year no longer receive the government contribution at all.

The $1,042.86 target: how to hit it

Only your own contributions count toward the $1,042.86 — employer contributions and previous government contributions don't. But hitting the target is easier than it sounds:

  • Employed full-time? At the default contribution rate (3.5% from 1 April 2026, rising to 4% from 1 April 2028), even a minimum-wage salary puts in far more than $1,042.86 a year through payroll alone. Most employees max the credit without thinking about it.
  • Self-employed or not working? You need voluntary contributions. That's about $87 a month or $21 a week — set up an automatic payment and forget it.
  • Part-way through the year? Check your contributions to date with your provider, then make a lump-sum top-up for the shortfall. Do it several days before 30 June — contributions can take time to process, and missing the deadline by a day costs you the whole top-up.

Don't leave money on the table: the June check

Make it an annual ritual — sometime in May or early June:

  1. Log in to your KiwiSaver provider and check your personal contributions since 1 July.
  2. Subtract from $1,042.86. That's your shortfall.
  3. Pay the shortfall into your KiwiSaver well before 30 June.
  4. Confirm it landed — then wait for the government's $260.72 (or your pro-rata share) around July/August.

Example: you've contributed $800 of your own money by mid-June. Top up $242.86 before the deadline, and the government adds 25% × $1,042.86 = $260.72. That's a guaranteed 25% return no investment can promise — because it's not an investment return, it's a top-up.

What changed and what's coming

  • 1 July 2025: government match halved (50c → 25c per $1), cap halved ($521.43 → $260.72), $180,000 income exclusion introduced, 16–17-year-olds became eligible.
  • 1 April 2026: default employee and employer contribution rates rise from 3% to 3.5% — automatic for most employees. If you want to stay at 3%, you can apply to Inland Revenue for a temporary rate reduction (3–12 months, renewable).
  • 1 April 2026: employers must start contributing for 16–17-year-old employees who are contributing.
  • 1 April 2028: default rates rise again to 4% for both employees and employers.

The $1,000 kick-start for new members was removed back in 2015 and hasn't returned — the annual government contribution is now the main incentive in the scheme.

Common mistakes

  1. Counting employer contributions. They don't count toward the $1,042.86. Only your money does.
  2. Topping up on 30 June. Processing delays can push it into the next KiwiSaver year. Top up a week early.
  3. Assuming payroll covers it. If you took a savings suspension, changed jobs with a gap, or went self-employed mid-year, check — don't assume. Changing jobs? Run your NZ final pay to make sure every dollar owed follows you out the door.
  4. Ignoring it while young. 16–17-year-olds are now eligible; a teenager contributing $1,042.86 gets the same $260.72 top-up as anyone else.

Frequently asked questions

When do I receive the government contribution?

After the KiwiSaver year ends on 30 June, Inland Revenue calculates your entitlement and the payment is credited to your KiwiSaver account — typically around July/August.

I'm self-employed. How do I get it?

Make voluntary contributions totalling at least $1,042.86 between 1 July and 30 June — about $87/month. The government then adds up to $260.72.

What if I earn over $180,000?

Since 1 July 2025, you're not eligible for the government contribution. Your own and your employer's contributions continue as normal.

Does the contribution count if I live overseas part of the year?

You must be mainly living in New Zealand. Extended time overseas can reduce the entitlement — check with Inland Revenue if you've been away.

The compounding case: what $260.72 a year becomes

$260.72 sounds small — until you compound it. The government contribution isn't just a yearly bonus; it's money invested in your KiwiSaver fund, earning returns for decades.

Take a 25-year-old who contributes $1,042.86 a year and collects the full $260.72 top-up every year until 65 — 40 years of top-ups. Ignoring investment growth entirely, that's $10,428.80 of free government money. Now add even modest growth: at a 5% average annual return, those yearly $260.72 top-ups compound to roughly $31,000 by age 65. The contributions cost you nothing beyond the saving you were doing anyway.

This is why the June top-up ritual matters most when you're young: every year's top-up you miss is a year of compounding you never get back. A 25-year-old who skips five years of top-ups doesn't lose $1,303.60 — they lose the decades of growth on that $1,303.60. For the bigger picture, explore the retirement planning hub.

KiwiSaver for the self-employed: the full playbook

Self-employed New Zealanders get no employer contributions and no payroll deductions doing the work for them — but the government contribution is fully available. The playbook:

  1. Automate $87/month. A monthly automatic payment to your KiwiSaver provider hits $1,044 a year — just over the $1,042.86 target. Set it for the 1st of each month and never think about it again.
  2. Time it within the KiwiSaver year. Twelve payments of $87 from July to June = $1,044. If you start mid-year, do the maths and add a catch-up lump sum before 30 June.
  3. Don't confuse it with tax. Voluntary KiwiSaver contributions aren't tax-deductible — but the 25% government top-up is a far better deal than a deduction would be anyway.
  4. Review your fund type. Since you're managing this yourself, make sure your money is in a fund that matches your age and risk tolerance — many self-employed members sit in default conservative funds for years, leaving growth on the table.
  5. Combine with the rate rises. The default employee rate rises don't affect you directly, but they're a useful nudge: if employees are heading to 4%, consider whether your $87/month still reflects your retirement goals.

Parents, note the flow-on: 16–17-year-olds with part-time jobs are now eligible for the government contribution too. A teenager putting $21/week into KiwiSaver gets the same $260.72 top-up — possibly the highest-return financial habit a young person can build.

Rules reflect the KiwiSaver changes from Budget 2025 (effective 1 July 2025) and the scheduled rate rises. This is general information, not financial advice — check current rules on inlandrevenue.govt.nz.
