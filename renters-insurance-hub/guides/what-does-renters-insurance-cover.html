What Does Renters Insurance Cover?

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

A standard renters insurance policy (form HO-4) has three core coverages. Together they protect your stuff, your savings, and your roof over your head. Here's what each one actually does — and where the gaps are.

  • 2. Personal liability coverage
  • 3. Loss of use (additional living expenses)
  • What's NOT covered

1. Personal property coverage

This is the heart of the policy: it pays to repair or replace your belongings when they're damaged or stolen by a covered peril — typically fire, lightning, windstorm, hail, theft, vandalism, and water damage from burst pipes (the exact list is in your policy's "named perils").

What's included: furniture, clothing, electronics, appliances you own, kitchenware, sports gear, and even items stolen outside your home. Yes — if your laptop is stolen from your car or a café, your renters policy usually covers it (minus your deductible).

Coverage usually applies at 50–70% of the property limit worldwide for items away from home, and high-value categories have sub-limits: jewelry is often capped at $1,500 per theft loss, cash at $200, firearms at $2,500. Anything above those caps needs a scheduled endorsement (see below).

Most renters need $20,000–$40,000 of personal property coverage. Run our belongings calculator for a room-by-room number based on your actual stuff.

2. Personal liability coverage

If you accidentally injure someone or damage their property, liability coverage pays for their medical bills, repairs, and your legal defense. The standard policy starts at $100,000, with $300,000 and $500,000 upgrades that typically cost only $1–$3 more per month.

Real examples it covers:

  • Your dog bites a visitor — medical bills and a potential lawsuit.
  • You leave the bathtub running and flood the unit below — repairs to your neighbor's ceiling and floors.
  • Your kid breaks a neighbor's window with a baseball.
  • A guest slips on your wet kitchen floor and breaks an arm.

Liability also covers legal defense costs, which alone can run into the thousands even for a frivolous claim. If you have roommates, pets, or a higher-risk lifestyle, $300,000 is the smart default — our coverage quiz factors this in.

3. Loss of use (additional living expenses)

If a covered event — say a fire — makes your rental uninhabitable, loss-of-use coverage pays the extra cost of living elsewhere while it's repaired: hotel bills, restaurant meals above your normal grocery spending, storage fees, even pet boarding. It's usually capped at 20–30% of your personal property limit (so $6,000–$9,000 on a $30,000 policy).

This is the coverage renters forget about until they need it. After a fire, finding a few thousand dollars for a hotel while you're also replacing everything you own is exactly the double-hit this coverage prevents.

What's NOT covered

No policy covers everything. Standard renters insurance typically excludes:

  • Floods and earthquakes — these need separate policies (flood via the NFIP or private insurers; earthquake as an endorsement or standalone policy).
  • Your roommate's belongings — each person needs their own policy. One policy cannot cover two unrelated adults' property.
  • Wear and tear, pests, and mold from neglect — maintenance is your job, not the insurer's.
  • Business property beyond a small sub-limit — typically $2,500 at home, $500 away. Home-business gear needs a business endorsement or separate policy.
  • High-value items above sub-limits — jewelry, art, and collectibles over the per-category caps.
  • Intentional damage and illegal activity — obviously.

Also note: your landlord's insurance covers the building, not you. It pays for the structure and the landlord's liability — your belongings and your liability are entirely your responsibility.

Endorsements worth buying

  • Scheduled personal property (floater) — lists individual valuables (engagement ring, camera, instrument) at an agreed value with no deductible. Costs roughly $10–$15 per $1,000 of jewelry per year. Worth it for anything over $1,500.
  • Replacement cost coverage — pays what it costs to buy the item new, instead of its depreciated value. Adds just a few dollars a month and dramatically improves claim payouts.
  • Identity theft coverage — reimburses costs of restoring your identity ($25–$50/year).
  • Water backup endorsement — covers sump-pump or sewer backups, a common exclusion, often for under $50/year.

Replacement cost vs. actual cash value

This single choice can double your claim check. Actual cash value (ACV) pays what your 5-year-old couch is worth today (depreciated). Replacement cost value (RCV) pays what a comparable new couch costs. RCV costs only slightly more in premium and is almost always the better buy — ask for it by name when you get quotes.
