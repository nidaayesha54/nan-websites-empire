Do I Really Need Renters Insurance?

Updated October 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: most renters do — and it's the best value in personal insurance. But "most" isn't "all," so let's do the honest math instead of a sales pitch.

The honest math

The average policy costs about $180/year. Now add up what you own — most renters land between $20,000 and $40,000 once they count clothing, electronics, furniture, and kitchenware. (Don't guess: our belongings calculator walks you through it room by room.)

One break-in where a laptop ($1,200), a bike ($600), and some clothes ($800) walk out the door is a $2,600 loss — 14 years of premiums wiped out in a single claim. And that's before liability: a standard policy includes $100,000 of personal liability coverage. One dog bite or one flooded downstairs neighbor can generate a five-figure bill. Your $15/month also buys legal defense if someone sues you over an accident in your rental.

Put simply: you're paying ~$0.50 a day to protect tens of thousands in belongings plus six figures of liability. There is no cheaper financial safety net available to a renter.

Your landlord's policy doesn't cover you

This is the #1 misconception. Your landlord's policy covers the building — walls, roof, plumbing — and the landlord's own liability. It covers zero of your belongings and zero of your liability. If a fire starts in your kitchen and damages three units, the landlord's insurer will pay the landlord — and may come after you for the damage you caused. Your own liability coverage is what stands between you and that bill.

When your lease requires it

Many leases now require renters insurance — typically $100,000 of liability and sometimes a minimum property limit. This is legal in most states, and it's not negotiable after you sign. Landlords require it because it reduces disputes: if your washing machine floods the unit below, your policy pays instead of everyone lawyering up. If your lease requires it, you'll need to show a declarations page or certificate of insurance before (or shortly after) move-in.

When you can reasonably skip it

Honesty demands the exceptions. You might reasonably skip renters insurance if all of these are true:

  • You could replace everything you own from savings tomorrow without financial pain — true self-insurance.
  • You have no pets, no roommates, and a low-risk lifestyle (minimal liability exposure).
  • Your lease doesn't require it.
  • You have a robust emergency fund covering several months of hotel costs (replacing the loss-of-use coverage).

That's a narrow group — mostly minimalists with deep savings. For everyone else, the premium is rounding error compared to the risk.

The verdict

If your lease requires it, the decision is made. If it doesn't, ask yourself one question: could a $5,000 surprise loss wreck my year? If yes — and for most renters it would — a ~$15/month policy is one of the smartest purchases you can make.
