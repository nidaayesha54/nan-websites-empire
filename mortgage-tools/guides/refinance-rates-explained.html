Refinance Rates Explained: When Does It Make Sense?

Updated 2026-10-12

Refinance rates explained: when refinancing makes sense, the 1% rule myth, break-even math, and costs to watch in 2026.

What a refinance actually does

Refinancing replaces your current mortgage with a new one — ideally at a lower rate, a shorter term, or both. You pay closing costs of 2–5% for the privilege, so the deal only works if the savings repay those costs before you sell or refinance again.

Forget the "1% rule"

The old advice — refinance when rates drop 1% — ignores costs and timelines. The only number that matters is break-even: closing costs ÷ monthly savings. A 0.5% rate drop with low costs and a long stay can beat a 1% drop with $12,000 in fees. Run your exact numbers in our refinance calculator.

Three refinance flavors

  • Rate-and-term: lower rate or shorter term, same balance. The classic win.
  • Cash-out: borrow more than you owe, pocket the difference. Useful for high-interest debt payoff; risky as lifestyle funding.
  • No-closing-cost: costs rolled into a slightly higher rate. Break-even is instant, but lifetime cost is higher — compare both.

Costs to watch

Origination fees, discount points, appraisal, title insurance, and prepaid escrows. Get a Loan Estimate from at least three lenders — the same rate can hide very different fees.

The term trap

Refinancing a 25-years-remaining loan into a new 30-year loan almost always lowers the payment — and often raises total interest paid. If you refinance, consider keeping the term close to what remains, or commit to paying extra principal.

Bottom line

Refinance when break-even lands well before your horizon and lifetime savings are positive net of costs. Verify with the break-even calculator — not a rule of thumb.
