How to Become a CNA in 2026

The complete step-by-step checklist — requirements, training hours, exam, costs, and how to get trained for free.

By CNAToolsHub · Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Becoming a Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) is one of the fastest ways into American healthcare: most people go from deciding to certified in 4–12 weeks, often for little or no money. CNAs provide hands-on patient care — bathing, feeding, mobility help, vital signs — in nursing homes, hospitals, assisted living and home health. Demand is structurally strong: an aging population and chronic staffing shortages keep entry-level hiring brisk in nearly every state.

Step 1 — Check the requirements

Every state sets its own rules, but the baseline is consistent nationwide. You generally must be at least 18 years old (some states allow 16–17 with restrictions), hold a high school diploma or GED (a few states waive this for adult learners), pass a criminal background check, and show proof of immunizations and a recent TB test. Physical stamina matters: the job means long shifts on your feet, lifting, and repositioning patients. English literacy is required since you'll document care and communicate with nurses. Before spending a dollar, confirm your eligibility with your state's nurse aide registry — a quick web search for "[your state] nurse aide registry requirements" gets you the official list.

Step 2 — Complete a state-approved training program

Federal law requires at least 75 hours of training, including 16 hours of supervised clinical practice — and many states require more (120–180 hours is common). Programs come in three flavors:

  • Community colleges (4–12 weeks, $300–$1,200): the best value for most students, with financial aid available.
  • Private vocational schools (3–6 weeks, $600–$2,000): faster and more flexible scheduling, but pricier — compare pass rates before enrolling.
  • Employer-sponsored programs (4–8 weeks, often $0): nursing homes and hospital systems train you free in exchange for a 6–12 month work commitment. This is the smartest financial route if you qualify.

Hybrid programs — theory online, labs and clinicals in person — are now widespread. Note that fully online CNA certification does not exist: the clinical hours must be hands-on with real patients under supervision. Whatever you choose, verify the program is approved by your state's board; unapproved programs won't qualify you for the exam.

⏱️ Timeline reality check: full-time students typically finish training in 4–6 weeks; evening/weekend students in 8–12 weeks. Add 2–4 weeks for exam scheduling and registry processing.

Step 3 — Pass the CNA certification exam

After training, you take your state's competency exam — most states use Prometric, Pearson VUE, or Credentia (formerly NNAAP). The exam has two parts: a written/oral knowledge test (usually 60–70 multiple-choice questions on patient care, safety, infection control and residents' rights) and a skills demonstration where you perform 5 randomly selected nursing-assistant skills (hand hygiene, vital signs, transfers, bed baths, etc.) in front of an evaluator. You must pass both. Typical exam fees run $80–$150. If you fail, most states allow at least two retakes — and your training program's pass rate is the single best predictor of whether you'll need one, so ask for it before enrolling.

Step 4 — Get on your state's nurse aide registry

Passing the exam places you on the state nurse aide registry — the official list employers check before hiring. Keep your listing in good standing: most states require renewal every 2 years, usually with proof of recent paid nursing-assistant work (often 8+ hours) and sometimes continuing education. A lapse can force you to retest, so calendar your renewal date the day you're listed.

Step 5 — Understand the costs (and free options)

Total out-of-pocket cost typically lands between $300 and $2,000: tuition plus $80–$150 for the exam, $50–$150 for background checks and health screenings, and $50–$100 for scrubs and supplies. But a large share of CNAs pay far less:

  • Employer-paid training: nursing homes facing shortages routinely cover 100% of tuition — ask facilities near you before paying anything yourself.
  • Workforce grants (WIOA): your local American Job Center can fund training, exam fees and even transportation for eligible applicants.
  • Community programs: Goodwill, the American Red Cross and adult-education centers run low-cost or free cohorts in many regions.

Even at full price, the math is compelling: at a $18.50/hour starting wage, a $1,750 total investment breaks even in under 3 weeks of full-time work. Run your own numbers with our ROI calculator →

Step 6 — Land your first CNA job

Certified and listed? Start applying immediately — many facilities hire new graduates directly, especially amid staffing shortages. Nursing homes hire the most new CNAs and often offer the fastest start; hospitals pay more but may prefer experience; home health and assisted living offer calmer environments. Apply to 10–15 facilities, follow up in person with your registry number in hand, and ask about shift differentials — evenings, nights and weekends commonly add $1.50–$3.00/hour. Within your first year, consider stacking advantages: a specialty certification (dementia care, restorative aide), per-diem agency shifts, or travel contracts can lift pay substantially. And keep the long game in view: CNA is the classic bridge into LPN and RN programs, many of which give credit for CNA experience.

✅ Your 2026 CNA checklist: confirm state eligibility → pick a state-approved program → complete 75+ training hours (16+ clinical) → pass the written + skills exam → get on the registry → apply everywhere → renew every 2 years. Most people finish the whole journey in under 4 months.
