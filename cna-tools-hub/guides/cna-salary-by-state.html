CNA Salary by State 2026

Where you work as a CNA matters more than almost anything else — the gap between the best and worst paying states is nearly $10/hour. Here's the full picture.

By CNAToolsHub · Updated October 2026 · 8 min read · Based on BLS Occupational Employment data

The national median CNA wage sits around $19–$20/hour (roughly $40,000/year full-time), but that single number hides enormous variation. A CNA in Washington earns nearly 60% more per hour than a CNA in Mississippi doing identical work. Four forces drive the gap: state minimum-wage floors (a $17 minimum lifts all healthcare wages), union density (strong in California, New York, the Pacific Northwest), Medicaid reimbursement rates (nursing-home pay follows state Medicaid funding), and mandated staffing ratios (California-style ratio laws tighten the labor market).

What actually drives the gap

  • Minimum wage floors: Washington's ~$17 minimum wage pulls the entire healthcare pay scale up; states at the $7.25 federal floor anchor pay near it.
  • Unions: SEIU and state nursing unions in California, New York and the Pacific Northwest bargain CNA wages well above market.
  • Staffing-ratio laws: California's mandated nurse-to-patient ratios force facilities to compete for aides, bidding wages up.
  • Medicaid rates: nursing homes — the largest CNA employer — pay what state Medicaid reimburses. Generous states pay more.
  • Cost of living (partially): high-pay states are mostly expensive states, but the wage premium usually outruns the cost difference for renters — check both before moving.

The relocation math

Consider a CNA earning Mississippi's $15.81/hour average who moves to Washington at $25.11/hour. That's $9.30/hour more — about $19,300/year full-time, before overtime or shift differentials. Even after higher rent, many CNAs come out far ahead; travel CNA contracts (typically 13 weeks, often $25–$35/hour plus housing stipends) let you test a high-pay market before committing to a move. If relocation isn't possible, the same logic applies locally: hospitals pay ~8% more than nursing homes on average, and night/weekend differentials add $1.50–$3.00/hour — a Mississippi night-shift hospital CNA can close much of the gap without leaving the state.

💡 Fastest pay raises without moving: switch to a hospital setting, pick up night or weekend differentials, add a specialty certification (dementia, restorative care), or take per-diem agency shifts that pay premiums for hard-to-fill slots.

Beyond geography: experience, setting, shift

Within any state, three levers move your personal wage. Experience adds roughly 5% at 1–3 years, 10% at 3–5 years and 15% beyond 5 years. Setting matters: hospitals pay the most, followed by home health, then nursing homes, then assisted living. Shift differentials are the quickest lever — evening, night and weekend premiums stack directly onto your base rate. How overtime, per-diem and agency work change the picture

Base hourly rates are only the starting point. Most facilities pay time-and-a-half for overtime, and chronic understaffing means overtime is frequently available — a $19/hour CNA working 8 overtime hours a week effectively earns like a $21.50/hour worker. Per-diem agency shifts routinely pay $22–$30/hour for hard-to-fill slots, and picking up one agency shift a week alongside a full-time job is a common strategy to lift annual income by $8,000–$12,000. Travel CNA contracts (typically 13 weeks) pay $25–$35/hour plus tax-free housing stipends, and they double as a low-risk trial of a higher-pay state before you relocate permanently.

Where demand is hottest

Pay and demand usually move together. The strongest hiring markets for CNAs right now share three traits: fast-growing 65+ populations (Florida, Arizona, Texas, the Carolinas), tight labor markets with mandated staffing ratios (California, Oregon, Washington), and large hospital-system expansions (Colorado, Utah, Tennessee metros). Rural facilities in states like North Dakota, Montana and Wyoming often pay above their state average because they must attract aides to remote locations — worth considering if you want maximum pay per unit of living cost. Wherever you are, facilities with the worst staffing ratios are usually the ones offering the biggest sign-on bonuses; treat a four-figure bonus as a signal to ask hard questions about workload before you sign.
