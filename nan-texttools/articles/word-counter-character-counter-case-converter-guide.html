1. What Is a Word Counter?

A word counter is an online tool that tells you how long a piece of text is. You paste or type your text into a box, and it instantly reports the number of words, characters, sentences and paragraphs — plus derived numbers like estimated reading time. No software to install, no account to create.

The main use cases are all about limits: a college essay that must be 500–650 words, a blog post brief asking for 1,500 words, a social post that has to fit 280 characters, a gig description capped at 1,200 characters. Instead of guessing, you paste your draft and read the exact numbers.

How does word counting work?

There is no single universal rule for what a "word" is, but nearly every counter follows the same simple convention: text is split on whitespace, and each chunk is one word. Under this convention:

  • "don't" counts as one word (the apostrophe is inside the word).
  • "well-known" counts as one word (hyphenated compounds stay together).
  • Multiple spaces, tabs and line breaks do not create extra words.
  • Standalone punctuation — a lone comma or dash between spaces — is ignored.
  • Emoji are not words; they are counted as characters only.

Different apps can disagree by a word or two, especially with hyphenated terms or contractions. Treat every counter as a convention, not a law — and when a strict limit matters (a university submission, a platform cap), verify against the platform's own counter.

2. Word Counter: What Each Metric Means

A good word counter shows more than one number. Here is what each figure on the NAN Word Counter tells you:

Words

The core metric. Useful for essays, articles, assignments and any brief with a word target. Most briefs count words the way described above: split on whitespace, contractions and hyphenated words count once.

Characters (with spaces)

Every character, including spaces, punctuation and line breaks. This is the number that matters for platforms with hard character limits — X (Twitter), SMS, meta descriptions — because those platforms count spaces too.

Characters (no spaces)

The same count with all whitespace removed. Some academic, translation and typesetting briefs ask for characters without spaces, so the tool shows both figures side by side.

Sentences

Segments ending in a period, exclamation mark or question mark. A quick sanity check: an essay with 30 words and 1 sentence probably needs breaking up.

Paragraphs

Blocks of text separated by blank lines. Helpful for blog briefs ("minimum 6 paragraphs") and for spotting walls of text that need splitting for readability.

Reading time

Estimated at 200 words per minute, the average adult silent reading speed. A 600-word article shows "3m 0s". Writers use it to label content ("5 min read"); readers use it to decide whether they have time.

Quick rule of thumb: if your target is a platform limit, watch the characters with spaces figure. If your target is an assignment or brief, watch words.

5. Case Converter Online: Five Styles, One Click

A case converter changes the capitalization of text instantly. The classic use case: you typed (or pasted) a paragraph in the wrong case — all caps from caps-lock, or a heading in lowercase — and retyping would take minutes. Paste it into the NAN Case Converter, click a style, copy the result.

The five styles

  • UPPERCASE — EVERYTHING IN CAPITALS. Use sparingly: headings, warnings, short labels. Whole paragraphs in caps read as shouting.
  • lowercase — everything in small letters. Handy for normalizing tags, usernames and URLs before comparing or storing them.
  • Title Case — Every Word Capitalized. The standard for headings, article titles and book names.
  • Sentence case — Only the first word capitalized, like a normal sentence. Best for fixing ALL-CAPS paragraphs: convert to sentence case and you get readable text in one click.
  • aLtErNaTiNg — tHiS sTyLe. Purely for memes and playful social posts.

Real-world example: a job seeker pastes a cover letter drafted on a phone with caps-lock stuck on. One click on "Sentence case" fixes the entire letter. A student converts an essay title to Title Case for the heading. A marketer lowercases a list of hashtags to match brand style. Seconds, not minutes.

6. Remove Duplicate Lines

Paste any list — emails, keywords, URLs, names, one item per line — and the duplicate line remover keeps the first occurrence of each line and deletes the rest, then tells you exactly how many duplicates it removed.

  • Keyword lists: merge keyword research from several tools, dedupe, and you have a clean unique list.
  • Email lists: remove repeated addresses before a newsletter send (fewer duplicates = fewer spam complaints).
  • Data cleaning: tidy exported CSV columns or log files before analysis.

The case-sensitive toggle controls matching: with it off (default), "Apple" and "apple" count as duplicates; with it on, they are treated as different lines. Choose based on your data — emails should usually be case-insensitive, passwords or codes case-sensitive.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Is the word counter free?

Yes — 100% free, unlimited use, no signup. All four tools run entirely in your browser.

Does my text leave my browser?

No. Counting, case conversion, deduping and cleaning all happen locally on your device with plain JavaScript. Nothing is uploaded, logged or stored on any server — which is also why the tools keep working offline after the page loads.

Why does my count differ from Microsoft Word?

Word and Google Docs use their own tokenization rules (for example, how they treat hyphenated words, numbers and text boxes). Differences of a few words on long documents are normal. For graded work, always do a final check in the app you will submit from.

Do spaces count as characters?

Yes — on virtually every platform with a character limit (X, SMS, Instagram, LinkedIn), spaces count. That is why the "Characters" figure includes them, with a separate "no spaces" figure for briefs that exclude whitespace.

How accurate is the reading time?

It is an estimate: words ÷ 200 per minute, the widely used average for adult silent reading. Dense technical text reads slower; light fiction reads faster.

Can I use these tools on my phone?

Yes — the page is mobile-first. Paste from any app, tap a button, and copy the result back.

9. Tips for Writers and Students

  1. Set the target before you write. Know the word or character limit first, then draft toward it — editing down from 1,200 to 800 words is painful.
  2. Write 10% over, then trim. Drafts improve when cut. Use the live counter while trimming to land exactly on target.
  3. Check platform limits in the counter, not after posting. Draft your X post or meta description in the tool and edit until it fits — with a small safety margin.
  4. Fix case, don't retype. Pasted an ALL-CAPS paragraph? Sentence case fixes it in one click.
  5. Dedupe before you analyze. Keyword lists, email lists and survey responses all skew when duplicates sneak in.
  6. Clean pasted text before publishing. PDF and web copy-paste almost always brings invisible extra spaces — one click in the whitespace cleaner fixes it.
