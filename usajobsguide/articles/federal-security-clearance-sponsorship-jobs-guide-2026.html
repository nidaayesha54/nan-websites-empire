Federal Security Clearance Sponsorship Jobs 2026: How Civilians Get a Clearance Without Having One

You do not need a clearance to apply for most cleared federal jobs — the agency sponsors it. Learn which announcements sponsor clearances, what the investigation involves, and how to make yourself the candidate agencies want to sponsor.

Thousands of federal jobs — across the Department of Defense, intelligence community, Department of Energy, and Homeland Security — require a security clearance. Many applicants assume they must already hold one to be considered. For most postings, that is wrong: the announcement will say "must be able to obtain and maintain a security clearance", which means the hiring agency sponsors your investigation after selecting you.

Understanding that single sentence is the key to the whole cleared-career path. This guide explains which postings sponsor clearances, how the investigation works, what disqualifies people, and how to position yourself so an agency is happy to sponsor you.

"Able to Obtain" vs. "Active Clearance Required"

Read the announcement carefully. "Must be able to obtain and maintain a Secret/Top Secret clearance" means the agency will sponsor your investigation — you do not need one now. "Active Secret clearance required" means you must already hold one at application time, and your application will be screened out without it.

Most entry-level and mid-career federal civilian announcements use the first phrasing. Intelligence agencies and some special-access programs use the second. When in doubt, the "Requirements" section of the USAJOBS announcement is the authority — not a recruiter's summary.

The Clearance Levels and What They Mean

There are three main levels: Confidential, Secret, and Top Secret — with SCI and special-access programs layered on top for intelligence work. Higher levels mean deeper investigations, and only a sponsoring agency or cleared contractor can start the process, usually after a tentative job offer.

What Investigators Actually Look At

Adjudicators apply the whole-person concept: finances, foreign contacts and travel, drug and alcohol use, criminal history, and honesty.

How to Make Yourself Sponsor-Worthy

Agencies sponsor clearances for candidates they already want. Make yourself that candidate: target mission-critical series where agencies struggle to hire — cybersecurity (2210), intelligence (0132), engineering, and acquisition roles are perennial examples. Apply broadly.

Your Clearance-Sponsorship Action Plan

  1. Target sponsor-friendly announcements. On USAJOBS, look for "must be able to obtain and maintain" language in the Requirements section.
  2. Build the underlying qualifications. Agencies sponsor candidates they want — get the degree, certification, or experience the role demands first.
  3. Clean up your background now. Resolve debts, document foreign contacts, and keep everything disclosable before you ever file an SF-86.
  4. Be radically honest on the SF-86. Disclose everything the form asks for. Omissions are the top reason for denial.
  5. Stay put through adjudication. Do not change jobs, move abroad, or take on new debt mid-investigation without telling your security officer.
Verify before you apply: clearance requirements and sponsorship policies differ by agency and program. Always confirm the exact requirement on the official USAJOBS announcement — and never pay anyone who promises to "get you" a clearance.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a federal security clearance without a job offer?

No. Only a sponsoring federal agency or cleared contractor can initiate a clearance investigation, and they do so after making a tentative job offer. You cannot apply for a clearance on your own.

Do most federal jobs requiring a clearance sponsor it?

Yes — most announcements say 'must be able to obtain and maintain' a clearance, which means the agency sponsors your investigation. Only postings that say 'active clearance required' screen out applicants who don't already hold one.

What disqualifies you from getting a security clearance?

The most common reasons are unresolved financial problems and dishonesty or omissions on the SF-86. Adjudicators use the whole-person concept, weighing finances, foreign contacts, drug/alcohol use, criminal history, and honesty together.

How long does a security clearance investigation take?

It varies by level, agency, and backlog. Many agencies grant interim clearances so you can start work while the full investigation is completed.

What is the SF-86?

The SF-86 is the Questionnaire for National Security Positions, filed electronically through the e-QIP system. It collects your personal history — residences, employment, finances, foreign contacts, drug use, and more — for the background investigation.
