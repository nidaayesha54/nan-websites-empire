Time-in-Grade Rules: How Federal Promotions Really Work

Time-in-grade requires 52 weeks at the next lower grade before most GS promotions. Here's how the rule works, when it doesn't apply, and how to plan around it.

Ever wonder why a brilliant GS-12 cannot jump straight to a GS-14 opening? The answer is usually time-in-grade — the federal rule requiring 52 weeks of service at the next lower grade before promotion. It shapes every federal career ladder, and misunderstanding it is one of the most common reasons qualified applicants get rated ineligible. This guide explains how the rule works, its exceptions, and how to plan your promotions around it. Based on 5 CFR Part 300, Subpart F; always confirm the requirement on each individual announcement.

What Time-in-Grade Means in Practice

Time-in-grade is simple on paper: to be promoted from GS-13 to GS-14, you must have served 52 weeks at GS-13. A live IRS Criminal Investigation announcement states it exactly: "For the GS-14, you must have been at the GS-13 level for 52 weeks." The rule prevents grade-skipping and ensures promotions reflect sustained performance at each level. It applies to advancement within the General Schedule — the same logic governs a GS-7 targeting GS-9, or a GS-11 eyeing GS-12.

How It Shapes Your Career Ladder

Understanding time-in-grade lets you plan promotions a year ahead. If you entered as a GS-9 in January, you become eligible for GS-11 consideration the following January — which means you should start watching GS-11 announcements around month ten and have your resume ready. Many feds pair this with career-ladder positions (advertised as GS-9/11/12, for example), where promotion to the next rung is non-competitive once you meet time-in-grade and performance requirements. Targeting ladder positions is the fastest legal route upward.

When Time-in-Grade Does NOT Apply

The rule is not universal. Common situations where time-in-grade may not block you:

  • Direct-hire authority (DHA) appointments — agencies filling critical shortages often waive it.
  • Certain excepted-service and veterans' appointments (VRA, VEOA pathways) — check each announcement.
  • Movement between pay systems — moving from a non-GS system (e.g., demonstration projects, wage grade) follows different equivalency rules.
  • Reinstatement eligibles returning at a previously held grade.

Because exceptions vary, read the "Requirements" section of every announcement — it states explicitly whether time-in-grade applies.

Time-in-grade vs. probation: these are different rules. Probation (1 year) tests your fitness for federal service; time-in-grade (52 weeks per level) gates your promotions. A new hire serves both clocks simultaneously.

Planning Promotions Around Time-in-Grade

  1. Know your anniversary. Mark the date you hit 52 weeks at your current grade — that is your promotion-eligibility date.
  2. Target ladder positions. Announcements like "GS-11/12/13" offer non-competitive promotion once you qualify.
  3. Start early. Federal hiring takes months; begin applying 2–3 months before your 52 weeks are complete.
  4. Document qualifying duties. Your resume must show a full year of work at the next-lower-grade level of difficulty.
  5. Check each announcement. Confirm whether time-in-grade applies or an exception (DHA, veterans' authority) waives it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the federal time-in-grade requirement?

You must have served 52 weeks at the next lower grade (or equivalent level) before being promoted to a higher General Schedule grade, under 5 CFR Part 300, Subpart F.

Does time-in-grade apply to all federal promotions?

It applies to advancement within the General Schedule. Certain direct-hire, excepted-service, and veterans' appointments may be exempt — check each announcement's Requirements section.

Can I skip a grade in the federal government?

Generally no within the GS system. Time-in-grade requires 52 weeks at each successive level, so grade-skipping is blocked for most competitive promotions.

What is a career-ladder position?

A position advertised at multiple grades (e.g., GS-9/11/12) where you can be promoted non-competitively to the next rung once you meet time-in-grade and performance requirements.

Is time-in-grade the same as the probationary period?

No. Probation (1 year) evaluates your fitness for federal service; time-in-grade (52 weeks per level) gates promotions. New hires serve both clocks at once.
