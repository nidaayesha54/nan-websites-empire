Just Engaged?

You Said Yes. Now What?

The first 10 steps of wedding planning — in the exact order that saves you money and sanity.

Congratulations! 🎉 Now take a breath — and resist the urge to immediately start pinning centerpieces. The biggest mistake newly engaged couples make is starting with details before locking foundations. Details are fun; foundations are what keep you on budget and on schedule.

Here are your first 10 steps, in order:

Step 1: Celebrate (1–2 weeks, zero planning)

This is a real step. Tell your people, wear the ring, enjoy the glow. Couples who dive straight into spreadsheets burn out by month four. Give yourselves a short, deliberate honeymoon phase from planning itself.

Step 2: Have the money conversation

Uncomfortable, essential, and first for a reason: your budget determines everything else — venue tier, guest count, which vendors are realistic. Decide the total number, who contributes, and whether anyone's contribution comes with strings attached (guest-list demands are the classic one). Write it down.

Step 3: Draft the guest list

Before venues, before anything bookable: a realistic headcount. Catering, rentals, and venue capacity all scale with this number. Start generous, then trim with your budget in hand. Pro tip: the "would we take them to dinner?" test resolves most borderline names.

Step 4: Choose your wedding party

Pick people, not roles — choose your closest humans first, then assign titles. And ask them personally; a heartfelt ask sets the tone for the whole experience.

Step 5: Pick a season and 2–3 target dates

You don't need the exact date yet. A season plus backup dates gives venues flexibility to offer you their best availability (and sometimes better pricing for off-peak months).

Step 6: Research and tour venues

With budget + headcount + dates in hand, you're finally ready to shop. Tour your top three and ask the unsexy questions: what's included, noise curfews, vendor restrictions, rain plan, payment schedule, cancellation terms. The answers matter more than the chandeliers.

Step 7: Decide on planning help

Full-service planner, month-of coordinator, or DIY? Be honest about your time, stress tolerance, and guest count. If you hire one thing, make it a day-of coordinator — it's the highest ROI purchase in wedding planning, full stop.

Step 8: Book the venue

This is your first big commitment and it unlocks everything downstream: now you have a date, a capacity, and a canvas. Read the contract twice. Then celebrate — the hardest decision is behind you.

Step 9: Book photographer (and videographer)

Top photographers book 9–12 months out, so this comes right after the venue. Look at full galleries, not Instagram highlights — you need to see how they handle dark reception halls and chaotic family formals.

Step 10: Build your master timeline

What NOT to do yet

  • Don't buy the dress before the venue (formality should match the setting).
  • Don't order invitations before the guest list is final.
  • Don't agonize over colors, favors, or napkin folds — details come in months 6–3.
  • Don't announce a date publicly until the venue contract is signed.

How to share the news (without the drama)

Before you post anything: tell your inner circle personally — parents, siblings, best friends. Nobody wants to learn about an engagement from Instagram. After that, post freely. Then set one boundary early: "We're so excited and we'll share details as we have them!" is a complete sentence that deflects 90% of premature opinions about dates, venues, and guest lists.

Aligning expectations with family

The money conversation (Step 2) often surfaces a second conversation: expectations. If anyone contributing money expects decision power, clarify it now — before deposits are paid. The cleanest framework: whoever pays for an item gets the final say on that item. Write down the big three agreements early: total budget, approximate guest count, and general vibe (formal vs. casual, local vs. destination). Most family wedding conflicts trace back to unspoken assumptions about these three.

Choose your planning style

There are three legitimate ways to plan a wedding — pick the one that matches your life, not your Pinterest board:

  • The spreadsheet couple: You love systems. Build a master tracker (budget, vendors, deadlines) in week one and update it weekly. You'll thrive.
  • The delegator: Hire a planner and make decisions in batches. Your job is to answer questions quickly, not to manage logistics. This is the lowest-stress path if budget allows.
  • The minimalist: Small guest list, one venue that does everything (ceremony + reception + catering in-house), minimal vendors. Fewer moving parts = fewer things to manage. Some of the happiest weddings are the simplest.

There's no prize for the most complicated wedding. There is a prize — a calm engagement and a joyful day — for the approach that fits your actual life.

The one purchase to make in month one

If you buy nothing else early, buy this: a wedding email address (free) that you and your partner both monitor. Every vendor inquiry, contract, and invoice flows through it. When you're searching for "that email from the florist" six months later, you'll be glad every thread lives in one place. Second purchase, if budget allows: book a venue tour weekend now — seeing spaces in person kills weeks of online dithering.

Insure the ring (yes, really, this week)

Engagement rings get lost, chipped, and stolen every single day. Adding a jewelry rider to your renter's or homeowner's insurance — or a standalone policy — typically costs 1–2% of the ring's value per year and takes one phone call. Do it this week while the excitement is fresh, because nobody remembers to do it in month eight. Photograph the ring, save the appraisal paperwork digitally, and you're covered.

Start a "wedding fund" account

Open a separate savings account (free at most banks) just for wedding money. Every contribution — yours, family, anyone's — lands there, and every vendor payment leaves from there. This does two magical things: it makes your real budget visible at a glance, and it ends the "wait, how much have we spent?" arguments before they start. Couples who co-mingle wedding money with daily spending consistently overshoot their budget without noticing.

Agree on your non-negotiables (each of you picks three)

Here's an exercise that prevents 80% of wedding arguments: separately, each partner writes down their three non-negotiables — the things that would genuinely diminish the day if missing. Then compare lists. You'll discover that most of what you assumed you'd fight about, neither of you actually cares about — and the few items that matter get protected in the budget from day one. Common non-negotiables: great food, a specific person officiating, live music, photography quality, having everyone together. Everything else is negotiable, and knowing that is freeing.

Set a "decision rhythm"

Wedding planning dies in two ways: deciding everything at once (burnout) or deciding nothing for months (panic). The fix is a rhythm — one planning session per week, 60–90 minutes, phones away. In each session: review what's due this month, make the pending decisions, and assign next actions. Between sessions: no wedding talk allowed (okay, a little). Couples with a rhythm finish planning feeling closer; couples without one finish it feeling like project managers who happen to be marrying each other.

Protect the relationship, not just the timeline

Here's what no checklist tells you: the engagement period is a stress test, and the couples who pass it do one thing differently — they treat the wedding as a project and the relationship as the priority. That means: never criticize your partner's taste in front of vendors, never make a big decision when you're hungry or exhausted, and keep at least one date night a month completely wedding-talk-free. The wedding is one day. The marriage is the point. Couples who remember that in month two are still laughing about the planning in year ten.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do first after getting engaged? +
Celebrate first — seriously, give it a week. Then: set your budget, draft your guest list, and start venue research. Those three unlock every other decision.
How long should you wait to start planning after engagement? +
Enjoy 1–2 weeks of pure celebration, then start the money conversation. Popular venues book 9–12 months out, so every month of delay shrinks your options — but burnout from planning too fast is real too.
Should I hire a wedding planner? +
If your budget allows, at minimum hire a month-of/day-of coordinator — it's the single best stress-reduction purchase most couples make. Full planners pay for themselves if you're planning a large or destination wedding.
