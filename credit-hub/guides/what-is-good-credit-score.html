What Is a Good Credit Score?

FICO ranges from 300–850. Here's what each tier means — and what it unlocks.

The FICO score ranges

RangeRatingWhat it means
800–850ExceptionalBest rates on everything; approvals nearly automatic.
740–799Very GoodTop-tier rates from most lenders; the practical "excellent" threshold.
670–739GoodApproved for most products at decent rates. The median US score sits here.
580–669FairSubprime territory: approvals possible but rates are higher; FHA mortgages allowed at 580+.
300–579PoorDifficult approvals; secured cards and credit-builder products are the usual path up.

What each tier unlocks

Credit cards

Premium rewards cards generally want 670+, with the best (high limits, big bonuses) clustering at 740+. Below 670, look at cards marketed for "fair credit" or secured cards that graduate.

Auto loans

Super-prime (780+) borrowers get the lowest APRs; the rate gap between prime and subprime auto loans can exceed 8 percentage points — thousands of dollars over a loan's life.

Mortgages

Conventional loans typically want 620+; the best rates cluster at 740–760+. FHA loans go down to 580 (500 with 10% down). Even a 0.5% rate difference on a 30-year mortgage is tens of thousands in interest.

What actually builds the number

Five factors, in order of weight: payment history (~35%), amounts owed (~30%), length of history (~15%), new credit (~10%), credit mix (~10%). Two habits cover most of it: pay on time, every time and keep balances low relative to limits.

Common myths

  • "Checking my score hurts it." No — checking your own score is a soft inquiry and never affects it.
  • "Carrying a balance helps." No — paying in full is always better; carrying a balance just costs interest.
  • "Closing old cards helps." Usually the opposite — it can raise utilization and shorten history.
  • "Income affects my score." It doesn't. Scores measure how you handle credit, not how much you earn.
