Deductible vs Premium, Explained Simply

2026-10-12 · 6 min read

Every health plan forces the same tradeoff: low premium + high deductible or high premium + low deductible. Here's how to do the math instead of guessing.

The tradeoff in one paragraph

Insurers let you choose where the risk sits. A $250/month plan with a $6,000 deductible bets you're healthy — you save $2,400/year in premiums vs a $450/month plan, but owe the first $6,000 of care yourself. The $450/month plan with a $2,000 deductible costs more guaranteed, but caps a bad year much lower.

Do the math for YOUR situation

Compare total yearly cost = premiums + out-of-pocket at the usage level you expect. Try it live with our interactive deductible explainer and plan comparator. The pattern that emerges:

  • Healthy year ($1,500 in bills): the cheap-premium plan almost always wins — you pocket the premium savings and barely touch the deductible.
  • Medium year ($8,000): often close; the balanced plan frequently wins.
  • Bad year ($30,000+): the low-deductible/low-OOP-max plan usually wins — the OOP max is what saves you, not the deductible.

Who should pick a high deductible?

  • You're healthy, rarely see doctors, and have the deductible saved in an emergency fund or HSA.
  • You want HSA access (only qualifying high-deductible plans allow it) — the tax savings can outweigh the risk.
  • You're buying mainly catastrophe protection and can self-fund routine care.

Who should pick a low deductible?

  • You have a chronic condition, take expensive prescriptions, or have planned surgery/childbirth this year.
  • You couldn't comfortably pay a $6,000 surprise bill.
  • Someone in the family is pregnant or manages an ongoing condition.

Note: The out-of-pocket max matters more than the deductible in bad years. A $6,000 deductible with a $7,000 OOP max can beat a $2,000 deductible with a $9,000 OOP max when bills get big.
