10 Best Study Techniques Backed by Research (2026 Guide)

Study Daily Reports · Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

Most students study harder than they need to — because they use the wrong techniques. In 2013, a landmark review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest evaluated ten popular learning techniques and rated most of them "low utility." A decade of follow-up research has only sharpened the picture. This guide ranks the techniques with the strongest evidence, so you can study less and remember more.

1. Active Recall (Practice Testing) — Highest Utility

The single most powerful technique: retrieving information from memory instead of re-reading it. Close the book, write down everything you remember, then check what you missed. Every retrieval attempt strengthens the memory trace — this is called the testing effect, replicated in hundreds of studies.

How to do it: after each chapter, make your own quiz questions. Use flashcards (physical or apps like Anki). Explain concepts aloud as if teaching someone.

2. Spaced Repetition — Highest Utility

Spreading study sessions over days beats cramming the same total time into one session. UCLA research found spaced learners retained up to twice as much long-term. Review new material after 1 day, then 3 days, then a week.

How to do it: schedule review sessions in advance. Spaced-repetition apps automate the intervals for you.

3. Interleaving — Moderate to High Utility

Mixing different topics or problem types in one session (e.g., alternating algebra, geometry and statistics) outperforms "blocking" one topic at a time. It feels harder — that's the point. Interleaving trains your brain to choose the right strategy, which is exactly what exams demand.

4. Elaborative Interrogation — Moderate Utility

Ask "why?" and "how?" about every fact. Why does this formula work? How does this concept connect to last week's material? Generating explanations forces deep processing and costs almost no extra time.

5. The Feynman Technique — High Practical Value

Named after physicist Richard Feynman: teach the concept in simple language. If you can't explain it to a 12-year-old, you don't understand it yet. The gaps you hit while explaining are precisely what to study next.

6. Concrete Examples — Moderate Utility

Abstract ideas stick when anchored to examples. For every definition, invent or find two concrete examples. "Opportunity cost" becomes "choosing to study means giving up a shift at work."

7. Dual Coding — Moderate Utility

Combine words with visuals: diagrams, timelines, mind maps. Your brain stores verbal and visual information in separate channels, so using both creates two retrieval paths to the same memory.

8. The Pomodoro Technique — High Practical Value

25 minutes of focused work, 5-minute break, repeat. Bounded sprints reduce procrastination and decision fatigue. Use our free Pomodoro study timer to run structured sessions with automatic long breaks.

9. Past Papers Under Timed Conditions — High Exam Value

Nothing predicts exam performance like practicing under exam conditions. Timed past papers build format familiarity, pacing skills and retrieval under pressure — three things content review alone can't teach.

10. Sleep and Exercise — The Multipliers

Not a "technique," but the highest-leverage factor of all: memory consolidation happens during sleep. An extra hour of sleep consistently beats an extra hour of midnight review. Regular aerobic exercise improves attention and long-term memory formation.

The bottom line

You don't need more hours — you need better methods. Start with active recall + spaced repetition; they deliver the biggest gains for the least effort. Add interleaving and the Feynman technique as they become habit, and protect your sleep like it's part of the syllabus. Because it is.

FAQ

What is the most effective study technique?
Active recall and spaced repetition have the strongest research support. Testing yourself on material — rather than re-reading it — consistently produces the largest gains in long-term retention.
Does highlighting help you study?
A major 2013 review rated highlighting as low utility. It feels productive but creates little lasting memory compared to retrieval practice.
How long should a study session be?
25–50 minute focused sessions with short breaks (the Pomodoro method) work best for most students. Try our free study timer to run structured sessions.
