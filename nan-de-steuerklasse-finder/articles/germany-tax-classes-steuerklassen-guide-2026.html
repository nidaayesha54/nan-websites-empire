German Tax Classes (Steuerklassen) 2026: Which One Are You In?

Germany assigns every employee a Steuerklasse (tax class) that decides how much income tax is withheld from each payslip. Pick wrong and your monthly pay looks mysteriously small. Here's what all six classes mean in 2026, in plain English, and how to choose.

What a Steuerklasse actually does

Here's the single most misunderstood fact: your tax class does NOT change how much tax you owe for the year. Germany's income tax rates are the same for everyone. The Steuerklasse only controls how much your employer withholds each month as an advance payment. Your annual tax return (Einkommensteuererklärung) reconciles everything — overpaid withholding comes back as a refund, underpaid becomes a bill.

So choosing a tax class is really a cash-flow decision, not a tax-saving strategy. For single people there's nothing to decide (you're Class I). For married couples, the choice genuinely moves hundreds of euros a month between partners.

The six classes, explained

Class ISingle, divorced, widowed, or separated. The default for most single expats. Standard withholding.
Class IISingle parents with a child in the household. Adds the single-parent relief (Entlastungsbetrag, at least €4,260/year) → slightly lower withholding.
Class IIIMarried: the higher-earning spouse. Gets roughly double allowances → lowest withholding, highest monthly net.
Class IVMarried: both partners earn similarly. Each gets the standard allowance (like Class I each). The default when you marry.
Class VMarried: the lower-earning spouse, paired with a Class III partner. No personal allowance → highest withholding of the married options.
Class VIYour second or further job. Maximum withholding, no allowances — because your main job already used them.

There's also Class IV with factor (Faktorverfahren): married couples can ask the Finanzamt to split withholding proportionally to each partner's income — fairer monthly pay for both, and it avoids the Class V shock described below. Note: from 2030, III/V is scheduled to be phased out in favour of the factor method, but for 2026 both systems run normally.

Married couples: III/V vs IV/IV

When you marry in Germany, you're both put in Class IV by default. You can then choose:

  • III/V — best when one partner earns much morehousehold total is optimized, but the Class V partner's payslip can be a shock. Mandatory annual tax return for III/V couples.
  • IV/IV — best when earnings are similar. Both partners are withheld roughly like single people. Feels fair month to month; the annual return may produce a small balancing payment or refund either way.
  • IV/IV with factor — the balanced option. Withholding split by actual income ratio. Often the fairest monthly outcome for unequal earners who dislike the III/V extremes.

Practical tip: if the lower-earning spouse in a III/V setup gets a significant raise, re-run the numbers — IV/IV (with factor) often becomes the better monthly deal.

Key 2026 parameters behind these: the basic tax-free allowance (Grundfreibetrag) is €12,348 per person, the top tax rate kicks in at €69,879, and the standard employee expense allowance (Arbeitnehmer-Pauschbetrag) is €1,230/year applied automatically.

How to change your tax class

  1. Married couples can request a change (Steuerklassenwechsel) at their local Finanzamt — usually via the ELSTER online portal or a paper form.
  2. You're generally allowed one change per calendar year (exceptions: divorce, death of a spouse, unemployment).
  3. The change takes effect from the following month.
  4. Changing class never changes your final annual tax — it only re-times the withholding. Don't fear switching if your situation changes.

Myths expats believe

"Class III saves tax." No — it shifts withholding timing. The annual bill is identical; the return settles the difference.

"I should stay in Class VI for my minijob." Class VI isn't a choice — it applies automatically to second employments. (Minijobs up to the monthly threshold are typically taxed flat-rate by the employer instead.)

"My employer picks my class." Your class comes from your registration data (marital status via the Bürgeramt). Only you (and your spouse) can request a change at the Finanzamt.

"Class II is for all single parents automatically." You must actually qualify — child living in your household and eligible for the relief — and claim it; check your Lohnsteuerabzugsmerkmale on ELSTER.

Checking your class on ELSTER

Your current tax class lives in your Lohnsteuerabzugsmerkmale (ELStAM) — the electronic wage-tax profile your employer pulls automatically. You can view it anytime in the ELSTER online portal (free registration with ID verification). Check it after life events: marriage, divorce, a child's birth, or starting a second job should all reflect there. If ELSTER shows the wrong class, your employer withholds wrong all year — the annual return fixes the money, but not the monthly cash-flow pain.

Church tax: the silent payslip line

If you're registered as Catholic or Protestant (which happens automatically if you declare a religion at the Bürgeramt), you'll pay Kirchensteuer — 8% of your income tax in Bavaria/Baden-Württemberg, 9% everywhere else — withheld alongside Lohnsteuer. On a €60,000 salary in Class I, that's roughly €100+/month many expats never expected. Declaring "no religion" (konfessionslos) at registration avoids it entirely; leaving the church later (Kirchenaustritt, ~€30–60 fee at the Amtsgericht) stops it going forward. Your Steuerklasse doesn't change the church-tax rate, but because church tax is a percentage of your withheld income tax, class-driven withholding swings move this line too.

Children: Kindergeld vs Kinderfreibetrag

Parents get €259/month per child in Kindergeld (2026) paid directly — plus the tax system automatically runs a Günstigerprüfung (favourability test): if the Kinderfreibetrag (child tax allowance) saves you more than the Kindergeld paid out, the Finanzamt applies the allowance instead. You don't choose; the better deal is applied automatically on your tax return. This interacts with tax class choice for couples: run the III/V vs IV/IV comparison with children in the model, because the allowance shifts the withholding math.

Expats: your first year in Germany

Arrived mid-year? You'll likely be in Class I (single) or IV/IV (married) from your Anmeldung date. Two things newcomers miss: (1) you're generally tax-resident from day one if Germany is your habitual home — worldwide income becomes declarable; (2) filing a tax return for your arrival year is strongly recommended even when not mandatory, because split-year withholding almost always overpays. That first return is also where you claim the big newcomer deductions: moving costs, double household (doppelte Haushaltsführung), and German courses if job-related.
