How to Brainstorm Logo Ideas: The 7-Step Process Designers Actually Use

Updated for 2026 · 10-minute read

Staring at a blank page is the worst way to design a logo. Professional brand designers don't wait for inspiration — they run a repeatable process that manufactures ideas, then filters ruthlessly. Here's the exact process, adapted for founders and DIYers.

Step 1: Write the one-sentence brief (15 min)

Before drawing anything, finish this sentence: "We're a [what] for [who] that feels [3 adjectives], unlike [competitor] which feels [their vibe]."

Example: "We're a sourdough bakery for neighborhood families that feels warm, honest, and a little nostalgic — unlike the sterile chain bakery that feels cold and corporate." Those three adjectives (warm, honest, nostalgic) become your decision filter for everything after.

Step 2: Word-mapping (20 min)

Take your three adjectives and free-associate. Write each in the center of a page and branch outward:

  • Warm → hearth → flame → ember → sunrise → blanket → hug
  • Honest → handshake → open book → clear glass → handshake → scale
  • Nostalgic → grandmother → vinyl → postcard → badge → script lettering

Circle the most visual words. These are your raw concept seeds — "hearth," "sunrise," "badge," "postcard" can all become marks.

Step 3: The competitor audit (30 min)

Collect 8–10 competitor logos. Lay them out and note the patterns: everyone blue? Everyone using a house icon? Everyone in a circle badge? Your job is to break at least one dominant pattern while staying credible in the category. Differentiation is a feature, not a risk.

Step 4: The 30-sketch sprint (30 min)

Set a timer. Draw 30 tiny thumbnails (2 minutes each max) — pen on paper, no erasing, no judging. Combine your word-map seeds with different logo types:

  • Wordmark: the name itself, customized (FedEx's hidden arrow lives here).
  • Lettermark/monogram: initials as the mark (IBM, Chanel).
  • Pictorial mark: a literal or abstract image (Apple's apple, Twitter's bird).
  • Abstract mark: pure geometry with meaning (Nike swoosh, Pepsi globe).
  • Emblem/badge: text inside a shape (Starbucks, Harley-Davidson).
  • Mascot: a character (KFC's Colonel, Mailchimp's Freddie).

Quantity first — the research is clear that idea #25 is usually better than idea #3. Need a jumpstart? Run our logo idea generator and sketch the concepts it gives you.

Step 5: The brutal filter (20 min)

Put all 30 sketches on a wall. Remove any that fail these tests:

  1. Memorable? Could someone redraw it from memory after 10 seconds?
  2. Simple? Would it survive as a 16-pixel favicon?
  3. Relevant? Does it match your three adjectives from Step 1?
  4. Different? Does it break at least one competitor pattern?
  5. Timeless? Will it survive 10 years without a redesign?

You should be left with 3–5 survivors. If you have more, you're being too kind — cut harder.

Step 6: Color and type exploration (45 min)

Only now — after the mark is chosen — do you explore color. Recreate your top 3 marks in black first, then apply palettes using color psychology: match your three adjectives to color families, then test each palette at small size and in grayscale. For typography, pair one distinctive display face (for the wordmark) with nothing else — a logo needs one voice, not a choir.

Step 7: Real-world mockups (30 min)

A logo doesn't live on a white artboard — it lives on storefronts, phone screens, and packaging. Mock up your finalist on:

  • A phone home screen (app icon size)
  • A business card and letterhead
  • Your actual storefront or website header
  • One social avatar (circle-cropped — the cruelest test)

The winner is the one that looks intentional everywhere, not just on the artboard.

Common brainstorming traps

  • Starting in software. Pixels make you polish too early. Paper keeps you in idea mode.
  • Asking "do you like it?" Ask instead: "what three words does this communicate?" — then compare with your adjectives.
  • Design by committee. Collect feedback, but one person decides. Logos designed by vote look like compromises.
  • Copying a trend. Gradients-everywhere (2018) and brutalist type (2022) both dated fast. Steal principles, not styles.
