LASIK vs Glasses: Lifetime Cost Comparison

Updated October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

LASIK surgery feels expensive — $4,000 to $5,000 for both eyes is real money. But glasses and contact lenses quietly drain your wallet year after year. Over a lifetime, which actually costs more? Let's run the 20-year math.

What LASIK Costs in 2026

The average LASIK price in the USA is $2,000–$2,500 per eye, or roughly $4,000–$5,000 for both eyes. Prices vary by technology (bladeless/custom LASIK costs more), surgeon reputation, and city. Beware of "$299 per eye" ads — those quotes apply to tiny prescriptions and balloon with add-ons. Most reputable surgeons charge one all-inclusive fee covering the procedure and follow-up visits.

Insurance note: Vision insurance almost never covers LASIK, but many plans give you a 15–25% discount at participating surgeons — worth $600–$1,250. Always ask. FSA/HSA dollars can also pay for LASIK pre-tax.

The 20-Year Showdown

Take a realistic middle case: $500/year on glasses or contacts plus exams. Over 20 years that's $10,000. LASIK at $4,500 breaks even in year 9 — and saves you roughly $5,500 over the remaining years. For a heavy contact lens user spending $700/year, break-even comes in under 7 years, with 20-year savings above $9,000.

Even a light glasses user at $300/year breaks even around year 15 and comes out ahead by year 20. The math favors LASIK for almost everyone under 45 who is a candidate — try your own numbers in our LASIK Break-Even Calculator.

Costs LASIK Doesn't Eliminate

Be fair to the glasses side — LASIK isn't a magic zero:

  • Reading glasses after 40: Presbyopia affects everyone; most LASIK patients eventually need cheap readers ($10–$30).
  • Enhancement procedures: 5–10% of patients need a touch-up within a decade, sometimes at reduced cost.
  • Ongoing eye health exams: You still need regular checkups for glaucoma, cataracts, and retinal health.
  • Dry eye treatment: A minority of patients need ongoing dry-eye care for months or years after surgery.

When LASIK Is NOT the Cheaper Choice

  • You're over 55: Cataract surgery (often covered by health insurance) will replace your lens anyway — LASIK's payback window is short.
  • You spend very little now: If $30 online glasses and a $100 exam are your whole budget, LASIK never breaks even.
  • You're not a candidate: Thin corneas, severe dry eye, unstable prescriptions, or certain conditions rule it out — only an eye surgeon can tell you.
  • Your prescription is still changing: Common in your early 20s; surgery on a moving target means paying twice.

How to Pay Less for LASIK

  1. Use your vision plan's LASIK discount — 15–25% off at network surgeons.
  2. Pay with FSA/HSA — pre-tax dollars effectively cut 20–30% off the price.
  3. Get 3 quotes — prices for the same technology vary by thousands between clinics in one city.
  4. Ask about financing — 0% plans for 12–24 months are common; compare the total, not the monthly payment.
  5. Time it right — clinics run promotions in slow months (often January and summer).

LASIK vs Glasses FAQs

How long does LASIK last?

For most patients, the vision correction is permanent. However, natural age-related changes (like presbyopia after 40 and cataracts after 60) still occur and are unrelated to LASIK.

Does LASIK hurt?

Numbing drops mean most patients feel pressure but no pain during the 10–15 minute procedure. Mild dryness and light sensitivity for a few days afterward are common.

Can I finance LASIK?

Yes — most clinics offer 0% financing for 12–24 months, and FSA/HSA funds can cover the full cost pre-tax. Combined with a vision plan's 15–25% LASIK discount, financing makes the one-time cost manageable.

What if my prescription changes after LASIK?

About 5–10% of patients need an enhancement within 10 years. Many surgeons include one enhancement free or at reduced cost — ask before you book.

The Bottom Line

For a typical glasses or contacts wearer under 45, LASIK ($4,000–$5,000 one-time) breaks even in 7–12 years and saves $5,000–$9,000 over 20 years — while eliminating the daily hassle of lenses. It's not right for everyone, but purely on cost, it wins for most candidates.
