Is Vision Insurance Worth It?

Updated October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

"Is vision insurance worth it?" is one of the most-searched insurance questions in America — and the answer is genuinely different for different people. Unlike health insurance, vision insurance is really a prepaid discount plan: you pay a small monthly premium in exchange for fixed low prices on exams, glasses, and contacts. Whether that trade works for you comes down to simple math. Let's do it.

The Quick Test

Vision insurance is probably worth it if any of these describe you:

  • You wear prescription glasses and buy new frames at least every 1–2 years
  • You wear contact lenses daily or weekly
  • You have a condition needing frequent exams (glaucoma, diabetes, strong prescriptions)
  • Your employer subsidizes the premium (drops your cost to $5–$13/month)

It's probably not worth it if you get one routine exam a year and buy nothing else — paying $150–$200 out of pocket can beat a $180–$240 premium.

Scenario 1: The Glasses Wearer

Sarah buys new glasses every year. Without insurance: exam $175 + frames and lenses $300 = $475/year. With a $15/month plan ($180/year): $10 exam copay + $25 lens copay, with a $150 frames allowance covering most of a $200 frame pair — roughly $285/year total. Savings: ~$190/year. Clear win.

Scenario 2: The Contact Lens User

Mike wears daily disposable contacts costing $400/year, plus a $175 exam = $575/year. With the same plan: $180 premium + $10 copay + ($400 − $150 contacts allowance) = $440/year. Savings: ~$135/year. Still worth it — and it gets better with pricier specialty lenses.

Scenario 3: Healthy Eyes, Annual Exam Only

Priya has 20/20 vision and gets one exam a year out of caution. Without insurance: $175. With insurance: $180 premium + $10 copay = $190. She actually loses $15. For her, self-paying (or a vision discount program) is smarter.

Bottom line of the three scenarios: corrective-lens wearers usually save $100–$200/year; exam-only users often break even or lose a little. Plug in your own numbers — it takes 60 seconds.

Five Things People Get Wrong About Vision Insurance

  1. "It works like health insurance." It doesn't — there are no deductibles or out-of-pocket maximums. It's a fixed-benefit discount arrangement.
  2. "The cheapest premium is the best deal." A $13/month plan with a $130 allowance can cost you more per year than a $17 plan with a $200 allowance. Always compare true yearly cost — our Plan Comparator does this for you.
  3. "It covers eye surgery." Medical eye issues (cataracts, injuries) are covered by health insurance. Vision plans cover exams, glasses, contacts — and usually only discount LASIK.
  4. "I can use any doctor." Out-of-network benefits are dramatically worse. A plan is only worth it if you like its in-network providers.
  5. "I don't need it if I have perfect vision." Mostly true for cost — but annual exams also screen for glaucoma, cataracts, and diabetic eye disease. If that's your motivation, a basic exam every year out of pocket is fine.

Employer Plan vs. Buying Your Own

If your employer offers vision insurance, take it — the subsidy typically makes it 40–60% cheaper than an equivalent individual plan, and the math almost always works in your favor. Buying on your own is still worthwhile for lens wearers, but run the numbers first: at $20/month ($240/year), a light glasses user might only break even.

Alternatives If Insurance Isn't Worth It for You

  • Vision discount programs — flat-fee memberships ($100–$150/year for a family) offering 20–40% off exams and eyewear, no premiums or copays.
  • Online retailers — complete glasses for $30–$100 from sites like Zenni or Warby Parker, often cheaper than an in-network copay plus upgrade fees.
  • FSA/HSA funds — pay for exams, glasses, contacts, and even LASIK with pre-tax dollars, saving 20–30% depending on your tax bracket.
  • Costco/Sam's Club optical — exams around $70–$100 and glasses well below mall optical prices, no insurance needed.

Worth-It FAQs

Is vision insurance worth it for kids?

Often yes — children need annual exams (many states require them for school), and kids break or lose glasses frequently. A family plan usually pays for itself with one child alone.

Should I get vision insurance if I had LASIK?

Probably not for cost reasons. Post-LASIK you mainly need annual health exams ($100–$175 out of pocket), which is usually cheaper than a yearly premium.

Can I have two vision plans at once?

Yes, coordination of benefits applies — one plan pays primary, the other secondary. It's rarely worth paying two premiums unless both are employer-subsidized.

The Verdict

Vision insurance is worth it for most glasses and contact lens wearers — typical savings of $100–$200 per year — and a no-brainer when an employer subsidizes the premium. For exam-only users, it's often a wash or a slight loss; alternatives like discount programs or online retailers usually win.
