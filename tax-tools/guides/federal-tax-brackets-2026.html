Federal Tax Brackets 2026: Full Guide

By NAN Marketing Agency · Updated October 12, 2026 · 6 min read

The US federal income tax uses progressive brackets: your income is taxed in layers, and only the income inside each layer gets that layer's rate. The figures below are 2026 estimates (inflation-adjusted 2025 IRS brackets); the IRS announces official 2026 numbers each fall.

2026 Estimated Brackets — Single

  • 10% on income up to $12,300
  • 12% on $12,300 – $50,000
  • 22% on $50,000 – $106,500
  • 24% on $106,500 – $203,500
  • 32% on $203,500 – $258,000
  • 35% on $258,000 – $645,000
  • 37% above $645,000

2026 Estimated Brackets — Married Filing Jointly

  • 10% up to $24,600 · 12% up to $100,000 · 22% up to $213,000 · 24% up to $407,000 · 32% up to $516,000 · 35% up to $1,290,000 · 37% above

2026 Estimated Brackets — Head of Household

  • 10% up to $17,500 · 12% up to $66,800 · 22% up to $109,000 · 24% up to $204,000 · 32% up to $259,000 · 35% up to $645,000 · 37% above

Married filing separately uses half the joint thresholds.

How Marginal Rates Actually Work

Being "in the 22% bracket" does not mean all your income is taxed at 22%. Example: a single filer with $60,000 of taxable income pays 10% on the first $12,300, 12% on the next chunk, and 22% only on the last dollars. That is your marginal rate (22%) versus your effective rate (total tax ÷ total income, much lower).

What This Means for You

  • A raise that pushes you into a higher bracket never makes you poorer — only the new dollars are taxed at the higher rate.
  • Deductions save you money at your marginal rate, which is why high earners benefit more from each deduction dollar.
