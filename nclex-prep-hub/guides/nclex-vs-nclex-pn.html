NCLEX-RN vs NCLEX-PN Explained

Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

Two exams, one confusing alphabet soup. The NCLEX-RN licenses registered nurses; the NCLEX-PN licenses practical/vocational nurses (LPN/LVN). You don't choose between them freely — your nursing program determines which one you're eligible for. Here's exactly how they differ.

The real difference: scope of practice

Both exams test safe, effective nursing care — but at different levels of responsibility. The NCLEX-RN tests management-level judgment: care planning, delegation, supervision of LPNs and unlicensed staff, complex assessments, and evaluation of outcomes. Expect questions where you're the charge nurse deciding what to delegate and what to keep.

The NCLEX-PN tests practical nursing within a structured framework: data collection (rather than comprehensive assessment), implementing established care plans, reinforcing teaching (rather than initial teaching), and reporting findings to the RN. PN questions focus on doing safe care correctly; RN questions focus on deciding what care is needed.

Content emphasis

Both exams cover the same four client-needs categories, but the weighting and depth differ:

  • Management of care: heavily tested on the RN (delegation, prioritization, supervision); lighter on the PN, where it centers on assignment within scope.
  • Pharmacology: both test it, but RN questions go deeper into evaluation and adverse-effect management.
  • Physiological integrity: the largest section on both exams — roughly half of all questions.
  • NGN case studies: both exams include Next Gen items, but RN case studies demand more independent clinical judgment.

Which exam do you take?

Your program decides. Graduated from an ADN or BSN program? You take the NCLEX-RN. Completed a practical nursing program? You take the NCLEX-PN. Some LPNs later complete RN bridge programs (LPN-to-RN) and then sit for the NCLEX-RN — a common and well-supported path.

Salary and career outlook

The exams lead to different roles with different earning power. In the US, registered nurses earn a median of roughly $86,000 per year (BLS data), with wide variation by state, specialty, and setting — ICU, OR, and travel RNs earn significantly more. LPNs/LVNs earn a median of roughly $60,000 per year, typically working in long-term care, clinics, and home health.

Job growth is strong for both, but the RN credential opens doors the LPN cannot: hospital acute-care positions, leadership roles, and graduate education (NP, CRNA, nurse educator). Many LPNs use the LPN-to-RN bridge — typically a 12–18 month program — to upgrade their license and earning power. If you're deciding between programs now, the BSN route costs more time upfront but pays the highest lifetime return in nursing.

Test-day differences in practice

On exam day the two tests feel similar — same Pearson VUE centers, same CAT format, same 5-hour limit. The difference is in what the questions demand of you. RN items constantly ask "what should you do first?" with several plausible nursing actions, testing your judgment. PN items more often ask "what should you report?" or "which action is within your scope?", testing your boundaries. A useful self-check while practicing: if you catch yourself thinking like a manager (delegating, prioritizing, evaluating), you're in RN mode; if you're thinking like a careful implementer (collecting data, following the plan, reporting changes), you're in PN mode.

Can you fail one and take the other?

No. Eligibility is tied to your education, not your preference. If you fail the NCLEX-RN, you retake the NCLEX-RN after your state's waiting period (typically 45 days). The good news: repeat pass rates improve dramatically with structured question practice — which is exactly what our tools are built for.

Study strategy for each

NCLEX-RN candidates: drill delegation and prioritization relentlessly — they're the highest-yield RN topics. Practice "who do you see first" and "what do you delegate to the LPN/UAP" questions daily. Management-of-care questions alone can decide your exam, so treat every delegation scenario as a mini case study: identify what's stable, what's unstable, and who is legally allowed to do what.

NCLEX-PN candidates: master data collection vs. assessment distinctions, scope-of-practice boundaries, and fundamental procedures. Know exactly what you must report to the RN versus handle yourself. PN exams love questions where the correct answer is "notify the RN" — but only when the finding is genuinely outside your scope or indicates a significant change.

One more myth to clear up

A persistent myth says the NCLEX-PN is "easy" and the NCLEX-RN is "impossible." Neither is true. Both use the same adaptive engine, both demand clinical judgment, and both fail candidates who rely on memorization alone. The PN's narrower scope doesn't make it simple — it makes the questions more precise about boundaries and procedures. Respect whichever exam you're taking, prepare with timed questions and rationale review, and you'll walk in ready.
