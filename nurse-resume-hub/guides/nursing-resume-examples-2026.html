Nursing Resume Examples & Template 2026

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

A great nursing resume does three things in under ten seconds: prove you're licensed, show you can handle the acuity, and make the recruiter want to call you. In 2026, US hospitals still route nearly every application through an applicant tracking system (ATS) before a human ever sees it — so your format matters as much as your experience. This guide gives you real, annotated examples for the four most common nursing roles, plus the exact template that passes both the software and the human scan.

The 2026 nursing resume template

Every example below follows the same skeleton. Memorize it — it works for RN, LPN, and APRN roles alike:

  1. Header: full name, credentials (RN, BSN), phone, professional email, city/state, LinkedIn URL (optional).
  2. Professional summary: 2–3 lines — specialty, years of experience, one standout achievement, certifications.
  3. Professional experience: reverse-chronological. Each role: title, facility, city/state, dates, then 4–6 bullet points starting with strong verbs and including numbers wherever possible.
  4. Education: degree, school, graduation year. New grads put this above experience.
  5. Licenses & certifications: state license with number (or "pending — expected [month]"), BLS/ACLS, and specialty credentials (CCRN, CEN, PCCN, OCN, RNC-OB, CNOR).
  6. Clinical skills: a keyword-rich line or two-column list matching the job posting.

Example 1: ICU Registered Nurse

Why it works: the summary names the acuity level, the bullets quantify ratios and outcomes, and certifications (CCRN, ACLS) appear in the first third of the page.

MARIA DELGADO, RN, BSN
Austin, TX | maria.delgado.rn@email.com | (512) 555-0147

PROFESSIONAL SUMMARY
ICU Registered Nurse with 5 years of experience in a 24-bed surgical intensive care unit. Reduced CLABSI rates 30% via line-care audits; charge nurse for night shift; CCRN-certified.

PROFESSIONAL EXPERIENCE
Registered Nurse — Surgical ICU | City General Hospital, Austin, TX | Jun 2021 – Present
• Managed 4:1 patient ratios in a 24-bed surgical ICU with 98% patient satisfaction scores.
• Led central-line care audits that cut CLABSI rates by 30% over 12 months.
• Precepted 6 new graduate nurses through unit orientation.
• Coordinated admissions and staffing for 12 RNs as night-shift charge nurse.

LICENSES & CERTIFICATIONS
Texas RN License #987654 | BLS, ACLS | CCRN (Adult)

CLINICAL SKILLS
Ventilator management, hemodynamic monitoring, vasoactive drips, CRRT, Epic EHR

Example 2: Emergency Department RN

Why it works: triage volume and door-to-provider times are the metrics ED managers care about — both appear in the first two bullets. Trauma credentials (TNCC, CEN) are front-loaded.

JAMES OKAFOR, RN, BSN, CEN
Houston, TX | james.okafor.rn@email.com | (713) 555-0192

PROFESSIONAL SUMMARY
Emergency Department RN with 4 years in a Level I trauma center (60,000+ annual visits). ESI triage, stroke/STEMI activation, and rapid-response leadership; CEN and TNCC certified.

PROFESSIONAL EXPERIENCE
Registered Nurse — Emergency Department | Memorial Hermann Trauma Center, Houston, TX | 2022 – Present
• Triaged 40+ patients per shift using ESI levels in a 55-bed ED.
• Cut average door-to-provider time from 28 to 19 minutes through a fast-track triage pilot.
• Activated stroke and STEMI protocols; assisted with 200+ trauma resuscitations.
• Mentored 4 new ED nurses and led monthly trauma simulation drills.

7 formatting rules for 2026

  1. Single column, no tables, no text boxes. ATS parsers read top-to-bottom, left-to-right; anything else gets scrambled.
  2. Standard headings only. "Professional Experience," "Education," "Licenses & Certifications" — creative headings ("My Journey") confuse the parser.
  3. One page under 10 years of experience; two max. Cut anything older than 10–15 years unless it's remarkable.
  4. Keywords from the job posting. If the posting says "hemodynamic monitoring," your resume should say "hemodynamic monitoring" — the skills checker helps you build this list per specialty.
  5. Numbers beat adjectives. "Cared for patients" is invisible; "managed 4:1 ratios in a 24-bed ICU" gets interviews.
  6. Credentials after your name, license number in the certs section. Recruiters verify — make it easy.
