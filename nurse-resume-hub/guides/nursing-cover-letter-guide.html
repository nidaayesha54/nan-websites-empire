Nursing Cover Letter Writing Guide 2026

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Your resume proves you're qualified. Your cover letter proves you're interested — in this unit, this hospital, this patient population. Nurse managers consistently say a tailored cover letter is the tiebreaker between two equally qualified candidates, and for new-grad residency programs (where everyone has similar credentials), it can be the entire difference. The good news: you don't need to be a writer. You need four paragraphs, specific details, and zero clichés. Here's the formula.

The 4-paragraph formula

  1. Paragraph 1 — The hook (3–4 lines). Name the exact position and facility, state your years of experience and specialty, and give one reason you're excited about this role. Never open with "I am writing to apply for…" — they know.
  2. Paragraph 2 — The evidence (4–6 lines). Two or three quantified achievements that match the job posting. This is a highlight reel, not a resume repeat — pick the wins most relevant to this unit.
  3. Paragraph 3 — The "why them" (3–4 lines). One specific thing about the facility: Magnet status, a unit-based council, a residency program, community reputation. This paragraph is what proves the letter wasn't mass-sent.
  4. Paragraph 4 — The close (2–3 lines). Thank them, express enthusiasm for an interview, and state your availability. Sign with your full name and credentials.

Keep the whole letter under one page — roughly 250–350 words. Use standard business-letter formatting: your contact info at top, date, hiring manager's name if you can find it (LinkedIn and the hospital website usually reveal it).

Annotated sample: experienced RN

Maria Delgado, RN, BSN
Austin, TX | maria.delgado.rn@email.com | (512) 555-0147

October 12, 2026

Dear Ms. Carter,

[P1 — hook: exact role + facility + experience + enthusiasm]
With 5 years of experience as a critical care nurse, I am excited to apply for the Registered Nurse — Intensive Care Unit (Night Shift) position at St. David's Medical Center. Your unit's nurse-led quality improvement culture is exactly the environment where I do my best work.

[P2 — evidence: quantified, relevant achievements]
Highlights from my background include:
• Reduced CLABSI rates by 30% through central-line care audits I designed and led
• Precepted 6 new graduate nurses; served as charge nurse for night shift
• Maintained 98% patient satisfaction scores while managing 4:1 ratios in a 24-bed surgical ICU

[P3 — why them: specific, researched]
What draws me to St. David's specifically is your Magnet recognition and the ICU's published work on delirium prevention — an area where my capstone research and current practice align closely. I would be proud to contribute to a team with that standard of excellence.

[P4 — close]
Thank you for your time and consideration. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my background aligns with your needs and am available for an interview at your convenience.

Sincerely,
Maria Delgado, RN, BSN

Phoenix, AZ | alex.rivera.rn@email.com | (602) 555-0163

Dear Hiring Manager,

As a May 2026 BSN graduate from Arizona State University with 720+ clinical hours and a 180-hour ICU capstone, I am excited to apply for the ICU Nurse Residency Program at Banner Health. Your residency's structured preceptorship and simulation curriculum are exactly the foundation I want for a critical care career.

During my capstone in the surgical ICU, I managed 2-patient assignments under preceptor supervision and presented a delirium-prevention project the unit adopted for orientation. My preceptors consistently noted my assessment accuracy, calm communication with families, and eagerness to take feedback. I am BLS/ACLS certified with an Arizona license expected November 2026.

What draws me to Banner Health is your residency program's national reputation for new-grad support — including the dedicated educator model and the low first-year turnover your outcomes data shows. I want to learn critical care the right way, and your program is where I want to do it.

Thank you for your consideration. I would be grateful for the chance to discuss my fit for your residency cohort.

Sincerely,
Alex Rivera, BSN

7 cover letter mistakes nurses make

  1. "To whom it may concern." Spend five minutes finding a name — the nurse manager, the recruiter on LinkedIn. "Dear Hiring Manager" is the acceptable fallback.
  2. Repeating the resume word-for-word. The letter is a highlight reel with narrative; the resume is the full record. If a sentence appears in both, rewrite one.
  3. Cliché soup. "Hardworking team player with a passion for helping people" describes every applicant. Replace adjectives with evidence: numbers, certifications, preceptor quotes.
  4. Wrong facility name. The fastest way to get tossed — and it happens constantly with mass applications. Proofread the facility name, unit name, and manager name every single time.
  5. Apologizing for being a new grad. Never write "Although I lack experience…" — frame clinical hours, capstone, and evaluations as the preparation they are. See our new grad guide.
  6. Going over one page. If it doesn't fit, you're repeating yourself. Cut ruthlessly.
  7. No call to action. End by asking for the interview and stating availability — don't just fade out with "Thank you."
