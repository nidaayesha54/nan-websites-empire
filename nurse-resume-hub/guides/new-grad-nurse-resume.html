New Grad Nurse Resume Guide 2026

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Every experienced nurse was once a new grad staring at a blank page, wondering how to fill a resume with "no experience." Here's the truth hiring managers won't always say out loud: new-grad residency programs don't expect work history. They expect evidence — of clinical competence, coachability, and genuine commitment to the specialty. Your 700+ clinical hours, your capstone, and your preceptors' evaluations are your experience. This guide shows you how to present them so a recruiter sees a safe, ready nurse instead of a blank page.

The new grad resume structure (education first)

Unlike experienced nurses, new grads lead with education — it's your strongest credential. The order:

  1. Header — name, "RN, BSN" (once licensed), phone, professional email, city/state.
  2. Professional summary — 2–3 lines: degree, graduation date, total clinical hours, capstone specialty, certifications, license status.
  3. Education — degree, school, graduation month/year, GPA if 3.5+, honors (cum laude, Dean's List), relevant coursework only if it fills space.
  4. Clinical experience — your rotations, formatted exactly like jobs (see below).
  5. Licenses & certifications — "RN License — [State], pending (expected [month year])" is perfectly fine; list BLS, ACLS, and any others.
  6. Clinical skills — honest, student-level skills. The skills checker has a "New Grad / General" list to start from.

Turning clinical rotations into experience

This is where most new grads go wrong — they write "Clinical rotations at various hospitals" as a single line. Instead, treat your two or three most relevant rotations (plus your capstone, always) as individual positions:

The formula: Role title — Unit, Facility, City (total hours), then 2–4 bullets describing what you actually did: patient assignments, skills performed, and any initiative you showed.

Senior Capstone — Surgical ICU | Banner Health, Phoenix, AZ | Jan–May 2026 (180 hrs)
• Managed 2-patient assignments under preceptor supervision, including ventilated and post-op patients.
• Performed head-to-toe assessments, titrated vasoactive drips per protocol, and assisted with central line insertions.
• Presented a capstone project on delirium prevention that the unit adopted for orientation.

Clinical Rotation — Labor & Delivery | Dignity Health, Phoenix, AZ | Fall 2025 (120 hrs)
• Supported 15+ vaginal deliveries; monitored fetal strips and coached patients through labor.
• Earned preceptor commendation for calm, clear communication with anxious first-time mothers.

Notice what's missing: the word "observed." Everything is framed as doing, because you were doing. If a bullet only says "shadowed" or "observed," cut it or rewrite it around what you practiced.

Writing a new grad summary that works

Recruiters decide in seconds whether you're residency-ready. A strong new grad summary answers three questions: Are you licensed (or when)? What clinical exposure do you have? Why this specialty?

Weak: "Hardworking new graduate nurse seeking a challenging position where I can grow my skills." (Says nothing; could be anyone.)

Strong: "New graduate RN (BSN, May 2026) with 720+ clinical hours across med-surg, ICU, OB, and pediatrics. 180-hour ICU capstone with strong preceptor evaluations for assessment accuracy and composure under pressure. BLS/ACLS certified; Arizona license pending, expected November 2026. Seeking ICU residency to build on critical care foundation."

5 mistakes that sink new grad resumes

  1. Listing "references available upon request." Wastes a line everyone expects anyway. Bring a separate reference sheet to interviews — include one clinical instructor and one preceptor.
  2. Padding with non-nursing jobs at the top. That barista job taught you work ethic, but it doesn't belong above your capstone. One line under "Additional Experience" is plenty.
  3. Claiming skills you can't defend. If you check "ventilator management," expect an interview question about vent settings. List student-level skills honestly — preceptors' evaluations back you up.
  4. Skipping the license line. "RN License — Texas, pending (expected Dec 2026)" tells the recruiter exactly where you stand. Omitting it makes them guess — and guessing recruiters move on.
  5. One generic resume for every application. Residency programs in ICU vs. peds want different keywords. Tweak your skills line and summary for each specialty — it takes five minutes with our resume builder.
