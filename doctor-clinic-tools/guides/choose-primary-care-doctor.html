How to Choose a Primary Care Doctor: 10-Step Checklist

By DoctorClinicHub · Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

Your primary care doctor (PCP) is your first stop for checkups, sick visits, and referrals. Here's how to find a good one.

Family medicine vs internal medicine

  • Family medicine: cares for all ages, including children — good for whole-family coverage.
  • Internal medicine: adult-only, often deeper focus on complex chronic conditions.
  • Med-peds: combined internal medicine + pediatrics.

The 10-step checklist

  1. Check your insurance network. Use your insurer's "find a doctor" tool — out-of-network visits cost far more.
  2. Decide on logistics. Location, hours (evenings/weekends?), telehealth options, and typical wait for appointments.
  3. Verify board certification. Look them up on the American Board of Medical Specialties (abms.org).
  4. Check for disciplinary history. Your state medical board website lists actions against licenses.
  5. Read reviews carefully. Look for patterns (rushed visits, billing issues) rather than one-off complaints.
  6. Consider communication style. Do you want a doctor who explains everything, or one who's efficient and direct?
  7. Ask about the practice. Who covers when your doctor is away? How are after-hours calls handled? Is there a patient portal?
  8. Book a "meet and greet" or first physical. Many practices offer brief introductory visits.
  9. Prepare first-visit questions: How do you handle referrals? What's your approach to preventive care? How quickly do test results come back?
  10. Trust your gut. You should feel heard, not rushed. It's fine to switch — continuity matters, but so does fit.

Red flags

  • Consistently dismisses your concerns or won't explain reasoning
  • Pushes unnecessary tests or procedures (get a second opinion)
  • Impossible to reach; portal messages go unanswered for weeks
  • Frequent billing surprises with no transparency

Cost tip

An annual preventive physical is typically covered 100% by ACA-compliant insurance with no copay — but mention a new symptom during that visit and part of it may be billed as diagnostic. Ask the office how they code it.
