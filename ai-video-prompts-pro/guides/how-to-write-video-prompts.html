How to Write AI Video Prompts: The 2026 Beginner Guide

Writing AI video prompts is a skill, not a talent. Every great prompt — whether it makes a cinematic short, a product ad or a viral clip — is built from the same six elements. Master them once, and you'll write better prompts than 95% of users on any tool: Runway, Pika, Luma, Sora or Kling.

The 6-element framework

  1. Subject — who/what is in frame? Be concrete: "an elderly fisherman mending nets," not "a man."
  2. Action — what is happening? One clear verb chain: "casting his net as gulls scatter."
  3. Camera — how does the camera move? "Slow push-in," "aerial descent," "360-degree orbit." This is the #1 upgrade most beginners skip.
  4. Lighting — what lights the scene? "Golden-hour backlight," "neon-drenched night," "volumetric god rays."
  5. Style — what should it look like? "Cinematic photorealism," "Studio-Ghibli-inspired anime," "vintage 35mm film."
  6. Mood — what should it feel like? "Quiet melancholy," "electric excitement," "serene calm."

Negative prompts: the secret weapon

Most tools accept a negative prompt — what you don't want. A good default:

blurry, low quality, distorted faces, extra limbs, watermark, text overlay, flickering, morphing

This single line eliminates the majority of common AI video artifacts.

5 beginner mistakes to avoid

  • Prompting like a search engine — "sunset beach 4k wallpaper" is keyword soup. Write sentences; models parse language, not tags.
  • Forgetting motion — it's a video model. If nothing in your prompt moves, you get a slideshow. Name the motion: swirling, sheeting, whipping, drifting.
  • Too many subjects — one hero subject per shot. Crowds confuse current models; keep the cast small.
  • Contradictory directions — "slow motion, fast-paced, calm but energetic" cancels itself out. Pick one energy.
  • Skipping the camera — the camera move is free cinematography. "Slow push-in" costs you three words and transforms the result.

Prompting for specific tools

  • Runway / Kling: respond well to cinematic language — lens types, film stocks, camera moves.
  • Pika: loves stylized, punchy descriptions; great for anime and cartoon styles.
  • Luma: excels with natural motion descriptions — describe physics (water, cloth, hair) in detail.
  • Sora: handles longer, narrative prompts with multiple beats — write mini shot lists.
