Personal Injury Statute of Limitations by State (2026)

Every state gives you a limited window to file a personal injury lawsuit — the statute of limitations. Miss it, and the court will almost always dismiss your case, no matter how strong it was. Here is how the deadlines work, how they vary by state, and the exceptions that can shorten or extend them.

The most common deadlines at a glance

For general personal injury claims, most states allow 2 or 3 years from the date of injury:

  • 2 years — California, Texas, Florida, Pennsylvania, Virginia, Illinois, and most other states
  • 3 years — New York, Massachusetts, Michigan, North Carolina, Washington, Wisconsin
  • 1 year (the shortest) — Kentucky, Tennessee, Louisiana
  • 4–6 years (the longest) — Maine and North Dakota (6), Missouri (5), Nebraska, Utah, and Wyoming (4)

When does the clock start?

Usually on the date of injury. But under the discovery rule, the clock may start only when you knew — or reasonably should have known — that you were injured. This matters most for medical malpractice, toxic exposure, and injuries that surface slowly.

Exceptions that can change your deadline

  • Minors and incapacitated persons — deadlines are often "tolled" (paused) until they turn 18 or regain capacity.
  • Claims against the government — city, county, and state defendants frequently require written notice within 90–180 days, long before the lawsuit deadline.
  • Medical malpractice — many states set separate, shorter limits (e.g., 1 year in Ohio and Louisiana, 2.5 years in New York) and may require pre-suit notice.
  • Defendant leaves the state — some states pause the clock while the defendant is absent.
  • Wrongful death — often has its own (sometimes shorter) deadline, measured from the date of death rather than injury.

What happens if you miss the deadline?

The defendant's lawyer files a motion, and the judge dismisses the case. It does not matter that you were hurt, that the other side was at fault, or that negotiations were ongoing. Settlement talks do not pause the deadline — only a filed lawsuit (or, in rare cases, a written tolling agreement) does.

How to protect yourself

  1. Check your deadline early — ideally within weeks of the injury.
  2. Assume the shortest possible deadline if anything is unclear.
  3. Watch for government-notice rules — they are easy to miss and unforgiving.
  4. Talk to an attorney well before the cutoff — building a case takes months; do not wait for the deadline to force the issue.

The bottom line

Deadlines range from 1 to 6 years, but 2–3 is typical — and exceptions can cut them drastically. Confirm your specific deadline with a licensed attorney in your state as early as possible, and read our guide on whether you need a personal injury lawyer.
