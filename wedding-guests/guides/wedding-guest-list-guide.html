Who to Invite to Your Wedding: The Guest List Guide

By GuestListPro · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

The guest list is the single hardest part of wedding planning. Every name you add costs money — the average wedding guest costs $200–$300 in catering, rentals, and favors. Every name you cut risks a family feud. Here's how to build a list you're happy with, without the drama.

Step 1: Build the Dream List First

Before cutting anything, write down everyone you'd invite if money and venue size didn't matter. Both partners do this separately, then merge. This "dream list" is your starting point — most couples start at 200–250 names and need to land at 120–150.

Divide your dream list into three tiers:

  • Tier 1 — Non-negotiables: immediate family, wedding party, best friends. These people are coming, period.
  • Tier 2 — Important: extended family you're close to, close family friends, long-term friends.
  • Tier 3 — Nice-to-have: coworkers, distant relatives, friends-of-friends, your parents' friends.

Cutting becomes easy when you work tier by tier instead of name by name.

Step 2: Apply the One-Year Rule

Haven't spoken to them in a year? They don't make the cut. This single rule eliminates about 30% of most dream lists — old college acquaintances, ex-coworkers, your cousin's college roommate. Weddings are expensive; nostalgia isn't a seating assignment.

Step 3: Handle Plus-Ones With a Clear Policy

Plus-one etiquette causes more arguments than almost anything else. Pick one policy and apply it evenly:

  • Married/engaged/living together: always invited as a couple. No exceptions.
  • Long-term dating (1+ year): invite the partner. You know them, they're basically family.
  • Single and casual dating: this is where you can draw the line. A blanket "no plus-ones except married/engaged" policy is completely acceptable — and fair, because it's consistent.
  • Wedding party: traditionally gets plus-ones regardless, since they're investing significant time and money in your day.

The key word is consistent. The moment you make an exception for one coworker, every other coworker notices.

Step 4: Navigate Family Politics

Parents often have their own invite list — sometimes 30+ names. Here's the framework that works:

  1. Agree on a total headcount first (driven by budget and venue).
  2. Split it: typically 50% couple's friends, 25% bride's family, 25% groom's family — adjust to fit.
  3. Give each set of parents their number and let them prioritize within it.
  4. If parents are contributing financially, they get a slightly bigger say — but the final call is yours.

Step 5: The B-List Strategy (Done Right)

About 15–20% of guests decline a typical wedding invitation. Smart couples plan for this:

  • Send A-list invitations 10–12 weeks before the wedding.
  • As declines come in, send B-list invitations at the 6–8 week mark.
  • B-list guests receive the exact same invitation — same design, same wording. Nobody knows which list they were on.
  • Never tell anyone they're on the B-list. Ever.

Is it rude? Not if handled well. What's rude is an empty venue because you were too proud to plan for declines.

Step 6: Kids — Yes or No?

An adults-only wedding is increasingly common and completely fine. If you go this route:

  • Address invitations to the adults by name — no "and family."
  • Put it clearly on the wedding website: "We love your little ones, but our venue can't accommodate children."
  • Make exceptions only for babes-in-arms and immediate family — and apply them consistently.
  • Expect some pushback from parents of young kids. That's okay. It's your wedding.

Quick Answers

What percentage of wedding guests typically RSVP no?

On average, 15–20% of invited guests decline. For destination weddings, expect 30–40% declines. Plan your B-list accordingly.

Is it rude to have a B-list for a wedding?

Not if handled well. Send A-list invitations 10–12 weeks out, then fill declined spots with B-list guests at 6–8 weeks. Nobody needs to know which list they were on.
