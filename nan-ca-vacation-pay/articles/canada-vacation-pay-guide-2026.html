Canada Vacation Pay Guide 2026: Rates by Province & How It's Paid

The complete guide to Canadian vacation pay: province-by-province rates, years-of-service rules, payout timing, and mistakes to avoid.

In-depth 2026 guide~6 min readFree

Every year, thousands of Canadian workers leave money on the table without knowing it. Vacation pay is not a bonus your employer chooses to give you — it is a statutory right in every province and territory, calculated as a percentage of your wages. But the percentage is different in British Columbia than in Quebec, it rises with your years of service, and the rules about when it gets paid vary too.

This guide explains exactly how vacation pay works in Canada in 2026: the rates for each jurisdiction, how the years-of-service thresholds work, what counts as "wages," when the money must reach you, and the common mistakes that cost workers real dollars. Vacation time vs. vacation pay: two separate rights

The first thing to understand is that Canadian employment law gives you two distinct entitlements. Vacation time is the number of paid weeks off you are owed each year — typically 2 weeks when you start, rising to 3 (and in some jurisdictions 4) as your service grows. Vacation pay is the money: a statutory percentage of your wages that funds that time off.

Your employer must provide both. They cannot give you the weeks off and skip the pay percentage, and they cannot pay the percentage and refuse you the time. The two are linked — the pay percentage roughly corresponds to the weeks (4% ≈ 2 weeks of a 52-week year, 6% ≈ 3 weeks) — but they are legally separate obligations.

Vacation pay rates by province and territory (2026)

Canada does not have one national vacation pay rate. Each province and territory sets its own minimums, and the rate steps up after a set number of years of service with the same employer:

Saskatchewan is the outlier: instead of the 4%/6% model, it grants vacation as weeks — 3 weeks after 1 year (3/52 of annual wages ≈ 5.77%) and 4 weeks after 10 years (4/52 ≈ 7.69%). Federally regulated employees fall under the Canada Labour Code, which is the only regime that reaches 8%.

How your years of service move the rate

The service thresholds are the detail most workers miss. "Years of service" generally means continuous employment with the same employer — including time on approved leave in most jurisdictions. The moment you cross the threshold, the higher rate applies to the wages you earn from that point on (it is not retroactive to your start date).

A concrete example: an Ontario worker earning $60,000. In years 1–4, they accrue 4% = $2,400 per year. Once they complete 5 years of service, the rate jumps to 6% = $3,600 per year — an extra $1,200 annually for the same salary, plus an extra week of time off. Over a decade, crossing that threshold is worth thousands of dollars, so it pays to know exactly when your anniversary falls.

Quebec's threshold is the shortest in the country (3 years), while Newfoundland and Labrador's is the longest (15 years). If you are federally regulated, there are two step-ups: at 5 years (to 6%) and at 10 years (to 8%, the highest statutory rate in Canada).

What counts as "wages" for vacation pay

The percentage is applied to your gross wages, which in most provinces includes more than just your base salary:

  • Regular salary or hourly earnings
  • Overtime pay
  • Commissions
  • Non-discretionary bonuses (bonuses tied to performance targets)
  • Shift premiums and statutory holiday pay in several jurisdictions

What is generally not included: the vacation pay itself (it is not counted twice), discretionary gifts, and expense reimbursements. The exact inclusions vary by jurisdiction — Ontario's Employment Standards Act, for example, has a detailed definition of "wages" — so if a large part of your income is commission or bonus-based, check your province's guide. When must vacation pay be paid?

In most provinces, vacation pay must be paid at least one day before your vacation starts. This is the rule many employers quietly break by simply adding a vacation-pay line to every paycheque. That practice is only lawful in most jurisdictions if you agree to it in writing — it is not the employer's default choice to make.

Quebec works differently: it uses a May 1 to April 30 reference year. Your vacation entitlement for the year is calculated on wages earned during the previous reference year, and the pay is due when the vacation is taken.

The non-negotiable rule everywhere: accrued but unused vacation pay must be paid out when your employment ends, usually with your final pay. This applies whether you quit, are fired, or are laid off. If your final paycheque arrives without that payout, you are owed money.

5 mistakes that cost workers vacation pay

1. Not knowing your threshold date. If your employer keeps paying 4% after your 5th anniversary in Ontario, the underpayment compounds every pay period. Check your rate the month you cross the threshold.

2. Accepting paycheque-by-paycheque vacation pay without agreeing. If you never agreed in writing, your employer may owe you a lump sum before each vacation — and interest arguments get stronger the longer it goes on.

3. Forgetting commissions and overtime. If 30% of your income is commission, your vacation pay should be calculated on 100% of your wages, not 70%. Ask payroll which earnings base they use.

4. Assuming the rate is the same everywhere. Moving from Quebec (6% at 3 years) to New Brunswick (6% at 8 years) can quietly cost you two percentage points for years if nobody updates your file.

5. Leaving unused vacation pay behind when you quit. You do not forfeit it. It is wages owed to you, and employment standards offices in every province will enforce the payout.

What to do if your employer underpays you

Start with payroll or HR in writing — many underpayments are file errors, not malice, and a short email with your anniversary date and the statutory rate resolves them. If that fails, every province and territory has an employment standards office that investigates wage complaints free of charge and can order your employer to pay what is owed, sometimes going back several years. Keep your pay stubs and your record of employment; they are your evidence.

Your contract or collective agreement can only ever improve on these minimums, never reduce them. If your contract says 3 weeks from day one, that overrides the statutory 2 — but a contract can never lawfully pay you less than your province's minimum rate.

FAQs

Is vacation pay taxed?

Yes. Vacation pay is employment income and is subject to income tax, CPP/QPP, and EI deductions just like regular wages, whether it is paid as a lump sum or on each paycheque.

Do part-time and casual workers get vacation pay?

Yes. The statutory minimums apply regardless of full-time or part-time status — the percentage applies to whatever wages you actually earn.

Can my employer force me to take vacation at a specific time?

Generally yes, with reasonable notice — employers can schedule vacations to suit business needs. But they cannot use scheduling to deny your statutory minimum time or pay.

What if I work in one province but live in another?

Employment standards follow the jurisdiction where you work, not where you live. Remote workers should confirm which province's rules their employer applies.

Does vacation pay apply during parental or sick leave?

You generally do not accrue vacation pay on Employment Insurance benefits, but your years of service usually continue to accumulate during approved leaves, keeping your threshold progress intact.
