AI-Edited Videos & the New Disclosure Rules: What the EU AI Act (Article 50), C2PA and Platform Labels Mean for You

Refreshed November 23, 2026 · 10 min read · By AIVideoEditor

On August 2, 2026, the transparency obligations of the EU AI Act — Article 50 — took effect. In plain terms: AI-generated or AI-manipulated video must now be marked as such in a machine-readable way, and anyone publishing it to an EU audience has to disclose the AI's role. This is no longer a "best practice" or a platform nicety. It is law, and it lands squarely in your editing workflow — the labeling happens in the editor, at export, and at upload. This guide gives you the practical version: what the rule requires, how C2PA content credentials work inside the tools you already use, where the disclosure buttons live on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram, and the voice-cloning consent rules that can sink a video even when the label is on.

1. What Article 50 actually requires (in creator terms)

Strip away the legal language and Article 50 says two things that matter to you:

  1. Providers of AI systems must mark their outputs. If your video was generated or meaningfully altered by AI, it should carry machine-readable provenance — a tag, watermark or metadata that tools can detect. This is the "provider" obligation, but it only works if you don't strip it.
  2. You, the person publishing it, must disclose the AI's role. Deepfakes, synthetic voices, AI-edited footage that could mislead — you must tell the audience, clearly and where they can see it. A caption mention counts; burying it in a description paragraph usually does not.

Note what counts: "AI-edited" is not limited to fully generated clips. AI background replacement, AI voice cleanup or voice swaps, AI avatars speaking your script, face swaps, and auto-generated B-roll all sit inside the disclosure zone. Plain transcript-based cuts and caption styling do not trigger it — that is editing assisted by AI, not content created by AI. When in doubt, the platforms' own guidance is: disclose. Over-disclosure has no penalty; under-disclosure does.

2. C2PA content credentials: the label your editor can bake in

C2PA Content Credentials are becoming the default way AI output gets marked: a tamper-evident metadata record attached to the file at export, listing which AI model or tool touched it. Think of it like EXIF data for the AI era. Why you should care:

  • YouTube, TikTok and Meta can read C2PA and use it to detect AI-generated content automatically — including content whose uploader "forgot" to tick the disclosure box.
  • Exporting with credentials protects you if your clip gets reposted without credit. The provenance travels with the file.
  • Stripping credentials looks like concealment. Running AI footage through a tool that scrubs metadata, or re-exporting through a chain that drops the credentials, undermines the one thing Article 50 providers are required to do. Don't do it.

As of November 2026, C2PA export support is live or rolling out in the major editing pipelines: Adobe's tools (Premiere Pro, Firefly) attach credentials by default, OpenAI's and Google's generators embed them, and most serious AI editors have added an export toggle. Make it part of your export preset: always export AI-assisted projects with content credentials on. Check the export dialog — the toggle often sits near the watermark/format options.

3. Platform disclosure rules as of November 2026

The platforms moved faster than most regulators. Here's the current state of the three that matter most:

YouTube: altered-content disclosure

YouTube requires disclosure when your video contains realistic-looking AI-generated or manipulated content — synthetic scenes, altered footage, AI voices presenting as real people. In YouTube Studio the setting lives in the upload flow ("Altered content" checkbox), and confirmed uploads show a disclosure label in the description area. Skipping it on realistic AI content risks removal or reduced distribution. YouTube also runs its own detection (C2PA reads, SynthID watermark reads), so the checkbox is not your only line of defense.

TikTok: automatic AI labeling

TikTok labels AI-generated content both from its own detection and from C2PA metadata in your file — and its rules require you to disclose AI-generated content at upload. The AI-generated label appears as a visible tag on the video. TikTok is the strictest of the three about realistic AI content presented without disclosure.

Instagram/Facebook: "AI info" label

Meta labels content it detects as AI-generated with an "AI info" tag, and its policy asks creators to disclose when AI meaningfully altered realistic content. The safest practice on Meta: enable the label in your editor/export AND tick disclosure at upload. Two layers, zero ambiguity.

The pattern across all three: the platforms detect what you don't disclose. The label no longer hurts reach — what hurts is getting caught without it.

4. Where the label buttons live in your editor

You don't need a legal department; you need to know where the toggles are. As of November 2026:

  • Adobe Premiere Pro (Firefly features): generative features attach C2PA credentials by default. Generative Extend and other Firefly-powered tools tag their output — verify in export settings.
  • CapCut: AI-generated content from CapCut's AI features carries platform-readable markers; check the export/publish panel for the AI-content toggle and leave it on.
  • Descript: Overdub (AI voice cloning of yourself) and AI-dubbing features are the ones that need disclosure; transcripts, filler-word removal and captions do not. Use the publish/share settings to confirm labeling.
  • DaVinci Resolve: Resolve's AI features (voice isolation, magic mask) are assistive, not generative — no disclosure triggered. If you composite in AI-generated B-roll from elsewhere, the disclosure travels with that asset, so keep its credentials intact.
  • Runway / Luma / Kling / Veo pipelines: exports from AI generators embed C2PA or invisible watermarks (e.g., SynthID) by default. Do not re-export through apps that strip metadata.

Practical rule: the disclosure happens at three moments — export (credentials on), upload (platform disclosure checkbox ticked), and caption (a plain "AI-generated/edited with AI" line for human viewers). One is not enough.

5. Voice cloning and likeness: the rule that ends channels

This is the compliance area most likely to actually get a creator in trouble, because it has real victims. Rules in force in 2026:

  • Consent first, always. Cloning someone's voice without permission is illegal in an expanding set of jurisdictions — Tennessee's ELVIS Act is already in force, and the US federal NO FAKES bill (S. 4591) cleared the Senate Judiciary Committee in June 2026 and has sat on the Senate calendar as No. 446 since June 24, 2026, with no floor vote as of November 9, 2026. It is still a bill, not law. What is law now: the TAKE IT DOWN Act (Pub. L. 119-12), which criminalizes publishing intimate deepfakes, and the DEFIANCE Act, which passed the Senate on January 13, 2026 and awaits the House. Commercial use without consent is the fast lane to a takedown and a lawsuit.
  • Reputable voice tools require verification. Descript's Overdub, ElevenLabs and their peers require voice-consent verification to clone a voice. Treat any tool that clones a stranger's voice with no questions asked as a red flag, not a feature.
  • Label synthetic speech. An AI-narrated video should say so. Listeners deserve to know the voice is synthetic, and Article 50's disclosure duty covers audio-only AI content too.
  • Parody is not a blanket shield. "It's just a joke" does not fix a realistic fake of a real person's voice or face used commercially. If a reasonable viewer could be fooled, disclose — and get consent.

6. Your 10-minute pre-publish compliance checklist

Run this on every video that used AI beyond captions and cuts — use our editing checklist tool to keep it as a reusable template:

  1. Inventory the AI. Which parts are AI-generated or AI-altered? (Background, voice, avatar, B-roll, music.) Write it down.
  2. Export with credentials. C2PA/content-credentials toggle ON in the export dialog; confirm metadata survived by re-importing.
  3. Tick the platform disclosure at upload (YouTube "Altered content", TikTok AI label, Meta "AI info").
  4. Add one human-readable line in the caption or first comment: e.g., "Created with AI — edited in CapCut/Premiere, voice generated with ElevenLabs."
  5. Check voice consent. Every synthetic voice on the timeline: is it yours, licensed, or consented? If you can't answer in five seconds, replace it.
  6. Check the captions. Auto-captions are a legal accessibility win — time them properly with our caption timer and make sure the disclosure line isn't buried behind them.

Total added time: under ten minutes once it's routine. Total risk removed: most of it.

7. What actually happens if you skip it

Let's be concrete. On platforms: unlabeled AI content that gets detected gets labeled anyway (losing your credibility), downranked, demonetized, or removed — TikTok and YouTube both remove realistic unlabeled AI content, especially around sensitive topics. On the legal side: the EU AI Act carries fines scaled to company size for providers, and publishing obligations are enforceable against distributors and deployers serving EU audiences. In the US, voice-clone cases are already being brought under right-of-publicity and state AI laws. None of this requires malice — "I forgot" is not a defense against any of it.

8. November 23 update: the law moved — and the first big likeness lawsuit landed

Three things changed in the month since this guide was published, and together they show where disclosure rules are heading. First, the likeness fight got its highest-profile test case: on November 17, 2026, LeBron James sued PlayVision (the "Bron" sports-betting app) in New York federal court, alleging a November 4 video ad used an AI-generated version of him — his build, tattoos, a "Bron" No. 23 jersey and a simulated voice — without his permission. This is the scenario section 5 warned about, now playing out with a global celebrity and a commercial advertiser. The takeaway for ordinary creators is not the damages figure; it is that a realistic AI version of a real person in an ad is now the kind of thing that draws a federal lawsuit within two weeks.

Second, the federal picture sharpened in both directions. The TAKE IT DOWN Act is now law (Pub. L. 119-12) — publishing intimate deepfakes is a federal crime, and platforms must remove them on notice. The DEFIANCE Act, which gives victims of non-consensual intimate deepfakes a civil right of action, passed the Senate on January 13, 2026 and is still waiting on the House. But the NO FAKES Act (S. 4591) has not moved since June 24, 2026: it sits on the Senate calendar as No. 446, no floor vote in either chamber as of November 9, 2026, and it dies with this Congress on January 3, 2027 unless both chambers pass it. Do not plan your compliance around a federal voice-and-likeness right that does not exist yet — plan around the state laws that do (Tennessee's ELVIS Act, California and New York digital-replica rules) and the platform rules that are already enforced.

Third, the platform enforcement behind this guide's advice is getting easier to verify. The three-step routine in section 4 — credentials on at export, disclosure ticked at upload, one plain line in the caption — is still the whole playbook, and nothing in the last month weakened it. What changed is the cost of skipping it: detection now happens fast enough that an unlabeled realistic AI clip can be labeled, downranked or removed before it ever reaches an audience, and the LeBron case shows the legal exposure is no longer theoretical either. If your workflow still treats disclosure as optional, November 2026 is the month to fix that — before a client project, not after one.

FAQ

Do AI captions and transcript editing require disclosure?

No. Assistive AI (transcription, captions, silence removal, filler-word removal) is editing, not generation. The disclosure duty covers content AI created or realistically altered — scenes, faces, voices, synthetic presenters.

Does the AI label hurt my video's reach?

No evidence of that as of November 2026. What hurts reach is detection-without-disclosure: removals, downranking and demonetization are the documented outcomes of skipping the label.

My editor doesn't show a C2PA option. What do I do?

Update it — credential export was added to most editors during 2026. If your tool truly lacks it, export normally and tick the platform disclosure at upload; the checkbox plus a caption line satisfies the human-facing part of the duty.

Is stock footage from AI generators fine for client work?

Yes, with two checks: keep the C2PA credentials intact through your edit, and verify the generator's license covers commercial use (most paid tiers do; free tiers are often non-commercial). Tell the client it contains AI-generated material.
