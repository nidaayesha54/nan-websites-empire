How to Write AI Video Prompts: The Complete 5-Part Method

Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

Writing AI video prompts is a skill, not a lottery. The models behind Sora, Runway, Kling, Veo, and Luma all respond to the same thing: specific, structured descriptions of motion. This guide teaches the complete method — from your first prompt to advanced control techniques.

Part 1: The subject — be concrete, not poetic

"A beautiful scene" means nothing to a model. "A red lighthouse on a rocky cliff" means everything. Use nouns you can point at: species, materials, colors, clothing, ages. If a detail matters to you, name it.

Part 2: The action — describe motion explicitly

This is where video prompting differs from image prompting. Name the verb and its quality: "walking slowly," "splashing playfully," "rotating smoothly." One action per shot — two simultaneous actions confuse the motion model and cause morphing.

Part 3: The camera — direct the shot

Camera language is the fastest quality upgrade in prompting. Learn six moves and reuse them:

  • Wide establishing shot — sets the scene
  • Slow push-in — builds emotion and focus
  • Aerial / drone — scale and awe
  • Tracking shot — follows the action
  • Close-up — intimacy and detail
  • Low angle — power and drama

Part 4: Lighting & mood — set the feeling

Lighting does 80% of the "cinematic" work. "Golden-hour light, soft shadows" will outperform any filter. Pair it with one mood word — joyful, tense, peaceful, mysterious — and keep them consistent. Contradictions ("bright dark lighting") are a top cause of weird output.

Part 5: Style — lock the look

End with a style anchor: "cinematic film still," "ultra photorealistic, 8k," "anime cel shading." This keeps frames consistent across regenerations — essential if you're cutting multiple clips together.

Advanced techniques

Front-load what matters

Models weight the start of your prompt more heavily. Put subject + action first, style tags last.

Use negative prompts

Tell the model what to avoid: blurry, extra limbs, flickering, watermark, distorted faces. Our negative prompt builder generates one in seconds.

Control duration expectations

Short clips (5–10s) handle one action cleanly; longer generations drift. Plan sequences as multiple short shots instead of one long prompt.

Iterate on one variable

When a result is close but off, change only one element — the camera, the lighting, the action — and regenerate. Changing everything at once teaches you nothing.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Vague subjects: "a nice dog" → "a golden retriever puppy"
  • No motion verb: add one action per prompt
  • Style soup: pick one style, not three
  • Skipping negative prompts: artifacts are predictable — block them
