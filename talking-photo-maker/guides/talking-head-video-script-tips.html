Talking Head Video Script Tips: Hooks, Pacing & Pauses

A talking avatar is only as good as its script. The tool renders the face; the script holds the viewer. These six techniques are what separate talking head videos people finish from ones they scroll past.

1. The 3-second hook

You have three seconds before the thumb moves. Open with a pattern interrupt — a bold claim, a surprising question, or a curiosity gap. Never open with "Hi guys, welcome back to my channel" — that's a viewer-repellent. Compare:

  • ❌ "In this video I'm going to talk about budgeting tips."
  • ✅ "You're losing $200 a month and you don't even know it."

The second version creates an open loop the brain wants closed. Every script should start with one.

2. Pacing: the 75-word rule

AI voices speak at roughly 150 words per minute — that's 75 words per 30 seconds of video. Count your words before you generate: a 200-word script is not a 30-second video, it's 80 seconds, and viewers will feel the drag. Short sentences read as confident; long ones read as robotic. When in doubt, cut 20%.

3. Pause placement

Pauses are where meaning lives. Mark them with ellipses (...) or [pause] in your script — most talking photo tools honor them. Place pauses:

  • After the hook — one beat of silence lets the claim land.
  • Before a key number or reveal — anticipation doubles impact.
  • Between ideas — gives the avatar's expression time to shift naturally.

Never place pauses mid-thought; it breaks the illusion that someone is actually speaking.

4. The HBC structure

Every high-retention talking head video follows Hook → Body → CTA:

  • Hook (10%): the 3-second pattern interrupt.
  • Body (75%): 1–3 beats, each a single idea with a mini-payoff. Transition between beats ("which brings me to...").
  • CTA (15%): one clear action — not three. "Follow for part two" beats "like, subscribe, comment, share and check my course."

5. CTAs that convert

The best CTAs feel like continuations, not interruptions. Tie the CTA to the video's promise: "I just showed you the first step — follow and I'll show you the other four tomorrow." Specific, low-friction, curiosity-driven.

6. Before/after examples

Before: "Hello everyone. Today I want to discuss the important topic of time management, which is something many people struggle with in their daily lives..." (23 words, zero tension, viewers gone.)

After: "You're not lazy — your system is broken. [pause] Here's the 2-minute fix that actually sticks." (16 words, open loop, pause for impact.)

Same topic, completely different retention. The difference is all in the writing.
