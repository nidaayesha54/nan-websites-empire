How Much Does an AI Music Video Cost in 2026? Real Pricing, Tool by Tool

By AIMusicVideo · 11 min read · Refreshed November 9, 2026

The AI music video market changed more in October 2026 than in the two years before it. New video models (Veo 3.1, Kling 3.0, Runway Gen-4.5) reshuffled the price-per-shot math, song-first tools like Freebeat and BeatViz matured into full video workflows, and fresh hands-on reviews tested what the "free" tiers actually give you. If you're budgeting a release this month, here's the honest picture — what one full 3:30 music video really costs, tool by tool, and how to keep your whole budget under $50 without watermarked, non-commercial leftovers.

1. What "free" actually buys you

Every major AI music video tool has a free tier in 2026, but they are trialware, not production tiers. Hands-on October 2026 testing on phones and desktop found the same pattern across the board:

  • Freebeat: 30-second clips, watermarked, non-commercial license. Fine for a teaser or a concept test — not for a release.
  • LTX Studio: 800 one-time credits, personal use only. Enough to storyboard and pre-visualize, not to ship.
  • Runway: roughly 125 one-time credits, which translates to about 10 seconds of its best model. A prompt sampler, nothing more.
  • Phone apps (Sondo, Yuna, Mozart AI, One More Shot): most lead with a weekly plan. Testers found the first finished video costing $4.99–$19.98 depending on the plan, and downloading often required a subscription on top of the pay-per-video price. Yuna and Mozart AI kept faces closest to the source photo.

The takeaway: free tiers are excellent for one thing — proving your concept before you spend. Generate the key look, the lyric timing, the avatar face. Then pay for exactly one production pass instead of burning paid credits on experiments. Our concept generator and lyric planner are genuinely free tools you can use for the planning stage so paid credits only go toward final frames.

2. Cost of one 3:30 video, tool by tool

For a typical 3 minute 30 second song at 1080p, published October 2026 pricing breaks down roughly like this for the raw-generation models (prices change often — always check the vendor before you pay):

  • Veo 3.1 Lite — ~$17 for a full 3:30 video in raw footage, one take per shot. The budget champion among the big models, but "raw footage only" means no editing, no beat-matching, and no retakes included.
  • Kling 4.0 — ~$33 at 720p / ~$49 at 1080p for a full 3:30 video (API rates ~$0.157 a second at 720p, ~$0.235 at 1080p, checked November 6, 2026). Kling 4.0 replaced 3.0 on October 15, 2026 and generates up to 30 seconds with sound in a single take — audio is now included, and the longer takes mean far fewer shots to stitch. A cheaper Flash tier (~$0.094 a second, 720p, up to 20 seconds) covers lyric-hook clips and test renders.
  • Runway Gen-4.5 — ~$39. Highest control of the general video models, but not music-first: you supply the editing skill.
  • Veo 3.1 Standard — ~$84. Best raw quality, but the premium is steep for a full-length video.
  • Veo 3.1 Fast — ~$25. The sweet spot for iterating: cheap enough to burn a take or two, fast enough to keep momentum.
  • LongStories.ai — ~$50 (as low as ~$26 at 480p). The outlier: it ships a complete video with voices and captions, not raw footage — closer to a finished product price.
  • Freebeat / BeatViz / Neural Frames: credit systems that vary with video length and model choice; plan on $15–$40 for a full-length project depending on model tier.

Now the honest correction the price tables leave out: raw-footage tools look cheapest until you count retakes. Most videos need at least a few shots regenerated — faces drift, motion artifacts ruin a take, a scene reads wrong against the lyric. Budget for 30–50% more generations than your shot list calls for. A 12-shot video plan means budgeting roughly 16–18 generations.

3. The hidden costs nobody quotes

Five line items inflate AI music video budgets more than model pricing:

  • Retakes. The single biggest cost multiplier. See above: pad every plan by 30–50%.
  • Music generation itself. If you're making the song with AI too (Suno, Udio), those credits are a separate spend. Tools that bundle song + video from one brief (Enkio, Google's Flow Music) can collapse this cost into a single workflow.
  • Editing and beat-matching. Raw-footage models don't cut to the beat. Either your time in Premiere or a tool that automates it.
  • Upscaling and mastering. Generating at 480p or 720p to save credits, then upscaling the final edit, is cheaper than generating 1080p throughout — but it adds a tool (and sometimes a fee).
  • Subscriptions traps on phones. The October 2026 app tests flagged weekly plans as the worst value: a $9.99/week plan that still doesn't cover one full video. Read the credit math before subscribing, not after.

4. The under-$50 release strategy

Here's a concrete strategy that October 2026 pricing actually supports. Goal: a finished, watermark-free, monetizable 3:30 video plus vertical cuts for Shorts/Reels/TikTok, for under $50 total.

  1. Plan free. Concept, lyric timing, shot list — use our free tools plus the free tiers (LTX's 800 credits are perfect pre-visualization).
  2. Generate keyframes cheap. Run your stills and style references on a low-cost tier first. Lock the look before you pay for motion.
  3. Animate with Veo 3.1 Fast or Lite. ~$17–$25 for the raw footage of a full song, with 30–50% retake padding built into your plan.
  4. Edit yourself. Beat-mapped editing is the skill gap between a $24 video and a $2,000 video. CapCut and Premiere both cut to markers; spend an hour, not money.
  5. Cut vertical versions from the same footage. Your 9:16 Shorts, lyric hooks, and countdown clips come from the same paid generations — don't regenerate for every platform.

Total: roughly $20–$40 for the video generation, zero for planning, zero for editing. The remaining budget is your insurance for retakes.

5. Song-first tools: the Freebeat / BeatViz / Neural Frames decision

October 2026 reviews split the market into two camps, and choosing the right one saves real money:

  • Music-first tools (Freebeat, BeatViz): designed around song-to-video. Beat sync, lyric workflows, and avatar focus are built in. If your deliverable is a music video for an actual finished song, start here — you'll spend less on post-production because the tool already understands beats.
  • Audio-reactive tools (Neural Frames): stem and frequency reactivity for abstract, electronic, or ambient visuals. Best value when your aesthetic is motion-design rather than narrative.
  • General video models (Runway, Veo, Kling): maximum creative control, zero music understanding. Cheaper per raw frame, more expensive in your editing time.

Rule of thumb: if you're an independent artist with a finished track, a music-first tool wins on total cost. If you're a filmmaker who edits manually and wants total control, the general models win. Mismatch — using a cinematic model for a simple lyric video, or a lyric tool for a narrative short — is how budgets quietly double.

6. Budget it before you generate

The cheapest generation is the one you don't need. Before touching any paid tool, run the numbers: shot count × estimated retakes × per-shot cost. Our AI vs traditional budget calculator is built for exactly this — it compares AI production against a traditional crew shoot so you can see where the real savings land (spoiler: it's usually 90%+). Build the concept with the concept generator, plan the timing with the lyric planner, then spend your $50 once — on the production pass, not the experiments.

7. When not to use AI (the honest exceptions)

An AI video budget of under $50 beats a $50,000 crew shoot on cost — but not on everything. Know the exceptions so you don't burn credits solving the wrong problem:

  • Real-performance footage. If the video's entire point is watching the actual artist perform — live energy, real choreography, genuine crowd shots — AI approximates it badly and audiences can tell. Hybrid is the smart move: shoot the performance cheaply, generate only the fantasy cutaways.
  • Lip-sync of your own face to a foreign vocal. Lip-sync tools work, but frame-level quality still needs checking syllable by syllable. For a 3:30 song, budget 1–2 hours of lip-sync review regardless of tool.
  • YouTube Content ID and commercial rights. Several "free" AI video outputs carry non-commercial or personal-use licenses (Freebeat's free tier, LTX personal use). A video you plan to monetize must come from a commercial tier — the $0 option that gets your video claimed or pulled is the most expensive option of all.
  • Client work with approval rounds. Client feedback cycles multiply retakes. Price AI client videos per round, not per video — three revision rounds at AI costs still undercuts a reshoot, but only if you quoted for the rounds.

Used within these limits, AI video in October 2026 is the best production value in music history: a release-quality video for less than the cost of a studio microphone. Plan free, spend once, and let the beat-mapped edit do the heavy lifting.

8. November 2026 update: Kling 4.0 changes the math

The biggest pricing change since this guide was first published is Kling 4.0, which rolled out to all Kling users on October 15, 2026. It replaces the Kling 3.0 figures in section 2 above — and it changes the budgeting logic, not just the numbers.

Fewer takes, bigger stakes. Kling 4.0 generates up to 30 seconds with sound in one pass, double Kling 3.0's ceiling. A 3:30 song now needs roughly seven 30-second takes instead of twelve or more short shots, so the stitching and beat-matching work shrinks. The trade-off: a bad 30-second take costs more than a bad 8-second one — about $4.71 at 720p or $7.05 at 1080p per take at API rates. The 30–50% retake padding in section 2 still applies; it just applies to fewer, larger generations.

Flash first, full second. The practical workflow now is: lock the look and the lyric timing with Kling 4.0 Flash (~$0.094 a second at 720p, clips up to 20 seconds) or Veo 3.1 Fast, then run the final pass on full Kling 4.0. Flash is also the cheapest way to make the vertical lyric hooks and countdown clips in section 4 — a 15-second hook costs roughly $1.41 at 720p.

The under-$50 strategy still holds — with a new cheapest path. Veo 3.1 Lite at ~$17 for raw footage remains the budget champion from section 2, but Kling 4.0 at ~$33 (720p) is now the "fewest takes" option: less editing time, included audio, and no separate lip-sync step for vocal shots. If your time is worth more than the ~$16 gap, Kling 4.0 is the better buy; if every dollar counts, Veo Lite plus a manual edit still wins. Either way, verify the per-second rate on the vendor's pricing page before you pay — Kling's own developer API and its subscription plans price the same model differently, and the gap between them is several dollars on a full-length video.
