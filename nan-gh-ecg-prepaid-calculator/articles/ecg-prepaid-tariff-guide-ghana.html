ECG Prepaid Tariffs Explained

How ECG prepaid tariffs decide how many kWh your cedis buy: lifeline and residential bands, PURC quarterly reviews, and tips to stretch your units.

Home  /  Guides

"I bought GH₵100 of credit but got fewer units than last month." It's the most common prepaid-meter complaint in Ghana — and the answer is almost always the tariff band. The Electricity Company of Ghana (ECG) doesn't sell you a fixed number of units per cedi. Your units depend on your customer category, your consumption band, and the PURC's quarterly tariff reviews. Here's how it all works.

How ECG prepaid pricing works

ECG prepaid meters deduct credit as you consume, but the price per unit (kWh) isn't flat. The Public Utilities Regulatory Commission (PURC) sets tariffs by customer category and consumption band, and reviews them quarterly based on the cedi–dollar exchange rate, inflation, fuel costs (especially natural gas) and the generation mix. That quarterly review is exactly why the same GH₵100 buys a different number of units from one quarter to the next. The tariff bands (approximate 2026 figures)

Indicative rates per kWh:

Lifeline residential (0–30 kWh/month)≈ GH₵0.87/kWh
Standard residential (0–300 kWh/month)≈ GH₵1.97/kWh
Non-residential (0–300 kWh/month)≈ GH₵1.78/kWh
Non-residential (301+ kWh/month)≈ GH₵2.16/kWh

What this means in practice: a standard residential customer buying GH₵100 gets roughly 51 kWh, while a lifeline customer (using under 30 kWh a month) gets about 115 kWh for the same money — the lifeline band is deliberately subsidised for low-income households. But there's a catch: if your monthly consumption exceeds 30 kWh, you lose the lifeline rate on subsequent purchases and the standard residential rate applies.

Note the oddity in the table: small businesses (non-residential, 0–300 kWh) actually pay slightly less per unit than standard residential customers. Cross the 300 kWh line, though, and the rate steps up.

Why your units vary month to month

  1. Quarterly PURC reviews. Tariffs move every quarter. For example, tariffs rose about 9.86% in January 2026 under the multi-year tariff order, then fell about 4.81% in April 2026 as the cedi strengthened and inflation eased. Your units per cedi move with them.
  2. Service charges. A fixed monthly service charge is deducted from your credit — lifeline customers pay a smaller one (around GH₵2.13/month) than standard residential customers (around GH₵10.73/month).
  3. Crossing band thresholds. Moving from under 30 kWh to over 30 kWh (or past 300 kWh) changes your effective rate for the rest of the month.
  4. Debt and arrears. Outstanding balances on the meter are recovered from new purchases before units are issued.

Reading your meter and token

Every ECG token receipt shows the units issued and the amount paid. Divide amount by units to get your effective rate per kWh for that purchase, then compare it with the bands above. If the rate doesn't match any band, check for debt deductions or a recent tariff change. ECG's mobile app and USSD services let you check balances and purchase history without visiting an office.

Seven ways to make your units last

  1. Know which band you're really in. A household consistently under 30 kWh/month keeps the lifeline rate — worth protecting if you're close to the line.
  2. Switch to LED bulbs — lighting load can fall by roughly 80%.
  3. Unplug standby loads. Decoders, chargers and appliances on standby draw power all day.
  4. Batch heavy appliance use. Irons, heaters and washing machines are the biggest unit-eaters; run them together, not scattered through the day.
  5. Service your fridge. An old fridge with failing seals runs its compressor far longer than it should.
  6. Track weekly, not monthly. Checking your meter balance weekly catches waste early — a sudden drop usually means something was left on.
  7. Buy credit before it runs out at night. Running dry means a dark house until vendors open; keep a buffer purchase on the meter.

Frequently asked questions

How many units will GH₵50 buy?

For a standard residential customer at roughly GH₵1.97/kWh, about 25 kWh. A lifeline customer gets far more — around 57 kWh. Use the calculator with your exact category.

Why did my units drop after a tariff increase?

PURC reviews tariffs quarterly. When tariffs rise, each cedi buys fewer units. Check whether a new quarterly tariff took effect — ECG and PURC announce these publicly.

Can I change from postpaid to prepaid?

Yes — ECG runs meter replacement and new-service programmes. Contact your district office; there may be a waiting list depending on meter availability in your area.

Is the lifeline tariff automatic?

It applies to residential customers whose monthly consumption stays within 0–30 kWh. Exceed 30 kWh in a month and you lose the lifeline rate on subsequent purchases.

Postpaid vs prepaid: which is actually cheaper?

Ghana runs both systems side by side, and each has partisans. Here's the honest comparison:

PrepaidYou see exactly what you spend, in real time. No estimated bills, no billing "errors," no debt accumulation. The discipline is built in — when units run low, you adjust behaviour immediately.
PostpaidYou consume first and pay later, which smooths cash flow — but estimated billing has historically been a major source of disputes, and debts can accumulate silently for months.

On pure tariff, the per-kWh rates are the same — the difference is behavioural, not mathematical. Prepaid wins for most households because visibility changes behaviour: studies of prepaid metering consistently show 5–15% lower consumption, simply because users watch the meter. If you're disciplined about checking a postpaid bill line by line, the gap narrows. If you're not, prepaid will save you money.

One prepaid-specific cost to watch: vending fees. Buying through some third-party vendors or mobile money channels adds a small service fee per transaction. Buying larger amounts less often minimises the fee drag — another reason to top up monthly rather than weekly.

What to do when the meter runs out

Every prepaid user has been there: the lights die at 11pm and the vendor is closed. A survival kit:

  1. Know your emergency credit. Many ECG prepaid meters allow a small emergency overdraft — learn how to activate it on your meter model before you need it, not during the blackout.
  2. Keep a buffer. Never let the balance hit zero. Recharge when you drop below a week's worth of typical use, not when the alarm sounds.
  3. Save your vendor options. ECG's app, USSD codes and mobile money integrations let you buy credit at any hour — set them up and test a small purchase in daylight.
  4. Know your meter number by heart (or saved in your phone). You can't buy credit without it, and fumbling for it in the dark is miserable.
  5. Have a torch and charged power bank. Obvious, but the households that suffer most from an empty meter are the ones with no backup light.

And if the meter itself seems to be draining abnormally fast — units vanishing with everything switched off — that can indicate a fault or, occasionally, meter tampering on a shared line. Log the drain with everything off for a few hours, then report it to your ECG district office with the numbers.

Keep a simple energy diary

The households that spend least on prepaid credit share one habit: they write down each purchase — date, amount, units received. After two or three months the diary reveals your real consumption pattern, your true effective rate per kWh, and exactly how tariff changes hit you. It takes thirty seconds per purchase and it turns every future complaint, budget and appliance decision into an informed one. Start with your next token. Pair the diary with a full household budget to keep energy spending in its lane.
