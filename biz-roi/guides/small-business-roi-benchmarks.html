What's a Good ROI for a Small Business? 2026 Benchmarks

You ran your numbers in our business ROI calculator and got a percentage. Now the real question: is it any good? A 60% ROI sounds great — until you learn the business next door earns 300%. This guide gives you realistic 2026 benchmarks by industry so you can judge your own numbers honestly.

The Quick Rule of Thumb

For a small business, 100%+ ROI over your investment horizon is generally good — it means you doubled your money. Under 12 months payback is healthy; under 6 months is excellent. Negative ROI or payback beyond 3 years means the investment is questionable unless there is strategic value (brand building, market entry) you are deliberately paying for.

Context matters: 50% ROI in one year beats 200% ROI over five years. Always state the time period when comparing.

Payback Period Targets

  • Under 6 months: excellent — reinvest aggressively.
  • 6–12 months: healthy — the standard target for most small businesses.
  • 12–24 months: acceptable for capital-heavy businesses (restaurants, retail).
  • Over 24 months: risky — make sure you have the cash runway, and question whether the capital earns more elsewhere.

Why ROI Differs So Much by Industry

Three forces drive the differences:

  1. Startup cost. A consultant invests $5,000; a restaurant invests $300,000. The same $60,000 annual profit is a 1,200% ROI for the consultant and 20% for the restaurant.
  2. Margins. Services run 50–80% margins; restaurants run 5–15%. Margin is the engine of ROI.
  3. Scalability. Digital products sell the same unit infinitely; a storefront sells only what walks in. Scalable models compound ROI.

Annualizing ROI for Fair Comparisons

Never compare a 3-year ROI against a 1-year ROI directly. Convert to an annualized figure with this formula:

Annualized ROI = (1 + ROI as decimal)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

Example: a 200% ROI over 3 years is not 66% per year. It is (1 + 2.0)^(1/3) − 1 = 44.2% per year. This is the number to compare against savings accounts, index funds (historically ~10%/year) and other opportunities competing for your capital.

A useful mental shortcut: if your annualized business ROI beats 15–20%, you are outperforming most passive investments by a wide margin — the business is earning its risk premium. Below 10% annualized, ask whether the capital (and your time) works harder elsewhere.

ROI vs. Profit Margin: Don't Confuse Them

Owners often quote a "40% ROI" when they mean a 40% profit margin. They are different:

  • Profit margin = profit ÷ revenue. It measures efficiency per sale.
  • ROI = profit ÷ investment. It measures return per dollar invested.

A business can have a 50% margin and a terrible ROI (tiny revenue against a huge build-out), or a 10% margin and a fantastic ROI (lean operation turning inventory fast). You need both numbers — margin tells you if the engine is efficient, ROI tells you if the investment was worth it. Our calculator shows ROI; our break-even tool exposes your margin structure.

How to Read the Table for Your Situation

Three practical ways to use the benchmarks:

  1. Sanity-check your projection. If your plan shows 400% annual ROI for a full-service restaurant, your assumptions are almost certainly wrong — revisit revenue or cost estimates.
  2. Choose between ideas. Comparing a food truck (40–100%) against a consulting practice (150–400%)? The table tells you which model class has the structural advantage before you spend a dollar.
  3. Set a walk-away line. Decide in advance: "If my realistic ROI is under 50% with payback over 18 months, I will not invest." Writing the rule down before you fall in love with the idea is what separates investors from gamblers.

Remember that benchmarks describe established businesses. Year-one ROI is usually lower because of ramp-up costs and learning curves — budget for it rather than being surprised by it.

How to Improve a Weak ROI

  1. Cut the denominator — negotiate startup costs down. Every dollar saved on investment lifts ROI directly.
  2. Raise prices — a 10% price increase often flows almost entirely to profit.
  3. Cut variable costs — renegotiate suppliers, reduce waste.
  4. Shorten payback — pre-sell, take deposits, or launch an MVP before the full build.
  5. Kill losers early — if payback stretches past 24 months with no path to improve, redeploy the capital.

Before committing money, verify your numbers twice: once with the ROI calculator and once by hand using our step-by-step ROI guide. Also find your break-even point so you know the minimum sales needed to survive.

How Investors and Lenders Read Your ROI

If you ever pitch for funding or a loan, your ROI calculation becomes your credibility:

  • Banks care most about payback period and break-even — can you service the debt? A payback under the loan term with 1.25x debt-service coverage is the bar.
  • Angel investors want 10x money multiples over 5–7 years on the winners, knowing most bets fail. Your small-business ROI needs to show a credible path to that scale, not just profitability.
  • Partners compare your projected ROI against what they could earn passively. If your plan shows 8% annualized, a partner would rather buy an index fund — and they are right.

Present three scenarios — conservative, base, optimistic — and show the ROI for each. Anyone who has funded businesses before will trust a founder who shows the downside case unprompted.

Do Benchmarks Differ by Region?

Yes, mainly through rent and wages. A restaurant in Manhattan faces triple the rent of one in Ohio, which compresses ROI even at identical revenue. When using the table above, adjust:

  • High-cost metros (NYC, SF, LA): expect ROI 20–40% below the national typical range for location-dependent businesses.
  • Low-cost regions: add 15–30% to the typical range — cheaper rent and labor lift margins directly.
  • Online businesses: geography barely matters; use the national figures as-is.

The most reliable benchmark is always a comparable local business. Ask owners in your area what payback they experienced — most will share ranges if you ask respectfully.
