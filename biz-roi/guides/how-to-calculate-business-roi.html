How to Calculate Business ROI: Step-by-Step

Business ROI answers one question: for every dollar I put in, how many dollars do I get back? This guide walks you through the formula, two worked examples, and the two companion metrics (payback period and money multiple) every owner should know. Prefer automation? The ROI Formula

ROI = (Total Profit − Investment Cost) ÷ Investment Cost × 100

Where Total Profit = (monthly revenue − monthly expenses) × number of months, and Investment Cost = everything you spend to start (equipment, deposits, licenses, initial inventory, setup marketing).

Step-by-Step Calculation

  1. List every startup cost. Include the costs people forget: business licenses, deposits, insurance, initial marketing, 3 months of operating buffer.
  2. Estimate monthly revenue realistically. Use conservative sales × price. If unsure, use 50% of your optimistic estimate.
  3. List monthly expenses. Rent, wages, supplies, software, loan payments, your own salary draw.
  4. Compute monthly profit: revenue − expenses.
  5. Multiply by your horizon (12, 24 or 36 months) for total profit.
  6. Apply the formula. (Total profit − startup cost) ÷ startup cost × 100.

Worked Example 1: Coffee Cart

  • Startup cost: $18,000 (cart, permits, initial stock)
  • Monthly revenue: $9,000
  • Monthly expenses: $5,500
  • Monthly profit: $3,500
  • Total profit (12 months): $42,000
  • ROI = (42,000 − 18,000) ÷ 18,000 × 100 = 133%
  • Payback: 18,000 ÷ 3,500 = 5.1 months — excellent.

Worked Example 2: Online Store

  • Startup cost: $12,000 (website, initial inventory, ads budget)
  • Monthly revenue: $6,000
  • Monthly expenses: $4,800 (COGS, ads, tools)
  • Monthly profit: $1,200
  • Total profit (12 months): $14,400
  • ROI = (14,400 − 12,000) ÷ 12,000 × 100 = 20%
  • Payback: 12,000 ÷ 1,200 = 10 months — workable, but margins need watching.

Payback Period and Money Multiple

Payback period = startup cost ÷ monthly profit. It tells you when you recover your investment. Under 12 months is healthy for most small businesses.

Money multiple = total profit ÷ startup cost. A 2.0x multiple means every invested dollar returned two dollars of profit. Our calculator shows all three metrics side by side.

5 Common Mistakes

  1. Forgetting your own salary. If you work full-time in the business, your labor has a cost — include a salary draw in expenses.
  2. Optimistic revenue. Halve your first-year revenue estimate. You will thank yourself later.
  3. Ignoring the time horizon. 100% ROI in one year is very different from 100% over five years.
  4. Skipping break-even. ROI assumes you survive long enough to earn it — check your break-even point first.
  5. Comparing across industries. A 40% ROI is weak for consulting but strong for a restaurant. See our industry benchmarks.

Going Further: Net Present Value in 60 Seconds

Simple ROI treats a dollar earned in year three the same as a dollar earned today. Finance professionals discount future dollars because of inflation and opportunity cost. You do not need a finance degree — just know the concept:

If your business returns $30,000 in year 3, that money is worth less than $30,000 today. At a 10% discount rate, it is worth about $22,540 in today's dollars. For most small business decisions, simple ROI plus a payback check is enough — but if you are comparing a 5-year project against putting the money in an index fund, discounting keeps the comparison honest.

Using ROI to Compare Two Opportunities

ROI is most powerful as a comparison tool. Suppose you have $30,000 and two options:

Option A: Food truckOption B: Cleaning business
Startup cost$30,000$8,000
Monthly profit$4,000$2,500
12-month ROI60%275%
Payback7.5 months3.2 months

Option A earns more dollars per month, but Option B earns far more per dollar invested and pays back in a third of the time. If capital is your constraint, Option B wins. If your time is the constraint and you can only run one business, absolute profit matters more — but you should still demand that the winner clears your walk-away ROI threshold. Service vs. Product Businesses: Different ROI Shapes

Service businesses (consulting, agencies, home services) have low startup costs and high margins, so ROI percentages look spectacular — 200–400% is common. The catch: revenue is capped by your hours unless you hire, and hiring compresses margins.

Product businesses (e-commerce, food, retail) need more capital and carry inventory risk, so ROI percentages look modest — 30–100%. The advantage: revenue can scale without proportional labor, and a winning product compounds.

Neither shape is "better" — but you must compare like with like. A 60% ROI that embarrasses a consultant would delight a restaurant owner. Keep our industry benchmarks open in another tab while you calculate.

Pre-Investment Checklist

Run through this list before you commit capital:

  • ☐ Startup costs listed completely (including buffer, licenses, deposits)
  • ☐ Revenue estimated conservatively (halved optimistic case)
  • ☐ Your own salary included in expenses
  • ☐ ROI calculated for 12, 24 and 36 months
  • ☐ Payback period under 12 months (or justified exception)
  • ☐ Break-even units achievable at realistic sales — verified with the break-even calculator
  • ☐ Result compared against industry benchmarks
  • ☐ 3–6 months of expense runway kept in reserve after investing
