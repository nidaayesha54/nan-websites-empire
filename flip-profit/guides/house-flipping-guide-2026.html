How to Flip Houses in 2026: The Complete Beginner's Guide

Updated October 2026 · 12-minute read · By FlipProfit

House flipping looks simple on TV: buy ugly, renovate fast, sell for a fat profit. In reality, most beginners lose money on their first flip — not because flipping doesn't work, but because they buy wrong. This guide teaches you the math, the rules, and the process that professional flippers actually use in 2026.

What house flipping really is (and isn't)

Flipping means buying a property below market value, adding value through renovation, and selling at a profit — typically within 3–9 months. It is not passive income. It's a fast-moving, capital-intensive business where your profit is made the day you buy, not the day you sell.

The golden principle: you make money on the buy. If you overpay, no renovation can save the deal. That's why every section below revolves around buying at the right price.

The 70% rule — the formula that protects you

The 70% rule is the single most important formula in flipping:

Maximum purchase price = (ARV × 0.70) − rehab costs

ARV is the "after-repair value" — what the home will sell for once renovated. The 30% discount covers your profit margin, holding costs, selling costs, and a cushion for surprises.

Example: A renovated 3-bed sells for $260,000 in the neighborhood (ARV). It needs $40,000 in rehab. Your maximum offer: ($260,000 × 0.70) − $40,000 = $142,000.

Is the rule rigid? Experienced flippers in hot markets sometimes stretch to 75%, but beginners should treat 70% as a hard ceiling. Try it on any deal with our fix-and-flip calculator — it checks the rule automatically.

How to estimate ARV with comps

ARV is where most beginners go wrong — they use optimistic numbers. Get it right:

  1. Find 3–5 sold comps within 0.5 miles, sold in the last 3–6 months.
  2. Match the finished product: same bed/bath count, similar square footage (±20%), similar lot and style.
  3. Use sold prices, not list prices. Listings are wishes; closings are facts.
  4. Adjust conservatively: if comps range $250k–$270k, use $255k, not $270k.
  5. Check days-on-market: if renovated homes sit 90+ days, the market is soft — lower your ARV.

Free comp sources: Zillow and Redfin sold listings, your county assessor's site, and — best of all — a friendly local agent who'll pull MLS comps for future business.

How flippers finance deals

  • Hard-money loans: the flipper's standard tool. 10–12% interest, 1–3 points upfront, 6–12 month terms, funded in days. Expensive but fast.
  • Cash: fastest and cheapest if you have it, but ties up capital.
  • Private money: individuals lending at 8–10%. Built on relationships and track record.
  • Conventional loans: rarely work — too slow, and most lenders won't finance uninhabitable properties.

Rule of thumb: if your holding costs (mostly loan interest) exceed 6 months of projections, your profit evaporates. Speed is money.

How to find profitable deals

Good deals never hit the MLS at a discount — they're found, not listed. Proven channels:

  1. Direct mail: absentee owners, tired landlords, pre-foreclosures. Old school, still works.
  2. Driving for dollars: spot distressed homes, look up owners, make offers.
  3. Wholesalers: they find deals and assign contracts for a fee. Vet their ARV numbers yourself.
  4. Agent relationships: pocket listings and "coming soon" properties before they go public.
  5. Auctions & REOs: foreclosure auctions and bank-owned listings — competitive but real discounts exist.

Whatever the source, run every lead through the flip calculator before you fall in love with it.

Taxes: what the IRS takes from your flip

Flips held under a year are taxed as short-term capital gains — meaning your profit is taxed as ordinary income, at your marginal rate (often 22–37% federally, plus state tax). A $40,000 profit can shrink to $26,000 after taxes. Three things smart flippers do:

  • Track every receipt. Rehab costs, holding costs, and selling costs all reduce your taxable gain. Sloppy records = paying tax on money you already spent.
  • Consider an entity. Many active flippers operate through an LLC taxed as an S-corp to manage self-employment tax. Talk to a CPA before your third flip.
  • Know dealer status. Flip several houses a year and the IRS may classify you as a "dealer," which kills installment-sale treatment and changes how losses work. Again — CPA territory.

This isn't tax advice, just a warning: model your deals after tax, not before. A 20% pre-tax ROI at a 30% combined tax rate is really a 14% ROI.

Scaling: from your first flip to ten a year

One profitable flip proves the model. Ten a year builds a business. The jump requires systems:

  1. Deal flow pipeline: you need 50–100 analyzed leads to close one flip. That means consistent marketing — direct mail, wholesaler relationships, agent network — running every single week, not when you feel like it.
  2. Reliable crews: one great general contractor is worth more than a 5% cheaper bid from a stranger. Pay on time, feed the relationship, and keep their calendar full.
  3. Capital stack: recycling the same $100k through 3 flips a year beats sitting on $300k doing one. Hard-money lenders, private lenders, and eventually lines of credit let your capital work multiple jobs.
  4. Standardized scopes: pros use the same finish packages across flips — same paint, same fixtures, same flooring. It cuts decision time, speeds contractors, and makes costs predictable to the dollar.

Most flippers who scale past 5 deals a year say the same thing: the bottleneck was never money or deals — it was contractor capacity and their own analysis speed. Run numbers fast (that's what our calculator is for), decide fast, and protect your crews' schedules like gold.
