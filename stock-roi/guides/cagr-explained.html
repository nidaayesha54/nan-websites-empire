CAGR Explained: The One Number That Tells the Truth About Returns

Someone tells you their stock "doubled in 5 years." Sounds great — but is it? A 100% gain over 5 years is very different from 100% over 2 years. CAGR — the compound annual growth rate — converts any gain over any period into a single yearly rate you can actually compare. Here's how it works, with real examples.

What is CAGR?

CAGR is the constant annual growth rate that would take an investment from its starting value to its ending value, assuming profits are reinvested each year. It smooths out the bumps — the crashes, the rallies — into one clean "as if it grew steadily" number.

That's why professionals use it instead of "total return." Total return answers "how much did I make?" CAGR answers the more useful question: "how fast did my money grow per year?"

The CAGR formula

CAGR = (Ending Value ÷ Beginning Value)1 ÷ Years − 1

Three inputs, that's it:

  • Beginning value — what you started with
  • Ending value — what you ended with (including reinvested dividends for total-return CAGR)
  • Years — the holding period (fractions allowed, e.g. 2.5 years)

CAGR example 1: the doubled stock

You buy $10,000 of stock. Five years later it's worth $20,000. Total return: 100%. Impressive — but what's the yearly rate?

CAGR = (20,000 ÷ 10,000)1/5 − 1 = 20.2 − 1 ≈ 14.87%

So "doubled in 5 years" = 14.87% per year. Excellent — well above the market's long-run ~10%.

CAGR example 2: the volatile ride

You invest $10,000. Year 1: +40%. Year 2: −20%. Year 3: +30%.

Ending value: $10,000 × 1.40 × 0.80 × 1.30 = $14,560. Total return: 45.6%.

The average of the yearly returns is (40 − 20 + 30) ÷ 3 = 16.7%. But CAGR = (14,560 ÷ 10,000)1/3 − 1 ≈ 13.3%.

Notice CAGR (13.3%) is lower than the simple average (16.7%). This is the crucial insight: volatility drags down compounded growth. The simple average overstates what you actually earned. CAGR doesn't lie.

Fund B's total return looks bigger (140% vs 80%) — but Fund A grew faster per year (15.8% vs 13.3%). CAGR makes the comparison fair.

CAGR vs average return: why it matters

The simple average return is mathematically misleading for multi-year periods because it ignores compounding order. A −50% year followed by +50% leaves you at 75% of your start (not break-even): $10,000 → $5,000 → $7,500. The "average return" is 0%, but you lost 25%. CAGR correctly reports −13.4%.

Rule of thumb: for anything held longer than a year, quote CAGR, not average return.

What is a good CAGR?

  • ~10% — long-run US stock market (S&P 500) before inflation; ~7% after inflation
  • ~5% — long-run bond index returns
  • 15%+ over a decade — exceptional; very few funds sustain this
  • 20%+ — Buffett-level over decades (Berkshire Hathaway ≈ 20% for ~60 years)

Anyone promising a guaranteed 20%+ CAGR is selling something. Treat it as a red flag.

CAGR limitations

CAGR is honest about growth rate, but it hides two things:

  1. Volatility — a smooth 10% CAGR and a wild ±40% ride to the same endpoint look identical. Risk matters.
  2. Cash flows — CAGR assumes one lump sum. If you added or withdrew money mid-period, use XIRR instead (our DCA calculator models regular contributions).

FAQ

What is a good CAGR for stocks? +
For broad US stock market index funds, a 7–10% long-term CAGR before inflation is historically typical. Individual stocks vary enormously — a 15%+ CAGR over a decade is exceptional.
CAGR vs average return: what's the difference? +
Average return is the simple mean of yearly returns; CAGR is the single smoothed rate that compounds to the same ending value. CAGR is always equal to or lower than the average return, and it's the honest number for comparing investments.
Can CAGR be negative? +
Yes. If your ending value is below your beginning value, CAGR is negative — e.g., $10,000 → $8,000 over 2 years is a −10.6% CAGR.
