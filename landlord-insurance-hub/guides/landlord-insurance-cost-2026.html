How Much Does Landlord Insurance Cost in 2026?

The average landlord insurance policy in the United States costs roughly $1,200–$1,800 per year in 2026 — about 15–25% more than a comparable homeowners policy. But national averages are nearly useless for budgeting, because your premium depends on your property type, your state, your deductible, and how much coverage you buy. A wood-frame duplex in Florida can easily cost triple what a brick single-family rental costs in Ohio.

This guide breaks down what landlords actually pay in 2026, the factors that move your rate the most, and nine proven ways to bring it down. For a personalized number in under a minute, try our free premium estimator.

Average Landlord Insurance Cost by Property Type

The single biggest driver of your premium is what kind of property you rent out. More units mean more risk — more tenants, more plumbing, more chances something goes wrong:

  • Single-family rental: ~$1,100–$1,600/year. The cheapest standard landlord policy; one roof, one tenant household.
  • Duplex: ~$1,500–$2,100/year. Two units roughly double the plumbing, wiring and tenant-injury exposure.
  • Triplex / 4-plex: ~$1,900–$2,800/year. Small multifamily pricing reflects higher claim frequency.
  • Condo rented out: ~$700–$1,100/year. The HOA master policy covers the building exterior; your policy covers the interior, appliances and liability.
  • Small apartment building (5+ units): ~$3,000–$5,000+/year. At this scale you are approaching commercial policy pricing.

Cost by State: Why Location Matters So Much

Insurance is priced on expected losses, and nothing predicts losses like geography. States with hurricanes, wildfires, hail belts or heavy litigation cost far more to insure:

  • Most expensive: Florida, Texas, Louisiana and California routinely run 30–60% above the national average, driven by hurricanes, wildfires and hail.
  • Cheapest: Parts of the Midwest (Ohio, Indiana, Iowa, Wisconsin) often come in 15–25% below average, with fewer natural disasters and lower litigation rates.
  • Watch zones: Colorado (hail), Oklahoma (tornadoes) and the Carolinas (hurricanes) can spike in specific ZIP codes even when the state average looks moderate.

Within a state, your ZIP code matters as much as the state itself. Distance to a fire station, crime rates and local replacement costs all feed into the rate. Two identical houses ten miles apart can be quoted hundreds of dollars apart.

The 7 Factors That Move Your Premium Most

1. Property type and units. See above — each additional unit raises exposure.

2. Location risk. Weather, crime, litigation culture and fire protection class. This is the factor you can't change, so get quotes early before buying a property.

3. Construction type. Wood-frame homes cost roughly 15–20% more to insure than masonry/brick, because they burn faster and sustain more wind damage.

4. Property age. Pre-1980 buildings with original wiring and plumbing draw surcharges of 15–25%. Updated electrical, roof and plumbing can earn discounts that offset this.

5. Deductible. Moving from a $1,000 to a $2,500 deductible typically saves 15–20% on premiums — one of the fastest levers available.

6. Claims history. One claim in the last 3 years raises rates ~10–20%; two or more can raise them 30%+ and limit your choice of carriers.

7. Coverage limits. Every extra $100K of dwelling coverage adds to the premium, as does a year (vs. 6 months) of loss-of-rent coverage. Use our coverage calculator to right-size limits before you shop.

Landlord Insurance vs. Homeowners: The Price Gap

Expect to pay 15–25% more than an equivalent homeowners policy. Insurers justify this with data: tenants file claims more often than owner-occupants, turnover creates vacancy risk, and loss-of-rent coverage is a benefit homeowners policies don't include. 9 Ways to Lower Your Landlord Insurance Cost

  1. Raise your deductible. From $1,000 to $2,500: the single fastest saving, often 15–20%.
  2. Bundle policies. Insuring with the same carrier as your auto or personal home policy typically saves ~10%.
  3. Shop 2–3 carriers every 2 years. Rates drift; loyalty is rarely rewarded. A 30-minute comparison shop commonly saves $200–$400.
  4. Install security and safety devices. Smoke/CO detectors, security systems and deadbolts earn 5–10% in discounts.
  5. Update old systems. New roof, electrical panel or plumbing can erase age surcharges.
  6. Maintain a claim-free record. Pay small repairs out of pocket instead of filing $1,200 claims — claims follow you for years.
  7. Require tenant renters insurance. It doesn't lower your premium directly, but it reduces liability exposure and claim disputes.
  8. Consider actual cash value vs. replacement cost. ACV is cheaper but pays depreciation — usually a bad trade for the dwelling itself.
  9. Ask about landlord-specific discounts. Professional management, multi-unit ownership and payment-in-full discounts exist at many carriers.

The Bottom Line

Budget roughly $1,200–$1,800 per year for a typical single-family rental, more for multifamily and high-risk states — and treat that as a starting point, not a quote. The landlords who pay the least are the ones who right-size coverage, raise deductibles, and re-shop every couple of years.
