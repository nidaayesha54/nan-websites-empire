Landlord Insurance vs Homeowners Insurance

New landlords ask this constantly: "Can't I just keep my homeowners insurance on the rental?" The short answer is no. A standard homeowners policy (HO-3) assumes you live in the home — and once tenants move in, the insurer can deny your claim entirely. Here's how the two policies differ, what each costs, and exactly when to switch.

The Core Difference: Who Lives There

Homeowners insurance (HO-3) protects an owner-occupied home and the family's belongings inside it. Landlord insurance (DP-3) protects a tenant-occupied investment property and the owner's rental income. Insurers treat these as fundamentally different risks: tenants change, maintenance habits vary, and vacant periods create exposure that owner-occupied homes don't have.

The moment you rent out a home, most HO-3 policies exclude or void coverage for tenant-related losses. A fire during a tenancy could leave you with a denied claim and a mortgage still to pay.

What Happens If You Keep Homeowners on a Rental?

Three bad outcomes, in order of likelihood: (1) the insurer discovers the tenancy (at claim time or renewal) and cancels or non-renews the policy; (2) a claim is denied because the property's use changed without notice; (3) your mortgage lender flags the gap in compliant coverage and force-places expensive insurance on you. None of these are hypothetical — they happen every day.

If you're between tenants for a few weeks, call your agent: most policies allow short vacancy windows, but extended vacancy (30–60+ days) usually requires a vacant-property endorsement or policy.

Cost Difference: Is Landlord Insurance Worth the Extra?

Landlord insurance costs 15–25% more than homeowners — roughly $200–$400 extra per year on a typical single-family home. For that you get loss-of-rent coverage and liability structured around tenants. One month of lost rent ($1,800) dwarfs the annual premium difference. When to Switch Policies

  • Moving out and renting your old home: switch from HO-3 to DP-3 before the first tenant moves in. Tell your agent the date; don't backdate anything.
  • Buying a rental property: bind a landlord policy at closing, exactly like you would homeowners on a primary residence. Your lender will require proof.
  • House hacking (living in one unit, renting others): this is a gray zone — an HO-3 often doesn't fit, and a DP-3 doesn't fit either. Many carriers write a homeowners policy with a rental endorsement, or a landlord policy with owner-occupancy noted. Ask specifically.
  • Short-term / Airbnb rentals: standard landlord policies often exclude short-term rentals. You may need a home-sharing endorsement or a dedicated STR policy.
  • Moving back in: switch back to HO-3. Landlord policies don't cover your personal belongings the way homeowners does.

What About Condo Landlords?

Renting out a condo adds a twist: the HOA master policy covers the building exterior, and you need coverage for everything from the walls in — interior finishes, appliances, liability and loss of rent. This is usually written as a landlord HO-6 policy, not a standard DP-3. It costs less than a house landlord policy (often $700–$1,100/year) because the master policy carries the big structural risk. The key question to ask your agent: where does the master policy end and yours begin? "Bare walls" master policies leave far more to you than "all-in" ones, and under-insuring the gap is the most common condo-landlord mistake.

Can You Have Both?

Yes — and most landlords do. Your own home carries homeowners insurance; each rental property carries landlord insurance. Insuring them with the same carrier usually earns a multi-policy discount of around 10% on each. Just never put a tenant-occupied property on the homeowners policy.

Bottom Line

Homeowners insurance protects your home; landlord insurance protects your business. The price gap is small, the coverage gap is enormous, and keeping the wrong policy can void your claim when you need it most.
