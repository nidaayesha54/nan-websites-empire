What Does Landlord Insurance Cover?

Landlord insurance (usually written on a DP-3 dwelling policy form) protects the rental property as a business asset. It covers the building itself, your rental income when the property becomes uninhabitable, and your liability when someone gets hurt. What surprises most new landlords is the exclusions list — floods, earthquakes, wear and tear, and your tenants' own belongings are not covered. Here's the full picture.

1. Dwelling Coverage (the Building Itself)

This is the core of the policy: it pays to repair or rebuild the rental structure after a covered peril — fire, lightning, windstorm, hail, explosion, vandalism, burst pipes, and similar sudden events. Coverage should equal the rebuild cost, not the market value, and most policies require you to insure at least 80% of rebuild cost to collect full replacement-cost settlements.

Example: A kitchen fire causes $60,000 in damage to your rental. After your deductible, the dwelling coverage pays the repair bill — and you don't lose a property that generates $21,600 a year in rent.

2. Other Structures

Detached garages, sheds, fences and gazebos are typically covered at 10% of the dwelling limit by default. If your detached garage alone would cost $40,000 to rebuild, consider raising this limit.

3. Landlord-Owned Personal Property

Appliances, furnishings and equipment you own and keep at the rental — refrigerators, stoves, washers, lawn equipment — are covered, typically at a modest limit you can increase. Critical distinction: your tenants' belongings are never covered. That is what renters insurance is for, and many landlords now require it in the lease.

4. Loss of Rental Income

If a covered peril makes the property uninhabitable, this coverage replaces your lost rent while repairs happen — often for up to 12 months. After a serious fire, repairs routinely take 6–12 months, so this coverage is arguably the most valuable part of a landlord policy. Example: $1,800/month rent × 8 months of repairs = $14,400 protected. It usually does not cover lost rent from tenant non-payment or vacancies — only from covered damage.

5. Liability Coverage

If a tenant or visitor is injured on your property and you are legally responsible — a broken stair railing, an icy walkway you failed to clear, a dog bite — liability coverage pays legal defense and settlements, typically $300,000 to $1,000,000. One slip-and-fall lawsuit can easily exceed $100,000, which is why experienced landlords rarely carry less than $500,000 and often add an umbrella policy on top. What Landlord Insurance Does NOT Cover

  • Floods — requires separate flood insurance (NFIP or private), even for inland properties near rivers.
  • Earthquakes — separate earthquake endorsement or policy.
  • Wear and tear / maintenance — a worn-out roof, peeling paint and aging HVAC are your problem, not the insurer's.
  • Tenant-caused intentional damage — most policies exclude deliberate destruction; some offer vandalism coverage for non-tenant damage.
  • Your tenants' belongings — covered only by their renters insurance.
  • Vacant properties (extended) — properties vacant beyond 30–60 days often need a separate vacant-property policy; standard coverage can be restricted or voided.
  • Home-based business operations — if you run a business from the rental, that needs separate coverage.

DP-1 vs. DP-2 vs. DP-3: Which Form Matters

DP-1 (basic form) covers a short list of named perils and pays actual cash value (depreciated) — cheapest, but thin protection. DP-2 (broad form) adds perils like falling objects and water damage. DP-3 (special form) is open-peril on the dwelling — everything is covered except listed exclusions — and pays replacement cost. Most experienced landlords choose DP-3; the price difference is small relative to the protection gap.

Real Claim Examples

Burst pipe, January: A pipe freezes and floods the first floor of a duplex. Dwelling coverage pays $22,000 in repairs; loss-of-rent coverage pays 3 months of rent ($5,400) while the unit dries out. Total claim: ~$27,400.

Tree on the roof: A windstorm drops a tree onto the garage. Other-structures coverage pays the $9,000 rebuild after the deductible.

Slip on the stairs: A visitor falls on a loose handrail and breaks a wrist. Liability coverage pays the $45,000 medical settlement plus legal costs.

Bottom Line

A good landlord policy protects three things: the building, your rental income, and your personal assets against lawsuits. The gaps — flood, earthquake, maintenance, tenants' belongings — need separate solutions.
