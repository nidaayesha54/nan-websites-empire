1. What Is a QR Code (and Why Use a Generator)?

A QR (Quick Response) code is a two-dimensional barcode invented in 1994 that stores information — a website link, plain text, WiFi credentials, contact details — in a pattern of black squares your phone camera can read in a fraction of a second. A single QR code can hold up to about 4,000 characters.

A qr code generator turns your content into that pattern as an image you can print, share on WhatsApp, put on a flyer, or stick on your shop's wall. The generator on this site creates static QR codes: your data is encoded directly into the pattern itself, so the code works forever, needs no internet connection to scan, and cannot be tracked by anyone — including us.

Static vs. dynamic QR codes: Static codes (what this tool makes) are permanent and private — the data lives inside the code. Dynamic codes route through a server so the destination can be changed or scan counts tracked, but they require a subscription and expire if you stop paying. For menus, WiFi passwords, business cards and links that rarely change, static is the right choice.

2. How to Create a QR Code (Step by Step)

Using our free qr code generator takes under a minute:

  1. Open the Generator tab on NAN QR Tools.
  2. Pick a type: Text/URL for links and messages, WiFi for network credentials, or vCard for a contact card.
  3. Fill in the fields. For a URL, paste the full address (include https:// so phones open it directly).
  4. Choose a size. 256 px is fine for screens; pick 512 or 1024 px for printing flyers and posters.
  5. Click "Generate QR Code" and then "Download PNG". Done — test it with your phone camera before printing.

Tips for links and text codes

  • Keep URLs short. A shorter link means a simpler pattern with bigger squares — much easier to scan from a distance or when printed small. Use your site's short links where possible.
  • Always test before printing. Scan the downloaded PNG with two different phones (one iPhone, one Android) to be safe.
  • Print size matters: the minimum comfortable print size is about 2 × 2 cm (roughly 0.8 inches). Smaller than that and many cameras struggle.
  • Keep the quiet zone. Leave a clean white border around the code at least as wide as one row of squares — phones need it to find the code.

3. WiFi QR Code: Share Your Network Without Typing Passwords

A wifi qr code is one of the most useful QR codes you will ever make. Instead of spelling out a long password letter by letter for every guest, they point their phone camera at the code and join your network instantly — no typing, no mistakes.

How to make a WiFi QR code

  1. On NAN QR Tools, open the Generator and select the WiFi tab.
  2. Enter your network name (SSID) exactly as it appears — WiFi names are case-sensitive.
  3. Enter the password and choose the security type (WPA/WPA2 for almost all home routers).
  4. Tick "Hidden network" only if your router hides its SSID from the broadcast list.
  5. Generate, download the PNG, and print it for your living room, café counter, or office reception.
🔒 Privacy note: Your WiFi name and password are converted into the QR pattern locally in your browser. They are never uploaded anywhere — which is exactly how it should be for credentials.

Where a WiFi QR code pays off

  • Cafés, restaurants and salons: print it on a table tent or the counter — staff stop repeating the password all day.
  • Airbnb and guest rooms: frame it on the wall so guests connect the moment they arrive.
  • Offices: put the guest-network code in the meeting room instead of emailing credentials.
  • Events and weddings: add it to the welcome sign so hundreds of guests connect without bothering the host.

One caution: anyone who can photograph the code can join your network — use your router's guest network for public codes rather than your main private network.

7. How to Make QR Codes That Scan Every Time

  • High contrast only. Black squares on a white background scan best. Light colors on light backgrounds fail.
  • Don't shrink too far. Keep printed codes at least 2 × 2 cm; for scanning across a room (menus, billboards), go much bigger.
  • Keep the quiet zone. That white border around the code isn't decoration — scanners need it. Never crop it off.
  • Avoid glossy glare. When scanning with a camera, tilt the paper slightly to kill reflections from screens and laminated prints.
  • Shorter content = easier scanning. Long URLs create dense, fiddly patterns. Shorten links first.
  • Flat and undistorted. Codes on curved bottles or wrinkled paper are hard to read — keep the surface flat.
  • Test on real phones. Before printing 500 flyers, scan the code with at least two different phones.

8. Privacy & Safety: Read This Before You Scan

🔒 Our privacy guarantee: both the generator and the scanner on NAN QR Tools run 100% in your browser with open-source JavaScript libraries. Images you upload for scanning are decoded locally and never sent to any server. Nothing is logged, tracked, or stored.

That said, QR codes themselves deserve healthy caution wherever you scan them:

  • Check the URL before opening. Our scanner shows you the decoded link first — read it before tapping "Open link". A code can point anywhere.
  • Beware stickered-over codes. Scammers place their own QR stickers over legitimate ones (parking meters, restaurant tables). If a code looks tampered with, don't scan it.
  • Never enter passwords after scanning. A QR code that lands you on a login page asking for bank or email credentials is a phishing attempt — real services don't work that way.
  • WiFi codes are safe to create — but only share your guest network publicly, as anyone with the code can join.
