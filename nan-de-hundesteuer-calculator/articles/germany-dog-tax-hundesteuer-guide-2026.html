Germany Dog Tax (Hundesteuer) 2026: Costs by City, Registration & Exemptions

New in Germany and surprised to learn your dog comes with an annual tax bill? The Hundesteuer is real, it's set by your city, and it ranges from about €50 to over €150 a year. Here's everything expats need to know in 2026 — in plain English.

What the Hundesteuer is (and why it exists)

The Hundesteuer (dog tax) is a municipal tax every dog owner in Germany must pay — it has existed since the early 19th century, originally as a luxury tax. Today it's classed as an "expenditure tax": your city charges you for the privilege of keeping a dog, and the revenue (over €400 million nationwide in recent years) funds general municipal services. It's also deliberately designed as population control — the tax rises steeply for second dogs and for breeds classified as dangerous, discouraging casual multi-dog ownership.

There is no national rate. Each municipality sets its own through a local ordinance, so two identical Labradors can cost different amounts 50 km apart. After registering, you receive a dog tag (Hundemarke) as proof of payment.

Second dogs and "dangerous" breeds

Almost everywhere, the second dog costs significantly more than the first (Berlin: €180 vs €120; Stuttgart: €288 vs €144). The policy intent is explicit: each additional dog should be a deliberate decision.

Dogs on the breed list (Listenhunde) — typically Pit Bulls, American Staffordshire Terriers, Bull Terriers and similar — face punitive rates that can exceed €900 a year in some cities, plus special registration, and in some states outright keeping restrictions. If you're considering one of these breeds as an expat, research your state's rules first, not just the city's tax rate.

How to register your dog

  1. Register promptly after arrival or adoption. In Berlin, for example, you register via the Hunderegister (about €17.50) — which is separate from the tax registration at the Finanzamt. Other cities handle it through the Bürgeramt or online city portals. Deadlines vary but are usually within 2–4 weeks.
  2. Get the ID chip. Your dog must be microchipped (around €50 at a vet).
  3. Get the collar tag. Outside your home, your dog must wear a collar with your name and address.
  4. Pay by direct debit. The tax is usually collected annually by automatic bank transfer once you're registered.
  5. Liability insurance (Hundehaftpflicht) is mandatory in several states (including Berlin, Hamburg, and Lower Saxony) and strongly recommended everywhere — a large dog can cause five-figure damage claims.

Late registration = fines. Cities do enforce this, and the Ordnungsamt can fine unregistered owners. Register first, ask questions later.

Exemptions: who doesn't pay

You may be fully or partly exempt if your dog is a certified guide dog for the blind, a rescue/service dog, or a working shepherd/herding dog. Some cities also grant reductions for adopted shelter dogs (often for the first year — a nice incentive to adopt) and for owners on low incomes or unemployment benefits. Each exemption needs an application with proof; it isn't automatic.

Moving or saying goodbye: deregistration

The tax follows your registered address. If you move to another city, deregister (Abmeldung) at the old municipality and re-register at the new one — otherwise you'll keep getting billed. The same applies if your dog passes away or is rehomed: deregister promptly and keep the confirmation. Overpaid tax for the remainder of the year is typically refunded.

The Hundeführerschein: do you need a dog license?

Several German states require a Hundeführerschein (dog handler's license) — a theory + practical test proving you can handle your dog safely. Lower Saxony requires it for all new dog owners; Berlin offers a voluntary version whose holders get the green tag (grüne Plakette) and some leash-law relaxations. Requirements vary wildly by state, so check yours before getting the dog, not after — in mandatory states, owning a dog without the license is an administrative offence.

Leash laws, dog zones and etiquette

Germany is dog-friendly but rule-bound. Leash laws (Leinenpflicht) apply in most city centers, parks (outside designated dog zones), playgrounds, and during breeding season in forests and fields (typically spring). Many cities maintain fenced Hundeauslaufgebiete (off-leash dog areas) — learn where yours are. Poop bags are effectively mandatory: failing to clean up can draw on-the-spot fines from the Ordnungsamt, and repeat offences escalate fast. On public transport, dogs usually need a ticket (often half-fare) and large dogs may require muzzles depending on the city's transport rules.

What happens if you don't register or don't pay?

Don't test this. Municipalities cross-check vet records, pet databases, and neighbor reports more often than you'd think. Unregistered dog ownership draws fines that dwarf the tax itself, and back-taxes can be assessed for the unregistered period. The Ordnungsamt conducts spot checks in parks — no tag, no registration, immediate trouble. Registration takes 15 minutes online in most cities; the fine takes much longer to forget.

Bringing a dog from abroad

Moving to Germany with your dog? You'll need an EU pet passport, a valid rabies vaccination (given at least 21 days before entry), and the microchip — in that order, since the chip must predate the vaccination. Register for the Hundesteuer promptly after your Anmeldung (address registration) — and find your German tax class at the same time, since both start with registration; the tax clock starts with your German residence, not your arrival flight. Note that some "dangerous breeds" face import restrictions in certain states regardless of temperament — verify before booking the flight.

Popular breeds and their tax reality

Thinking of a specific breed? The tax angle: standard companion breeds (Labrador, Golden Retriever, Beagle, French Bulldog, Dackel) all pay the normal city rate — breed doesn't matter, only the count. Giant breeds (Great Dane, Bernese) pay the same tax but cost far more in food, insurance, and vet bills, so budget the total, not just the tax. Listenhunde (Pit Bull types, Staffies, and state-specific additions like Rottweilers in some states) trigger the punitive rates — often €600–900+/year — plus permit requirements. As an expat, the pragmatic shortlist is simple: if it's not on your state's list, the tax is the boring standard rate and life is easy.
