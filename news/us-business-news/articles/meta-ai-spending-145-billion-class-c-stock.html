Meta Lifts 2026 AI Spending Toward $145 Billion and Adds a Nonvoting Stock Class

October 26, 2026

Meta Platforms boosted its capital spending outlook and announced plans for a new class of non-voting stock on October 21, 2026, escalating its already enormous bet on artificial intelligence. Capital expenditures will be $135 billion to $145 billion in 2026, the company said during its quarterly earnings report, raising the bottom end of the range from $130 billion. The ceiling did not move — but the floor rose by $5 billion.

The money is going toward AI infrastructure: the chips and data centers needed to train large models, and the development of Muse, Meta's consumer AI agent. The quarterly results gave Meta some cover for the bigger budget. Third-quarter 2026 revenue came in at $64.60 billion, up 26% and ahead of the $63.37 billion Wall Street had expected, with diluted earnings per share of $3.83 after a $9.89 billion legal charge. Chief executive Mark Zuckerberg said: “Our investments are paying off, the core business is strong, and we’re seeing velocity across the board.”

A new share class with no say

The new class of publicly listed Class C common stock carries no voting rights. Existing holders of Class A and Class B shares will receive a one-time dividend of one Class C share for every share they own, effective January 19, 2027 — in effect a two-for-one stock split. The stock will be used for capital fundraising, employee compensation and potential acquisitions, and the company said it could raise hundreds of billions of dollars over several years. Crucially, it lets Meta raise equity without diluting voting power: Zuckerberg holds around 61% of Meta's voting rights despite owning a smaller slice of its economic value.

Investors were not reassured. Meta shares fell more than 7% on Thursday, October 22, and the cost of insuring the company's debt against default hit an all-time high, Channel NewsAsia reported — the latest sign that fears over the cost of the AI race are spreading to credit markets. The selloff came despite better-than-expected results and strong fourth-quarter guidance, highlighting growing investor unease over AI spending that is pushing historically cash-rich tech companies toward debt.

The bigger question

Meta is not alone in spending at this scale, and that is precisely what worries markets: borrowing to fund data centers leaves AI companies exposed if demand cools, while chips depreciate within five or six years and force another round of spending. Meta's answer is that its core advertising business is strong enough to carry the bet. The market's answer, at least this week, was a selloff — and a reminder that even a revenue beat cannot always outrun a $145 billion spending plan.
