Pakistan Calls for a Fairer Global Order at the United Nations

October 26, 2026

Pakistan has called for a more representative international order and consistent application of international law during a series of debates at the United Nations this week, with its interventions covering Security Council reform, military artificial intelligence (AI), weapons of mass destruction, radiation safety, human rights and conflicts in the Middle East. The message, delivered in New York, highlighted the need to balance international security with accountability and the interests of developing countries.

No new permanent seats, Pakistan argues

Addressing the General Assembly, Pakistan's acting permanent representative, Ambassador Usman Jadoon, opposed creating additional permanent seats on the Security Council, arguing that this would perpetuate existing inequalities. "The question is not whether the Council should be reformed, but how to make it more representative, democratic, transparent and accountable," he said. Pakistan favoured expanding elected, non-permanent membership through regular elections and fair regional rotation, while seeking adequate representation for Africa and other underrepresented regions. The position restates a long-standing Pakistani argument: that adding permanent members, with or without veto powers, would entrench historical privilege rather than democratise the Council, while a larger elected membership would tilt the balance toward the wider membership.

Military AI and human control

On military AI, Mr Jadoon called for inclusive, UN-led governance, warning that the technology could accelerate military decision-making and increase escalation risks, particularly in situations involving nuclear weapons. He also stressed the need for meaningful human control over autonomous weapons systems. At the General Assembly's First Committee, Counsellor Syed Atif Raza warned that advances in AI, synthetic biology and the life and chemical sciences were creating new security risks, and called for safeguards against the misuse of scientific developments without restricting developing countries' access to peaceful applications. Pakistan reaffirmed its commitments under the biological and chemical weapons conventions, stressing the importance of verification and international cooperation.

Why developing countries are central to the argument

Running through Pakistan's interventions was a single thread: rules written without the developing world will not hold. Whether the subject is who sits on the Security Council, who governs military AI, or who gets access to peaceful scientific applications, Islamabad argued that legitimacy comes from representation and consistent application of the law, not from power alone. For a country that is both a major troop contributor to UN peacekeeping and a host to one of the UN's oldest observer missions, the argument carries practical weight as well as principle. The debates continue in the General Assembly, but Pakistan's position for this session is now on the record: reform the Council by electing more members, govern new weapons technologies together, and keep human beings in control of decisions about war.
