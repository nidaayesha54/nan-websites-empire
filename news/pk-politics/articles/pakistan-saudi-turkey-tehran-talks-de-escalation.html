Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye Reported to Hold Tehran Talks on De-escalation

October 26, 2026

Senior officials from Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkiye were reported to be meeting Iranian officials in Tehran on Monday, October 26, to discuss regional security and efforts to prevent a wider escalation in the Middle East. The talks were described in reports as a de-escalation push at a time of rising tensions across the region, including reported missile and drone attacks by Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis against Saudi Arabia.

Who was reported to attend

Reports named Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan Army Chief Field Marshal Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin as part of the delegation heading to Tehran. Those names come from news reports rather than an official joint statement, and the precise agenda and format of the meeting had not been publicly confirmed by all sides at the time of reporting. Readers should therefore treat the attendance list as reported, not as an officially confirmed delegation.

The Makkah pact backdrop

The meeting comes against the backdrop of the Makkah Joint Defence Agreement, signed by Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkiye in August. Under that pact, the three countries committed to treating an armed attack against one member as an attack against all, raising important questions about collective security as regional hostilities intensify. The agreement has drawn close attention because it links three major regional players in a formal defence understanding, and any discussion in Tehran would inevitably be read in that light. Pakistan has also played an intermediary role in efforts to reduce tensions between Washington and Tehran, according to the reports, adding another layer to its involvement.

What is at stake

The stakes are high. Attacks near Saudi Arabia's holy cities, reported strikes on energy infrastructure and the risk of miscalculation all raise the danger of a wider conflict that could disrupt shipping, energy supplies and the safety of millions of people living and working in the Gulf. A meeting in Tehran, if it takes place as reported, would at minimum signal that direct channels between Riyadh, Islamabad, Ankara and Tehran remain open even during a crisis. Diplomats often use such channels to clarify intentions, warn against escalation and explore off-ramps that public statements alone cannot deliver.

What to watch for

The Tehran meeting will be watched for any sign of diplomatic progress, regional coordination or a pathway to prevent further escalation. No outcome had been announced in the reports available on October 26, and it would be wrong to present the talks as a breakthrough before any joint statement or official readout appears. What can be said is that the reported timing, on the same day Pakistan publicly condemned attacks near Makkah and Madinah, underlines how closely Islamabad's diplomacy and its public messaging are moving together in this crisis.
