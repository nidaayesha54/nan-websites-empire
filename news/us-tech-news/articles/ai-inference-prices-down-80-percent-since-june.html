AI Inference Prices Have Fallen as Much as 80 Percent Since June, Analysis Finds

October 26, 2026

The price of running artificial intelligence models has collapsed in recent months. OpenAI and Anthropic have quietly cut inference prices by up to 80 percent since June, according to an analysis of the June to September price slide, turning one of the industry's biggest cost centers into a fast-moving bargain for developers.

Inference, the cost of actually running a trained model to answer questions or write code, is where most AI spending now lands. When those prices fall this sharply, the economics of building AI products change for startups, enterprises and hobbyists alike.

What drove the price collapse

The analysis points to three forces. The first is Chinese competition: DeepSeek's Flash V4 model is reported to earn about $38 per GPU per hour while charging $0.60 per million output tokens, a price point that puts pressure on every Western lab. The second is better inference efficiency, as labs squeeze more work out of the same hardware. The third is the simple fact that GPU capacity is perishable, like airline seats: an idle chip earns nothing, so providers would rather cut prices than leave capacity unused.

Cache-read reductions matter disproportionately for agentic coding, where cached context can account for 70 to 75 percent of total cost, the analysis notes, which helps explain why coding-focused workloads have seen some of the steepest savings.

IBM says open models now cost a tenth as much on-premises

IBM chief executive Arvind Krishna told investors that open-weight models have closed the gap enough to make small on-premises deployments compelling for routine work such as email summarization and contract review, at about 10 percent of the cost of frontier models, according to The Register.

Independent developers appear to agree. Commenters discussing the price analysis said some have not paid for a frontier model in months, with open models paired with strong advisors now feeling close to top-tier performance for everyday tasks. Others cautioned that Krishna is, in part, talking his own book, since IBM would like to sell the accelerator hardware that runs those on-premises deployments.

A race to the bottom, or a gift to builders?

Observers are split on what the price war means. Some see a race to the bottom in which somebody eventually goes bankrupt. Others see exactly the outcome competition is supposed to produce: cheaper intelligence for everyone, with labs turning to regulation and differentiated features to defend their margins.

One notable side effect is that both OpenAI and Anthropic have grown more interested in regulation as prices fall, a shift that some analysts read as an effort to slow a commoditization trend they can no longer control with pricing alone.

For now, the practical takeaway for businesses is clear: the cost of running AI has rarely been lower, and contracts signed even a few months ago may already be out of date. Companies renegotiating AI deals this quarter have a strong case for demanding the new, lower rates.
