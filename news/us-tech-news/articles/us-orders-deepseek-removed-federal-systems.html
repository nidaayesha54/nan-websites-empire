US Orders DeepSeek Stripped From Federal Civilian Systems

October 26, 2026

U.S. federal civilian agencies must remove DeepSeek products from their information technology systems, along with any third-party products or services “developed using DeepSeek implementations,” under a Department of Homeland Security order. They may also no longer buy IT products or services from the Chinese AI developer or its affiliates.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin issued the order, dated July 24, 2026, under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act of 2018, and the department published it in the Federal Register on October 20, 2026, as Exclusion and Removal Order #2026-01 (Docket DHS-2026-0628). The order names Hangzhou DeepSeek Artificial Intelligence Co., Ltd., the developer of the R1 and V3 models, and covers its affiliates as defined in federal acquisition rules.

Why DHS acted

DHS said DeepSeek models pose “multiple threat vectors” and warned that use “may result in the transfer of sensitive data to the PRC if used by U.S. government employees or vendors.” The department also said use of DeepSeek by government agencies “could produce actionable intelligence on US persons and/or government agencies” and that the models themselves “may contain malware or other security vulnerabilities.” DeepSeek's privacy policy says it collects, processes and stores users' personal data in the People's Republic of China.

The scope is what makes this order different from an ordinary procurement ban. Because it follows the model into third-party tools, agencies must audit not only DeepSeek's own products but anything built on its models — including low-cost tools employees or vendors may have adopted without treating them as Chinese AI at all. Agencies that need more time or a narrower scope must request an exception in writing.

What the order does not do

The order covers only what civilian agencies buy and run. It does not restrict companies or the public, and it does not by itself bind the Department of Defense or the intelligence community, which have their own issuing authorities under the 2018 law. The law was first used in 2025, when the Director of National Intelligence excluded Acronis from intelligence community procurement. For federal IT managers, though, the message is clear: provenance of AI models is now a supply-chain security question.

One practical problem remains unresolved: the notice does not define “DeepSeek implementations,” The Hacker News noted, which means agencies will need to ask suppliers whether their products fall under the ban. In a July memo made public alongside the order, DHS told federal agencies to monitor flows of traffic in and out of government websites to address potential risks from DeepSeek, Reuters reported. The order also carries an exception process requiring written requests with compelling justification — though some coverage noted the 60-day window for such requests had effectively elapsed before the public filing appeared, leaving agencies to seek clarity from DHS directly.

Sources: The Hacker News, Reuters via The Hindu BusinessLine, Federal Register (Docket DHS-2026-0628)
