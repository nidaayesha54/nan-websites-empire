Musk Says SpaceX Closed $40 Billion in GPU Debt and Warns of a Neocloud Shakeout

October 26, 2026

Elon Musk said over the weekend that SpaceX has closed $40 billion of debt to buy graphics processing units, and warned that the crowded field of companies renting out GPU capacity faces a brutal shakeout as credit markets tighten.

In a post on X, Musk said the debt will fund chips due in the first and second quarters of 2027, and that he expects most of it to be repaid early. He credited SpaceX's investment-grade rating and its Starlink and launch revenue for getting the deal done. He did not name the lenders, though the Financial Times reported on October 7 that Apollo led the financing. Interest rates and other terms have not been disclosed.

Credit is drying up for AI data centers

Musk wrote that debt markets have almost entirely shut off money for companies without substantial revenue outside AI data centers. That warning lands at a moment when cheap credit has paid for much of the AI build-out, and tighter lending could slow construction of new capacity.

The companies most exposed are the so-called neoclouds, firms that rent out GPU capacity to AI developers. Musk said the more than 200 neoclouds in the market face what he called guppy-level death and consolidation, a phrase that quickly spread across tech and market coverage on Monday.

Neocloud stocks slide on Monday

The warning appeared to weigh on neocloud stocks in premarket trading on Monday. CoreWeave, Nebius and Iren were reported down 2.4 percent, 1.9 percent and 1.8 percent respectively in early trading, as investors reassessed which GPU-rental businesses can survive a funding squeeze.

At the same time, SpaceX shares were reported up modestly, a sign that markets are drawing a sharp line between companies with diversified revenue and those dependent entirely on AI infrastructure demand.

Why it matters this week

The financing debate arrives just as the biggest spenders in AI prepare to report earnings. Microsoft, Alphabet and Amazon all report later this week, and investors will be listening closely for how much they plan to keep spending on chips and data centers, and how they plan to pay for it.

Data center tenants are also pushing more risk onto developers. Tenants now demand late-delivery penalties of $5 million or more a day, up from about $1 million this spring, according to a report from The Information, and insurance brokers have begun offering delay-in-delivery coverage for developers facing those penalties.

Together, the credit squeeze and the rising penalties paint a tougher picture for the AI infrastructure boom: the money is getting more expensive, the deadlines are getting more punishing, and only the best-capitalized players may be able to keep building at full speed.
