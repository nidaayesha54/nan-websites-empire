Midterm Early Voting Is Underway Across America Ahead of November 3

October 26, 2026

Early voting for the 2026 midterm elections is already underway across much of the United States, weeks before Election Day on Tuesday, November 3, 2026. Every seat in the U.S. House of Representatives is on the ballot, along with 35 Senate seats, 36 governorships and thousands of state and local races, making this one of the most consequential midterm elections in years.

The first states to open early in-person voting were Minnesota, South Dakota and Virginia on September 18, followed by Vermont on September 19 and Illinois on September 24. October then brought a steady wave of openings: California, Maine, Montana and Nebraska on October 5; Indiana, New Mexico, Ohio and Wyoming on October 6; and Arizona on October 7. Georgia and Idaho followed on October 13, Iowa, Kansas, Rhode Island and Tennessee on October 14, and North Carolina on October 15.

A staggered calendar, state by state

The staggered calendar means the campaign is no longer building toward a single day. Texas opened in-person early voting on October 19, with voting running through October 30, while Maryland opened on October 22. Nevada and Massachusetts opened on October 17, and Washington began on October 16. Voters in some states can also register and vote on the same day, while a handful of states do not offer no-excuse early in-person voting at all, so checking a state election office or the federal vote.gov portal remains essential.

Turnout expectations are high. More than 100 million Americans are expected to cast ballots this year, based on turnout in the 2022 midterms, according to WION's reporting. Campaign spending has matched that intensity: political advertising had already attracted $3.8 billion by October 12, according to AdImpact, which tracks political advertising, with some of the heaviest spending in closely contested Senate races in Texas, Ohio and Michigan.

What is at stake

Republicans currently hold narrow majorities in both chambers of Congress, and control of the House and Senate is considered competitive heading into November. Democrats need a net gain of four seats to take control of each chamber, according to Reuters. Because early voters lock in their choices before the final debates and late advertising, the last week of the campaign may matter most to the voters who have not yet cast a ballot — and to the candidates still trying to reach them.

Mail voting adds another layer to the calendar. According to voter guides published this month, 47 states and Washington, D.C. allow no-excuse early voting in some form, while eight states plus D.C. conduct all-mail elections, and Alabama, Mississippi and New Hampshire do not offer no-excuse early in-person voting at all. Deadlines for requesting a mail ballot, returning it and voting in person vary by state and sometimes by county, which is why election officials consistently point voters to their state election office rather than to national summaries. With ballots already cast in many states, the remaining campaign is, in effect, two campaigns at once: one aimed at voters who have already decided, and one aimed at those still to come.

Sources: WION, Newstod, American News Brief, AdImpact
