Pakistan-Iran Trade Disruption Puts $2.8 Billion Annual Trade at Stake

October 26, 2026

The closure of the Gabd-Rimdan and Taftan crossings between Pakistan and Iran, in place since October 17, is about much more than gas cylinders. Pakistan-Iran trade totalled about $2.8 billion in the 2023-24 fiscal year, including roughly $684 million of Pakistani exports, according to the Federation of Pakistan Chambers of Commerce and Industry. Every day the crossings stay shut, that trade — and the livelihoods behind it — is at risk.

What crosses the border

The most urgent flow is energy: Pakistan imports about 7,000 metric tons of LPG a day from Iran, according to the Pakistan LPG Association, with more than 4,000 tons a day entering through Gabd-Rimdan alone. But the crossings also carry food and farm produce in both directions. More than 1,000 Pakistani containers carrying potatoes, bananas and rice for Iran were reported stranded when the crossings closed, and hundreds of trucks, including LPG tankers, are stranded on both sides of the border. The Quetta Chamber of Commerce and Industry has advised members to stop placing new orders with Iran until further notice — a signal of how uncertain traders consider the outlook.

Why the crossings closed

The Balochistan government, in the province where the crossings are located, said the temporary closure was intended to address security concerns and curb smuggling, while Iran's foreign ministry cited technical issues. The differing explanations matter because they point to different paths back to normal: a security-driven closure may reopen once authorities are satisfied, while a technical dispute may need a different kind of fix. There was partial movement during the week, with Gabd-Rimdan reopening for Pakistani exports of perishable goods — a lifeline for exporters of fresh produce, but not yet a full restoration of two-way trade.

What a prolonged closure would cost

For households, the visible cost is already clear: traders said the price of a 12kg LPG cylinder had risen to 7,000 rupees from 4,500 rupees within days. For exporters, the cost is spoiled produce and broken delivery commitments; for transporters, days of waiting with limited access to basic amenities. Pakistan LPG Association chairman Irfan Khokhar warned shortages and price increases could worsen if the disruptions persist. The $2.8 billion headline figure is an annual total, not a daily loss — but it frames the stakes: these are not marginal crossings. They are a working trade artery for Balochistan and beyond, and both countries have an economic interest in getting it flowing again.

Sources: Reuters, October 23, 2026; Federation of Pakistan Chambers of Commerce and Industry.
